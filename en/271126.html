Global Economic Pressures

GEOPOLITICS • FINANCIAL MARKETS
01

Debt, Trade, and Infrastructure Under Pressure

In response to the circumvention of export restrictions through Asian cloud clusters, escalating attacks and mine-related claims in the Strait of Hormuz, and persistent cost inflation, the U.S. Commerce Department, the White House, China's Ministry of Commerce, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), and Asia-Pacific central banks are pursuing measures with overlapping consequences. These include the reported suspension of AI server shipments to four ASEAN countries under a new 90-day U.S. licensing regime, proposed Section 301 tariffs, two-month extensions of suspensions of critical-mineral export controls, restrictions on Middle Eastern shipping, and tighter monetary policy. South Korea has raised its policy rate, while markets are pricing in December increases in the United States, Europe, and Japan at high probabilities. These developments threaten access to advanced semiconductors, affordable insurance, and predictable supply chains for Asian cloud providers, exporters in 14 industrial economies, and heavily indebted governments. Some of the costs could fall on commercial banks, corporate borrowers, diesel and electricity consumers, and taxpayers.

These developments have different institutional origins and implementation schedules. High market-implied probabilities do not constitute completed central-bank decisions, and proposed trade measures are not yet final.

Between November 23 and 26, 2026, pressure on physical transportation routes increasingly overlapped with restrictions on global computing infrastructure and sovereign financing. Futures markets temporarily moderated the price shock as supply flows were redirected, but the associated economic costs remain visible in European gas-storage shortfalls, worsening bank-loan delinquency, and the repatriation of gold reserves from overseas vaults.

Falling futures prices and the continuing movement of cargo indicate that markets retain some ability to absorb disruptions. The simultaneous financial and logistical pressures do not establish that each is a direct cause of the others.

Sovereign Debt & Liquidity

MONETARY POLICY • PUBLIC FINANCE
02

Higher Rates and the Distribution of Financial Losses

Governments are approaching December's central-bank meetings amid a conflict between exceptionally high funding requirements and tighter monetary policy. According to the OECD's Global Debt Report 2026, gross market borrowing by governments and corporations is expected to reach $29 trillion in 2026, up 17% from 2024, including about $18 trillion in gross OECD sovereign issuance, equivalent to 24% of GDP. Refinancing requirements reached a historical high of $13.5 trillion in 2025, nearly 80% of gross issuance, while net borrowing in 2026 is projected to approach the second-highest level on record, at nearly $4 trillion. As structural demand for long-dated debt weakens, governments are shifting issuance toward shorter maturities. In 2025, the ratio of fixed-rate bonds with maturities of 30 years or longer to securities maturing in one to five years fell to its lowest level since at least 2008. Treasury bills overtook bonds in issuance volume and now account for 15% of the OECD debt stock. Government interest expenses reached 3.3% of GDP. In 2026, interest payments are expected to outweigh the reduction in the debt-to-GDP ratio attributable to inflation, adding 2.5 percentage points compared with a 2.4-percentage-point reduction from inflation. Against this backdrop, IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva warned, according to coverage of the Fund's report, that global public debt would exceed 100% of GDP by 2030.

Shorter maturities can help governments meet immediate funding needs when demand for long-term securities is weak. The projected debt trajectory remains dependent on future growth, interest rates, inflation, and refinancing conditions.

Economic data released between November 23 and 26 reinforced expectations of monetary tightening. On November 26, the Bank of Korea raised its benchmark rate to 3.25%, its third increase of the year. The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis's second estimate, published November 25, revised annualized third-quarter 2026 GDP growth to 4.3% from a preliminary 3.4%, with October PCE inflation at 3.8% year over year and core PCE inflation at 3.3%. Following these releases, futures pricing and Reuters reporting cited by FXStreet on November 26 placed the probability of a 25-basis-point Federal Reserve rate increase on December 9 at approximately 95%–98%, from the current 3.75%–4.00% range. The U.S. Treasury's $44 billion seven-year note auction on November 25 showed weaker demand: the securities yielded 4.708%, 0.9 basis points above the when-issued yield, while the bid-to-cover ratio declined from 2.46 to 2.43. With eurozone inflation at 4%, twice the target, all 71 economists surveyed by Reuters and approximately 90% of market pricing pointed to an increase in the ECB deposit rate from 2.50% to 2.75% on December 17. The United Kingdom remains divided: with October inflation at 3.6%, 64 of 65 economists surveyed by Reuters on November 13 expected the policy rate to remain at 4.00%, while money markets priced an approximately 80% probability of a December increase and three increases by mid-2027. On November 26, Japanese overnight index swaps implied an approximately 80% probability that the Bank of Japan would raise its rate from 1.25% to 1.50% on December 18, which would make 2026 the first year in 37 years with three rate increases.

Market prices and economist surveys reflect expectations rather than decisions. The divergence in the United Kingdom illustrates the uncertainty surrounding the timing and extent of tightening.

The ECB's November 2026 Financial Stability Review emphasizes that defense and infrastructure spending, including in Germany, has lifted eurozone sovereign issuance to a post-pandemic high while the Eurosystem's balance sheet contracts. Highly leveraged, procyclical hedge funds are increasingly becoming marginal buyers of public debt. Similar pressure on the long end of the U.S. yield curve prompted the Treasury to expand buybacks of long-dated securities, but the stabilizing effect was short-lived. Meanwhile, the technology sector's collateral base has come under pressure. Following Anthropic's disappointing IPO debut the previous week, Wall Street reportedly lost more than $4 trillion in market capitalization, the S&P 500 fell 8.6% over five trading days, and the Nasdaq Composite erased its 2026 gains, according to Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg reporting cited in industry coverage. On November 25, the Nasdaq fell another 2.1%, and on November 26 S&P placed Nvidia's AA credit rating on CreditWatch Negative. Both the ECB and the Bank of England warned in their November financial stability reports of risks from circular debt financing of AI infrastructure and the possibility of stocks and bonds becoming positively correlated during a stagflationary shock.

Leveraged investors can also provide liquidity when traditional demand declines. The warnings identify possible financial transmission channels, not a certainty that a technology-sector correction will become a sovereign-debt crisis.

Losses in debt and equity markets may increasingly be transmitted to the broader economy and foreign investors through taxation and restructuring of sovereign balance sheets. Between November 24 and 26, Danantara Investment Management, the investment arm of Indonesia's sovereign wealth fund, cut its 2026 net-profit forecast by 76.5% to 800 billion rupiah following the decline in the Jakarta Composite Index. Its operating state-assets arm, Danantara Asset Management, retained its 332 trillion rupiah target. On November 24, Indonesia granted the fund tax deferrals to accelerate consolidation of state-owned enterprises: 380 of more than 1,000 entities had already been consolidated against a year-end target of 600.

The continued operating target and the tax deferrals indicate that the investment losses need not affect every part of Danantara equally. Consolidation may also serve administrative objectives independent of market losses.

Norway's Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG), the world's largest sovereign wealth fund, was valued at $2.3 trillion as of September 30 according to Reuters, and recorded a reported quarterly loss of $29.71 billion. Bonds drove the decline amid rising interest rates, while the equity portfolio, representing 72% of assets, returned a modest 0.5%. Including the strengthening krone's negative impact of 472 billion kroner and budget inflows of 80 billion kroner, the fund's quarterly valuation declined by 670 billion kroner. Norway's revised 2026 budget calls for withdrawals of 583.4 billion kroner, and the 2027 budget provides for withdrawals of $63.6 billion. In September 2026, Norges Bank Investment Management (NBIM) recommended reducing the target government-bond share of its fixed-income benchmark from 70% to 50%, with a modeled threshold as low as 40%. The recommendation received renewed attention in November 24 coverage and could imply sales of $80 billion–$106 billion in sovereign bonds, particularly U.S. Treasuries.

The benchmark changes are recommendations and modeled alternatives, not completed asset sales. Currency movements and budget transfers also contributed to the fund's valuation changes.

The United Kingdom's Autumn Budget 2026 introduces measures that could increase the tax burden on banks and sovereign investors. Beginning January 1, 2027, the banking surcharge rises to 6% for 15 months. Taxes on savings and property income increase, the taxation of corporate distributions expands, and foreign sovereign wealth funds lose tax immunity on income and capital gains from British real estate and trading activities. In the United States, the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget (CRFB) warned on November 24 that Social Security was less than six years from insolvency and that proposals for a debt-financed sovereign wealth fund would deepen the fiscal shortfall.

Changes in tax treatment may raise government revenue without necessarily shifting all costs to borrowers. The actual incidence depends on how banks and investors respond.

Companies are also reducing employment as they address operating costs. The private tracker LayoffHedge reports more than 817,100 job losses across over 796 companies since the start of the year. Intellizence's November 25 update recorded announcements by fintech company FNZ (1,500 jobs, November 23), Lufthansa (1,000 jobs amid fuel costs, November 22), and Allo (300 jobs, November 24). These followed November programs at Boeing (2,500 jobs, or 5% of its commercial aviation division), Vodafone Deutschland (2,300), and Qualcomm in India (1,000–1,500), as well as fall reductions at Barclays (5,000 jobs amid AI adoption) and National Australia Bank (2,500 jobs). In South Africa, the country's last manganese plant, Transalloys, warned of a 10% workforce reduction because of delays in approval of Eskom's tariff by the regulator NERSA. Amid budget pressures, European companies urged EU leaders on November 22–23 not to reduce funding for the Competitiveness Fund.

The employment figures cover different companies, industries, and reporting periods. Energy costs, automation, and other company-specific factors may account for different portions of the reductions.

Central banks are repatriating gold as the dollar's share of global reserves edges lower. In its semiannual reserve-management report, published November 25 with data as of September 30, 2026, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) disclosed that it had moved 60.08 metric tons of gold from overseas storage at the Bank of England and Bank for International Settlements to India between March and September. Total RBI gold holdings remained unchanged at 880.52 metric tons, but the domestic share rose from 77.23% (680.05 metric tons) to 84.05%–84.06% (740.13 metric tons). Holdings abroad fell from 197.67 metric tons, plus 2.80 metric tons in gold deposits in March, to 140.39 metric tons. Since September 2024, the RBI has moved nearly 230 metric tons of bullion into domestic vaults. Although India did not increase its overall gold tonnage in 2026, and on November 13 gold accounted for $98.61 billion, or 13.98% of its $705.34 billion in total reserves, following a decline in bullion valuations from $4,721 per ounce in April to $4,319 in September, global central-bank demand remains strong. According to the World Gold Council, net central-bank purchases totaled 228 metric tons year to date, including 61 in September, with the National Bank of Poland and the People's Bank of China leading at more than 100 metric tons each. The RBI report also records a decline in the U.S. dollar's share of global currency reserves in the second quarter of 2026 from above 57% to nearly 57%, while the euro's share rose from 20% to 21%. Spot gold (XAU/USD) stabilized on November 26 below the 3,426–3,427 level cited for November 25.

Repatriation changes the location and jurisdiction of gold custody, not necessarily its quantity or the overall currency composition of reserves. India's unchanged holdings and the modest shift in the dollar's share distinguish custody diversification from wholesale replacement of the dollar.

As reserve management becomes more diversified, Washington is also pursuing new trade and financial restrictions. After the U.S. Supreme Court struck down the administration's previous across-the-board reciprocal tariffs, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative proposed new Section 301 tariffs on November 25 against 14 of 16 investigated trading partners, following an eight-month inquiry into structural industrial overcapacity. Mexico and Norway were excluded for the time being. The proposed rate for China is 20%, bringing duties on some goods to as much as 45% when combined with first-term tariffs; the new surcharge could rise to 40% if the truce breaks down. Approximately $30 billion in imports are exempt through the Board of Trade mechanism. The agreed 15% ceiling remains for the EU, while India's proposed rate is up to 25%, falling to 17% if a deal is reached. Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Switzerland, Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, Bangladesh, and Cambodia are also included. A separate 10% aircraft tariff, affecting Brazil's Embraer, and sugar tariffs have also been proposed. Public hearings are required, placing potential implementation in January 2027.

These tariffs remain proposals subject to public hearings and negotiations. Exemptions, country-specific ceilings, and possible agreements could change their eventual effects.

Between November 20 and 23, under Operation Economic Outcast, the U.S. Treasury estimated the scale of Iranian shadow banking at $14.7 billion–$15 billion for January 2025 through August 2026. It designated Lebanese and Turkish channels allegedly used by Hezbollah to move gold and cash, including Lebanon's Gold Global Company and Turkey's Sankari Gold, increasing secondary-sanctions exposure for foreign counterparties. On November 26, Citigroup's plans to return to Venezuela became public amid President Trump's efforts to reopen the country's economy. On November 19, Middle East Forum analysts argued that rapprochement between Washington and Caracas could expose earlier arrangements involving Iranian oil exchanged for Venezuelan gold through Mahan Air and Turkey.

The Treasury figures are estimates of potential shadow-banking activity, and the projected consequences of closer U.S.–Venezuelan relations remain conditional. A bank's plans to reenter a market do not establish that prior arrangements have been dismantled.

Households and small businesses face the combined burden of higher energy bills and tighter credit. The Federal Reserve's Beige Book, published November 25, reports rising loan delinquency, weaker demand across loan categories and refinancing at small and midsize banks, tighter lending standards, and narrowing interest spreads alongside widespread price increases. AAA reported an average U.S. retail diesel price of $5.725 per gallon on November 26, compared with $3.778 a year earlier, a 51.5% increase. The Energy Information Administration raised its forecast for average winter heating-oil expenditure by 25% from the previous winter, to $2,179, while forecasting a 9% decline in natural-gas heating expenditure to $640.

The projected decline in natural-gas heating bills shows that cost pressures vary with the fuel and household involved. Tight credit and higher diesel costs need not translate uniformly into consumer expenses.

Shipping & Critical Commodities

MARITIME TRADE • ENERGY • MINERALS
03

Strategic Shipping Routes and the Cost of Supply Disruption

Middle Eastern straits and major global port terminals are experiencing recurring military incidents and labor disputes, although shipping has partially adapted through longer routes and alternative logistics arrangements. Following the initial closure of the Strait of Hormuz on February 28, 2026, the reinstatement of the U.S. blockade on July 14, and the IRGC's formal announcement of closure on October 26, tensions escalated further. On Monday, November 23, Iranian military commanders ordered a progressive tightening of the closure as Qatar sought to revive peace negotiations. On November 24–26, the IRGC claimed through Iranian media that an LNG carrier and five oil tankers had struck naval mines, including two vessels near Oman on November 26. UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) confirmed a projectile strike on a vessel west of Dubai, illustrating differences between independently reported incidents and IRGC claims. According to Al Jazeera figures cited by Global Oil Shock for week 48, traffic through Hormuz fell to 125 vessels during November 17–23, from 226 in the first week of November. Vortexa data through November 19 and Reuters reporting nevertheless indicate that some shipments continued through alternative vessel and transfer arrangements, including the conversion of smaller product tankers to crude-oil transport.

Reduced traffic does not mean that all shipments have stopped. UKMTO's confirmation of a projectile strike also does not independently substantiate the full set of Iranian mine-strike claims.

In the Red Sea, the Houthis, who reportedly established control over the Yemeni coast of Bab el-Mandeb between September and November, including Mocha, Perim Island, and Dhubab, directly threatened Jeddah and other Saudi ports on November 25–26, launched attacks toward Riyadh, and targeted the Saudi Ras Tanura export and refining hub. Saudi air defenses intercepted four drones. Saudi Arabia's East-West Pipeline, with a capacity of approximately 5.8 million barrels per day, was also reported attacked on November 9 and 12, shifting peak volumes toward Egypt's SUMED pipeline. Against this backdrop, on November 25 container shipping company Maersk informed Egyptian President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi of its intention to fully restore its Suez Canal routes. Canal revenue reached $593 million between November 1 and 24, double the year-earlier figure. In Eurasia, energy infrastructure on both sides of the Russia–Ukraine war continues to sustain damage. Reuters reported on November 20 that Russian primary oil refining had declined 45% since late October to approximately 400,000 metric tons per day, with 15 of 35 major refineries damaged or shut down. Russian attacks reported on November 20, involving more than 400 drones and over 110 missiles, damaged Ukrainian electricity infrastructure, forcing all three operating nuclear power plants to reduce generation and causing emergency outages in 11 regions.

Maersk's planned return and higher Suez revenue indicate that risks and operating conditions differ across routes. Rerouting may mitigate some supply losses without eliminating costs or restoring damaged infrastructure.

Among major port-terminal operators, the ten largest account for 44% of global throughput, or 441.1 million TEU. Labor conflicts over terminal operations coexist with shifts in concession ownership. On November 24, a preliminary labor agreement removed the immediate threat of a strike at Los Angeles and Long Beach after record third-quarter import volumes. In mid-November, unions coordinated strikes at 113 ports in 22 countries, including COSCO-controlled Piraeus, where roughly 3,000 workers took part, as well as Hamburg, Genoa, Valencia, Algeciras, Le Havre, Tanger Med, and Nhava Sheva. Workers also protested what they described as the militarization of port infrastructure. After Germany blocked COSCO's proposed acquisition of 80% of a Hamburg logistics operator on October 9, competitors expanded their networks. Dubai-based DP World extended concessions early in Manila and San Antonio, Chile, on November 15 and in Luanda through 2051, and is negotiating a $1.47 billion investment program in Callao, Peru. Singapore's PSA International began construction of a 4.7 million TEU terminal in Haiphong, Vietnam, between November 20 and 25, after launching the second phase of its Tuas megaport.

The proposed transactions, existing concessions, labor agreements, and strikes involve separate decisions. Expansion of alternative terminals may improve capacity even as concentration raises questions about control.

Oil prices show a divergence between financial benchmarks and the physical cost of transporting supplies through disrupted routes. Despite escalation in the Gulf, Brent lost about 20%–24% from its October peak of $117.64 on October 30 and fell to a three-month low of $88.97 per barrel on November 23. It subsequently rose 3.5% on November 25 and by midday November 26 traded at approximately $94.96 according to CNA, or above $95 according to Trading Economics. WTI stood at $84.44 per barrel, implying a $10.52 Brent–WTI spread; there was no WTI settlement on November 26 because of Thanksgiving. Factors weighing on prices include the IEA's estimate of a 1.7 million-barrel-per-day surplus in the fourth quarter, a similar 1.7 million-barrel-per-day estimate for October from Deutsche Bank, the restart of Libya's Sharara oil field, a 1.1 million-barrel increase in U.S. crude inventories in the week ending November 20 to 418.9 million barrels, and record purchases of Russian crude by India and China. Ahead of the OPEC+ ministerial meeting on November 29, the group faced disagreements over 2027 quotas following the UAE's withdrawal and demands from Iraq and Kazakhstan for higher assessments of their production capacity.

The estimated supply surplus and rising inventories could explain lower oil prices even as maritime risks persist. The relative importance of these factors will change with production, demand, and shipping conditions.

The LNG and natural-gas markets appear more vulnerable. A November 24 World Bank review indicates Middle Eastern gas production will fall by more than 11%, or 90 billion cubic meters, in 2026. The IEA reports a record 3% contraction in global LNG supply, with Qatari and Emirati export losses between March and October exceeding 60 billion cubic meters on an annualized basis. While U.S. LNG shipments rose 23%, approximately 30% of U.S. export flows shifted from Europe to East Asia. The EU entered the heating season with underground gas storage 74.6% full, well below the five-year average of 89.8% and the statutory November 1 target of 90%, strengthening the potential pricing power of flexible spot-market suppliers. Vitol, Trafigura, and Glencore could benefit from disruptions to long-term Qatari contracts, having expanded portfolios using U.S. LNG that is not subject to resale restrictions. According to Wood Mackenzie, major traders and portfolio players more than doubled LNG trading volumes to over 60 million metric tons in 2025. Earlier in 2026, a consortium of Vitol, Trafigura, and Glencore won a tender to supply five LNG cargoes to Egypt. Traders have also benefited from metals-market volatility: Trafigura earned approximately $2 billion from metals trading alone during its 2026 fiscal year, which ended in September.

Flexible U.S. LNG supplies may partly offset the loss of Middle Eastern cargoes. Actual winter prices and trader margins also depend on consumption, inventories, and the availability of alternative deliveries.

China has maintained leverage over critical minerals by granting short extensions to suspended restrictions. On November 9, 2026, one day before a year-long suspension was due to expire, the Ministry of Commerce and customs authorities issued Announcement 2026/58, extending the suspension of six October 2025 measures for only two months, until January 10, 2027. These include the extraterritorial 0.1% rule in Announcement No. 61 for foreign-made products containing Chinese rare earths. However, April's Announcement No. 18, requiring individual licenses for seven medium and heavy rare earths—dysprosium, terbium, samarium, gadolinium, lutetium, scandium, and yttrium—has remained in effect throughout, with exports of yttrium, dysprosium, and terbium approximately 50% below pre-restriction levels. Between November 24 and 26, ahead of the November 27 deadline, Beijing extended the suspension of Article 2 of its December 2024 restrictions under Announcement No. 46 for two months, to January 27, 2027. These measures concern gallium, germanium, antimony, superhard materials, and stricter graphite controls for U.S. shipments. Prohibitions involving U.S. military end users and the broader export licensing system remain in effect. In response, the United States has used public funds to support extraction and processing that may not be commercially viable under prevailing market conditions. The Pentagon provided MP Materials a 10-year commitment to purchase 100% of its magnets and a $110-per-kilogram price floor for neodymium-praseodymium (NdPr); USA Rare Earth received up to $1.6 billion under the CHIPS Act, and the House of Representatives approved the Critical Mineral Dominance Act (H.R. 4090).

The temporary suspensions provide some continuity for commercial trade, and U.S. investment could expand alternative capacity. The effectiveness of either approach depends on licensing decisions and whether new production becomes viable.

Shipping-risk premiums have become a substantial additional cost of physical delivery. Extra war-risk insurance premiums for Hormuz tankers rose from 0.25% of hull value before the conflict to 6%–10% in the fall, reaching as much as $20 million for a voyage, and remained near wartime highs in November according to shipping trackers. Spot freight rates for very large crude carriers (VLCCs) from the Gulf to China (TD3C) reached record levels of $1 million–$1.41 million per day in September and October. The Wall Street Journal and Windward estimated freight at $37 per barrel from the Gulf to Asia, including an approximately $26-per-barrel Hormuz-related premium, with loading at Ras Tanura costing $12 more per barrel than at Fujairah. Rates fell below $30 per barrel in mid-November. Nevertheless, 76% of the global VLCC fleet had been deployed east of Suez since mid-October, contributing to a shortage of large vessels in the Atlantic. The share of midsize tankers in west-to-east flows rose to 52% in November, while freight from the U.S. Gulf to China (TD22) reached $17.43 per barrel on November 20. U.S. refiners faced falling margins: the 3-2-1 crack spread dropped from $69.72 in October to $37.62 on November 24. Since November 13, Marathon Petroleum shares had fallen 15.2%, Valero 14.7%, and Phillips 66 10.8%, while retail diesel remained $5.725 per gallon.

The decline in some freight rates and refining spreads shows that the burden is unevenly distributed. Elevated insurance costs need not produce proportionate changes in every route, product price, or company's earnings.

AI Infrastructure & Digital Sovereignty

ENERGY SYSTEMS • SEMICONDUCTORS • CONNECTIVITY
04

Power, Connectivity, and Access to Advanced Computing

Electricity supply and subsea connectivity have become important constraints on technological sovereignty in the AI sector. The UN COP31 climate summit in Antalya ended on November 22 without a final agreement. Alongside the EU's rejection of softer wording on fossil fuels, a major issue was the tension between electrification ambitions under the 35-by-35 declaration and the electricity consumption of hyperscale AI data centers competing with residential users for grid connections.

The summit's outcome reflected several disagreements, including fossil-fuel language. Competition for electricity is an important constraint, but it does not alone explain the failure to reach an agreement.

National regulators and grid operators are increasingly restricting new AI campus connections and seeking to allocate network costs to corporations. On November 24, the U.S. Senate voted 62–33 to pass a bill requiring large AI data centers to pay for grid expansion to protect household electricity rates. The measure still requires House approval and the president's signature. On November 20, President Trump threatened substantial taxes and penalties on U.S. companies building AI data centers overseas, while acknowledging that the details and legal basis had not been finalized. In Europe, Norway's state grid operator Statnett suspended capacity reservations for new data centers until August 1, 2027, according to November 11 reporting. In Texas, ERCOT paused review of major new connections while auditing a 474-gigawatt application queue, potentially delaying 20% of the entire U.S. data-center development pipeline according to BloombergNEF.

These measures may reduce pressure on existing electricity customers while encouraging more realistic project commitments. The U.S. legislation and proposed overseas penalties are not yet enacted, and the extent of actual project delays remains uncertain.

Technology companies' nuclear-energy agreements offer long-term supply protection but may not close generation gaps before 2030. Meta has contracted for up to 6.6 gigawatts from Vistra, Constellation, and Entergy through 2035. Microsoft is financing the restart of Three Mile Island Unit 1, with 835–837 megawatts expected by 2027. Google signed a 20-year, $4.3 billion agreement for 890 megawatts of upgrades at Constellation, and the U.S. Department of Energy offered Constellation a conditional $5 billion loan. The IEA projects that nuclear power will contribute only 20 gigawatts by 2035, or 6% of 320 gigawatts of new data-center capacity, noting that small modular reactors (SMRs) have yet to be demonstrated at industrial scale. Oklo's Aurora reactor remains in the NRC preapplication stage following a 2022 denial. AWS abandoned plans for seven buildings near Calvert Cliffs nuclear power plant following local opposition, and the Fermi nuclear-powered data-center project in Texas became involved in a lawsuit alleging demands for political favors on November 20. To power continuously operating racks such as Nvidia's 120-kilowatt GB200 NVL72, hyperscalers are turning to fossil fuels. Meta is financing seven natural-gas plants totaling 5.2 gigawatts in Louisiana and became the first of the four major companies to acknowledge the difficulty of its 2030 net-zero target amid a 45% rise in emissions, according to The Verge. Amazon's project in Pecos County, Texas, includes more than 7 gigawatts of gas turbines.

The nuclear agreements may still contribute meaningful capacity over the longer term, and the IEA's estimate is a forecast. Near-term gas generation does not necessarily establish the eventual energy mix of all data centers.

Subsea fiber-optic infrastructure, which carries approximately 99% of intercontinental traffic and financial transactions, is also increasingly exposed to disruption. Damage to the SMW5 cable on November 18 off Pakistan's coast reduced the country's HTTP traffic to 36% of its usual level, according to Cloudflare Radar and NetBlocks. On November 8, Windward reported 53 hours of unusual maneuvering by a Chinese research vessel near subsea cables. In the Persian Gulf, where five subsea cable segments cross Hormuz, regional cable repair work by Alcatel Submarine Networks was suspended following Iranian threats to impose cable-laying fees, and the $700 million Iraq–UAE WorldLink transit cable project was canceled because of the fighting. In Europe, amid Finnish criminal proceedings concerning cable damage involving the Fitburg and Eagle S, NATO's disclosure of a reported Russian sabotage plot in September, and European Commission funding of €347 million, a UK parliamentary committee identified a critical shortage of repair vessels. Building a vessel takes three years and training crews takes ten years; the committee also identified vulnerabilities in terrestrial landing infrastructure.

A cable disruption or unusual vessel activity does not establish a deliberate act of sabotage. Repair capacity, new investments, and network redundancy can influence the duration and severity of outages.

On November 25–26, reported suspensions of server hardware shipments to Southeast Asia affected a major channel for access to advanced computing. According to the Financial Times, Nvidia instructed assembly partners including Dell and Supermicro to suspend AI server shipments to buyers in Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia. The measure follows U.S. Commerce Department rules that took effect on November 19, 2026, intended to close a route through which Chinese developers rented computing capacity from regional data centers.

A reported shipment suspension does not necessarily mean that all installed computing capacity is unavailable. Its duration and scope depend on the licensing rules and their implementation.

Existing cloud providers have 90 days to obtain U.S. licenses and must disclose and verify the direct and indirect beneficial owners of their end customers. Cloud-company executives interviewed by the Financial Times said Washington had not provided application instructions, describing substantial uncertainty. In 2025, Nvidia recorded $104 billion in revenue through its Singapore entity, up 72% year over year. In the first nine months of 2026, Malaysia approved 123.5 billion ringgit in data-center and cloud projects, accounting for 35.2% of all approved investment according to the Malaysian Investment Development Authority (MIDA). Nvidia's own governance crisis adds to the uncertainty. On November 12, CEO Jensen Huang resigned amid reported SEC and FBI investigations concerning his relationship with a Singapore cloud-provider executive accused of renting computing capacity to China and suspected of espionage. He was reinstated with President Trump's support on November 23, when shares rose 31.5%, and S&P placed the company's rating under negative review on November 26. China is meanwhile accelerating efforts to develop domestic technologies. On November 26, TrendForce reported that Huawei was preparing a proprietary high-bandwidth memory architecture with double the interface width, at the HBM4 level.

The licensing requirements may allow compliant companies to resume shipments, and the investigations do not themselves establish wrongdoing. China's reported memory development likewise does not establish commercial production or immediate replacement of imported equipment.

Corporate Power & Public Infrastructure

DEFENSE • SATELLITE SERVICES • TECHNOLOGY
05

Private Technology Providers and Government Dependence

Private technology companies increasingly act as operators of defense-critical infrastructure, giving their service decisions the potential to affect the capabilities of governments. On November 12–13, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy and Ukrainian Defense Ministry sources reported renewed restrictions on the speed of Starshield, the military version of SpaceX's Starlink satellite service, across the front line and occupied territories. Ukrainian military sources said the reduced speeds affected drone interception and medium-range drone operations. SpaceX officially denied making changes, while Kyiv urgently contacted the Pentagon and NATO partners to clarify the status of the service. The episode followed a reported week-long Starlink and Starshield slowdown in October, which the Wall Street Journal attributed to an instruction by Elon Musk. Service was reportedly restored following President Trump's intervention on October 22–24 and calls by lawmakers including Representative Joe Wilson and Senator Thom Tillis for Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to expand the Starshield contract and investigate the incident.

SpaceX's denial and the absence of a confirmed explanation leave the cause of the November service degradation disputed. Reported interruptions show the importance of service continuity without establishing that the provider deliberately curtailed military capabilities.

Similar concerns have emerged over European NATO members' dependence on U.S. military AI. Palantir's market capitalization rose from less than $25 billion to approximately $450 billion during the war, a rise linked by Responsible Statecraft, as cited by UNIAN, to integration with Ukrainian government agencies and Western defense budgets. Following the October Starshield disruptions, Palantir stated on October 22–23 that its Maven military AI platform deployed within NATO had no remote kill switch and could be maintained by appropriately cleared European personnel. The need for public reassurance from a private contractor illustrates concerns over whether European military staffs can independently ensure continuity of their command systems.

Palantir's statement specifically denies the existence of a remote kill switch and describes European maintenance arrangements. Reliance on private software does not by itself establish that the contractor can deactivate the system at will.

The concentration of civilian and intelligence infrastructure among a limited number of providers also raises concerns about operational resilience. November incidents included a nationwide U.S. Federal Aviation Administration flight stoppage on November 13, attributed to a router failure in Arkansas amid deployment of the Smart System AI management platform; a widespread Microsoft Outlook outage; a U.S. National Security Agency infrastructure incident; and a cyberattack on the European Commission on November 19 reported by Politico. The Bank of England's November Financial Stability Review classified common and critical technology providers as potential channels of systemic contagion across the financial system.

The incidents had different reported causes and do not demonstrate a common failure mechanism. The Bank of England's assessment identifies a channel of possible contagion, not proof that the individual events caused a financial crisis.

Systemic Economic Risk

INFLATION • CREDIT • SUPPLY CHAINS
06

How Disruptions Can Spread Across Markets

Developments in late November 2026 illustrate a possible feedback loop between pressure on shipping routes, commodity protectionism, and sovereign debt. Escalation in strategic straits—including IRGC mine-strike claims, a fall to 125 weekly Hormuz transits, threats to Saudi ports, and damage to Russian and Ukrainian energy infrastructure—coincides with marine war-risk insurance near wartime peaks, U.S. diesel above $5.72 per gallon, and European gas storage at 74.6% before winter. At the same time, China's two-month extensions of export-control suspensions for rare earths until January 10 and for gallium, germanium, and antimony until January 27, proposed U.S. Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners, and reported Nvidia server shipment restrictions affecting four ASEAN countries under a 90-day licensing transition are fragmenting production and computing supply chains.

The logistics and trade measures may reinforce one another, but alternative routes, supply surpluses, and licensing exceptions could mitigate particular disruptions. The Iranian claims remain unconfirmed.

Rising logistics, diesel, electricity-generation, and semiconductor costs could keep inflation above target ranges. AI data centers are competing for grid capacity and planning gas generation in the 5–7 gigawatt range, while U.S. core PCE inflation is 3.3%, eurozone inflation 4%, and UK inflation 3.6%. This may lead central banks to maintain or increase borrowing costs. The Bank of Korea already raised its rate on November 26, while markets expect December tightening from the Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England, and Bank of Japan. OECD governments simultaneously face trillions of dollars in refinancing needs and have shifted issuance toward short-term bills, which make up 15% of their debt stock, while relying more on leveraged hedge funds.

Central-bank decisions depend on broader inflation and growth data. The coexistence of higher energy costs and sovereign refinancing needs does not determine the size or timing of future rate moves.

Higher borrowing costs and technology restrictions could further affect collateral values and sovereign balance sheets. Norway's GPFG reported a quarterly loss of $29.71 billion and has considered reducing sovereign-bond holdings. The reported loss of more than $4 trillion in Wall Street capitalization after Anthropic's IPO and S&P's negative review of Nvidia's rating illustrate pressure on corporate asset valuations. The weakening of fiscal buffers could encourage governments to introduce targeted taxes on banks and foreign sovereign funds, as in the UK; require private data-center developers to finance grid improvements, as proposed by the U.S. Senate; and rely more heavily on private defense infrastructure providers such as Starshield and Maven, raising questions about operational control.

Sovereign wealth fund losses, proposed taxes, grid-cost legislation, and defense procurement are distinct developments. Their simultaneous occurrence does not establish that one directly caused the others; the eventual distribution of costs depends on policy implementation and market conditions.

Economic & Strategic Outlook

PUBLIC FINANCE • TRADE • TECHNOLOGY
07

The Distribution of Risk and Control

Sovereign financial risk may be increasingly shared by leveraged hedge funds, commercial banks, and end borrowers and consumers rather than remaining solely on the balance sheets of OECD treasuries and sovereign wealth funds. Short-term Treasury bills now represent 15% of the debt stock, while higher energy costs, bank tax surcharges, and the removal of certain tax immunities create additional channels through which these pressures may be transmitted.

Governments and sovereign funds remain directly exposed to refinancing and valuation risks. The extent to which higher costs are passed to private borrowers and consumers will depend on financing conditions and the response of affected institutions.

Control over strategic passages at Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb and over critical-mineral processing gives Iran, the Houthis, and China's Ministry of Commerce potential influence over shipping prices, insurance costs, European gas-fired generation, and U.S. and EU defense industries. China's licensing requirements for seven medium and heavy rare earths remain in effect, while suspensions of other restrictions have been extended for only two months, into January 2027.

Alternative routes, substitute suppliers, and domestic processing investments may reduce exposure. Control or influence over particular routes or materials does not establish complete control over global supply.

High-performance AI computing, frontline satellite connectivity, and military software are increasingly shaped by political decisions. Nvidia, SpaceX, and Palantir operate within, or in some cases exercise independent influence over, the U.S. technology and national-security ecosystem. Dependent actors in ASEAN, Ukraine, and NATO countries consequently face potential licensing or operational disruptions.

The companies perform different roles, and the mechanisms governing their services are not identical. Licensing decisions, contractual safeguards, alternative suppliers, and the companies' stated service arrangements can all affect the likelihood and consequences of disruption.
