REGULATORY DEVELOPMENTS

BIS ACTIONS • NOVEMBER 5–18
01

BIS Actions and the 14-Day Regulatory Review

The review covers November 5–18, 2026, an inclusive 14-day period ending November 18. It also considers documents already available for Public Inspection and official BIS/OFAC materials relevant to imminent regulatory action.

Executive Summary

For November 5–18, 2026, the review confirmed one published, material BIS action directly relevant to export controls: an extension through January 10, 2027 of the suspension of a rule that would automatically apply Entity List/MEU restrictions to foreign companies at least 50% owned by restricted persons. Effective November 9, it temporarily eases rather than tightens the compliance burden on global supply chains.

The main development for the AI/GPU sector technically falls outside the publication window, but it had already been officially placed on Public Inspection and announced by BIS: the interim final rule (IFR) “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors,” scheduled for publication on November 19, 2026. It introduces licensing for remote access to large amounts of advanced computing by companies in Country Group D:5/Macau; new licenses for shipments of advanced AI chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil; and enhanced customer due diligence for data centers, foundries, and chip exporters.

As of November 18, this IFR had not yet taken effect: the Public Inspection text identifies November 19 as both the publication and effective date. After publication, the earliest practical impact is expected for NVIDIA/AMD, global cloud/GPU providers, and data-center operators in Southeast Asia, because the controls concern not only physical chip deliveries but also subsequent provision of computing capacity to remote users.

The risk is especially material for NVIDIA and AMD because their SEC disclosures already indicate that the H20/H200 and MI308/MI325 product lines are sensitive to China licensing. NVIDIA previously recorded multibillion-dollar H20 charges, while AMD reported charges and license requirements involving MI308/MI325.

Wafer-fabrication equipment manufacturers—Applied Materials, Lam Research, and KLA—retain substantial structural exposure to China. However, no new published BIS tightening specific to semiconductor manufacturing equipment was identified during the research window. Their main incremental 90-day risks stem from potential later expansion of controls, diversion enforcement, and customer due diligence. China accounted for approximately 35% and 34.5% of Lam and KLA's most recently reported quarterly revenue, respectively.

The reviewed OFAC actions during November 5–18 consisted primarily of sanctions updates involving Iran, drug trafficking, and terrorism. No established direct connection to GPUs, advanced semiconductors, or AI infrastructure was identified, so they are not included among sector-specific regulatory triggers.

EXPORT CONTROL IMPLEMENTATION

LICENSING • SUPPLY CHAINS
02

Pending Rules and Semiconductor Supply Chains

Documents Not Yet in Effect at the November 18 Cutoff

The principal pending document was BIS's “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors.” It was already available on Public Inspection, but the Federal Register banner explicitly identified November 19, 2026 as the scheduled publication date, and the text set the effective date as the publication date. Accordingly, it had no legal effect at the November 18 cutoff.

Following publication, the document provides a 60-day comment period. Assuming publication on November 19, the calculated closing date is January 18, 2027. Comments concern an IFR already in effect: an open comment period neither converts it into a proposed rule nor postpones its effectiveness.

Key IFR provisions: a license is required when advanced SI items will be used in a data center that provides more than 12 million aggregate TPP to one or more linked data centers for companies headquartered, or with an ultimate parent headquartered, in D:5 or Macau; a subsequent change in end use also triggers the rule. Country Group D:5 includes China, Russia, Iran, Belarus, and North Korea, among others. Macau is formally classified in D:1/D:3/D:4, so advanced-computing rules identify it separately.

For existing data centers already providing such amounts of computing to D:5/Macau companies before publication, BIS allows 90 days after the effective date to obtain a license. If the rule is published November 19, the approximate deadline is February 17, 2027.

The IFR also introduces destination-based licensing for advanced SI items going to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil because of diversion risk. An exception is available for U.S.-headquartered ultimate consignees/end users that meet new monthly reporting requirements. This may be the most important technical mechanism for AWS, Microsoft, Google, Oracle, CoreWeave, and other U.S. operators: the exception is not an unconditional safe harbor, because it requires transparency concerning parties, products, quantities, and transactions.

In addition, the IFR formally removes the remaining provisions of the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, which BIS previously had not enforced, and shifts emphasis toward more targeted destination- and end-use-based controls.

The November 9 final rule has another forward-looking date: January 10, 2027, when the extended stay of the Affiliates Rule expires unless BIS takes further action. This creates an important regulatory decision point within the next 90 days.

Supply-Chain Analysis

NVIDIA

Facts. H20 is already subject to separate licensing for China/Hong Kong/Macau and D:5; NVIDIA reported a $4.5 billion charge related to H20 in FY2026. The company also obtained licenses for limited H200 shipments to Chinese customers, but said it shipped only part of the authorized quantity and that those sales accounted for less than 1% of Data Center revenue in the relevant quarter.

Mechanism of the new risk. The November 19 IFR does not name B200/GB200/H200 individually, but covers advanced-computing items in ECCN 3A090/4A090 and related.z items and regulates remote access to substantial amounts of their computing capacity. B200/GB200-class infrastructure may therefore be more exposed than less powerful compliant products: a higher aggregate computing capacity at an overseas data center makes it more likely that the end-use threshold will be reached.

H20. The principal H20 risks remain D:5/China licensing and Chinese policy. The new remote-access IFR also limits the option of circumventing physical-shipment restrictions by renting GPUs outside China.

H200. BIS's January rule had already identified H200 as an example of an item eligible for case-by-case review under specified performance limits and conditions. The new IFR makes the third-country cloud route less flexible.

Next 90 days: high risk, primarily for incremental China/D:5 opportunities and distribution channels through Southeast Asia, rather than for overall global AI demand.

AMD

AMD confirms that MI308 is subject to China/D:5 license requirements and that it received specific licenses for MI325; MI325 must undergo inspection in the United States before shipment to China. AMD also relies on TSMC for all GPUs and CPUs manufactured on 7 nm and more advanced process nodes.

For MI300/MI325/MI350-class accelerator deployments, the new risk resembles NVIDIA's: even if a chip is physically installed in Singapore or Malaysia, providing significant computing capacity to a Chinese parent company or its affiliates may require BIS authorization. Ninety-day risk: high for China-related AI accelerators; medium for AMD overall.

TSMC / Samsung

The new IFR expressly requires due diligence not only from chip exporters and data centers but also from foreign foundries, including checks of ownership and control involving D:5/Macau customers. This increases the compliance burden for fabless designers and may intensify verification of corporate ownership before tape-out or wafer shipment.

TSMC separately discloses that U.S. export rules can already delay or prohibit shipments of certain advanced-node products and that its Nanjing operation relies on an annual U.S. export license to receive controlled items. The principal risk is therefore less an abrupt shutdown of TSMC/Samsung global production than heightened screening of Chinese chip designers and customers with complex ownership structures.

FDPR: no new FDPR amendment was identified in the research window. It would therefore be incorrect to characterize the November IFR as a new FDPR expansion.

ASML / Applied Materials / Lam Research / KLA

No newly published rule raising equipment-control thresholds for China was found during November 5–18. Nevertheless, existing exposure is substantial: ASML explicitly states that exports of EUV, certain DUV immersion systems, and other products require licenses, and that Chinese customers generated 29.1% of its 2025 revenue.

Lam Research reported that China generated 35% of quarterly revenue for the quarter ended September 27, 2026, and explicitly warns that expanded export-license requirements could adversely affect revenue and profit. KLA reported 34.5% of quarterly revenue from China, while Applied Materials reported approximately 25% of Q4 FY2026 revenue from China.

Next 90 days: medium risk to backlog and shipment timing, but high headline risk if BIS issues a new semiconductor manufacturing equipment rule. The November 19 IFR concerns advanced computing more directly than deposition, etching, or lithography.

Microsoft / Amazon / Google / Meta / Oracle / CoreWeave

This is a potentially underappreciated channel for the new IFR. BIS regulates not only physical GPU exports but also remote provision of substantial computing capacity from a foreign data center to a customer headquartered, or with an ultimate parent, in D:5 or Macau. Thus a hyperscaler may own GPUs in Singapore yet still face a license requirement depending on its customer and aggregate TPP.

The reporting-based exception for U.S.-headquartered end users in the five new diversion-risk destinations reduces risk for internal Microsoft/AWS/Google/Meta/Oracle workloads but does not eliminate compliance costs.

“Commercial recommender systems” are excluded from the new prohibited end use, so some recommendation workloads at Meta and online retailers may be less affected. This is not a company-wide exemption and does not automatically extend to model training, foundation-model workloads, or cloud services for third-party customers.

Next 90 days: high operational and compliance risk for AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle, and GPU-cloud operators; medium direct revenue risk.

Intel / Apple / Broadcom / Qualcomm / Tesla / Dell / HPE / Supermicro

For Intel/Broadcom/Qualcomm, the relevant mechanisms are classification of advanced-computing and networking components and corporate end-user screening; the new IFR does not identify these companies by name. Apple and Tesla face less direct AI-accelerator export risk but may experience secondary effects through China operations, cloud training infrastructure, and semiconductor sourcing.

Risks are higher for Dell/HPE/Supermicro because servers containing controlled advanced integrated circuits may be classified under 4A090 or related.z provisions, while BIS expressly covers advanced-computing commodities and data-center deployments.

Chinese Companies: Huawei / SMIC / Alibaba Cloud / Tencent Cloud / Baidu

For Huawei/SMIC, the new IFR does not repeal existing Entity List/FDPR or advanced-node restrictions. The more significant new mechanism is a narrowing of the third-country computing route: a Chinese company may not receive a substantial amount of controlled U.S.-technology computing through a foreign data center without BIS authorization.

Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud, and Baidu may be affected as companies headquartered in China even if their servers are located abroad. The rule focuses on headquarters and ultimate-parent location, not solely on where GPUs are physically situated.

MARKET IMPLICATIONS

COMPLIANCE • DIVERSION
04

Market and Compliance Implications

Less Visible Market Consequences

The first potentially underappreciated change is a shift from controlling “where the GPU is located” to controlling “who actually receives the computing capacity.” New §744.24 makes a subsequent change in end use a separate authorization trigger. Compliance therefore becomes an ongoing duty for the data center, rather than a one-time check when importing a server.

Second, Singapore and Malaysia are no longer neutral AI-hardware hubs for the controlled advanced chips covered by this rule: both are expressly named among five diversion-risk destinations, alongside Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil. This raises the risk of arrangements involving purchase and placement of a GPU outside China followed by remote access from China.

Third, the rule reaches linked data centers, including digitally connected facilities, rather than only a single physical campus. That reduces the scope for dividing a computing cluster among multiple sites to avoid the aggregate-compute threshold.

Fourth, the formal easing created by the November stay of the Affiliates Rule may prove temporary. January 10, 2027 is an important decision point: if the automatic 50%-ownership rule returns, restricted-party screening would expand substantially to subsidiaries of Entity List/MEU entities and certain SDNs.

Equities may be most sensitive when investors extrapolate China revenue without accounting for licensing: NVIDIA/AMD through accelerator opportunities, and Lam/KLA/Applied Materials through Chinese wafer-fabrication equipment demand. Company disclosures show that export restrictions have already affected inventory, revenue mix, and addressable markets.

Potential transshipment hubs are now clearly subject to greater scrutiny: BIS itself names Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil. Moving diversion routes into those jurisdictions may therefore become less attractive.

U.S.-headquartered hyperscalers and compliant data-center operators may benefit if they can document ownership, workloads, and end users and use the exception and reporting regime for their own projects in the five countries.

INVESTMENT & POLICY OUTLOOK

DECISION POINTS • REFERENCES
05

Investment Outlook and Regulatory Deadlines

Investment Implications

Under pressure. NVIDIA and AMD are the clearest group: China/D:5 regulatory risk now extends beyond physical shipments to remote access, narrowing the addressable market for workarounds. NVIDIA has already demonstrated material financial sensitivity of H20/H200 to licensing changes, and AMD has done so for MI308/MI325. Lam Research, KLA, and Applied Materials also remain exposed because of substantial China revenue, although the new November IFR is not an equipment-control rule.

Relatively insulated. Apple, Tesla, and parts of consumer-oriented semiconductor demand have less direct exposure to 3A090/4A090 data-center export controls. Meta also benefits narrowly from the “commercial recommender systems” exclusion, but training and foundation-model workloads are not automatically covered. TSMC/Samsung are relatively insulated by diversified global foundry demand, yet remain under customer-screening and advanced-node compliance pressure.

Potential beneficiaries. U.S.-based compliant cloud and data-center infrastructure could gain an advantage over less transparent third-country intermediaries because the new framework increases the value of auditable KYC, reporting, and traceability. Likewise, friend-shoring to the United States, Japan, South Korea, and other A:5/A:6-friendly ecosystems may become relatively more attractive, although no specific new incentive rule was issued during November 5–18.

A separate positive factor for parts of the supply chain is the extension of the Affiliates Rule stay through January 10. Over the coming weeks, it reduces the chance that unnamed subsidiaries will automatically become subject to Entity List/MEU licensing solely because of at least 50% restricted ownership.

Final Assessment and Monitoring Priorities

Over the next 90 days, the principal regulatory trigger is the November 19 publication and practical implementation of “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors.” Particular attention should go to BIS guidance and FAQs on calculating aggregate TPP, defining linked data centers, checking indirect ownership and control, and applying the commercial-recommender exemption.

The second deadline is January 18, 2027, the calculated end of the IFR's 60-day comment period. Comments from hyperscalers, chip vendors, foundries, and governments of the five diversion-risk countries may provide early signals of future revisions.

The third date is January 10, 2027, when the extended Affiliates Rule suspension expires. The return of an automatic 50%-ownership approach would considerably expand the number of restricted counterparties and increase risk for global distributors, equipment vendors, cloud providers, and fabless/foundry chains.

The fourth date is approximately February 17, 2027, when the 90-day license window expires for existing overseas data centers that continue supplying substantial controlled computing capacity to D:5/Macau companies.

NVIDIA, AMD, and operators of global GPU-cloud infrastructure appear most sensitive over this period. A second tier includes Lam Research, KLA, and Applied Materials because of substantial China exposure and the possibility that diversion enforcement could precede additional equipment controls.

Signals of further tightening include adding countries to the diversion-risk list; new Entity List/MEU designations; changes to policies of denial or case-by-case review; new rules for semiconductor manufacturing equipment or advanced-node fabs; broader due-diligence requirements for cloud intermediaries; and a BIS decision not to extend the Affiliates Rule stay after January 10. The first three mechanisms are directly provided for or discussed within the current BIS regulatory framework.
