MONETARY THEORY

MACRO STRATEGY • BALANCE SHEETS
01

THE TWO-TIER MONETARY ARCHITECTURE

The architecture of modern fiat monetary systems relies on a foundation that has historically been misinterpreted in classical economic textbooks. For decades, the dominant academic paradigm leaned on the "money multiplier" model, which posited that central banks control the money supply by injecting reserves into the banking system, which commercial banks then multiply by lending out a fraction of those deposits. Data indicates that this model of fractional-reserve financial intermediation historically diverges from empirical and operational realities. In the modern economy, money creation functions as an endogenous process driven by commercial bank lending, while the central bank operates to manage the price of liquidity, ensure the integrity of the settlement system, and, under extraordinary circumstances, directly expand the broad money supply through asset purchases.

To analyze the money creation process, it is necessary to distinguish between two distinct forms of money circulating in the modern economy: central bank money (base money) and commercial bank money (broad money). Base money, often referred to as high-powered money or the monetary base (M0), consists of physical currency (banknotes and coins) in circulation and electronic reserves held in commercial bank accounts at the central bank. Base money represents a direct liability of the central bank. Physical cash is the only form of central bank money accessible to the general public. For the central bank, issued banknotes represent a liability, whereas for economic actors, they function as a liquid asset.

Conversely, electronic reserves are accessible exclusively to commercial banks, specific non-bank financial institutions, and state treasuries. These reserves serve as the ultimate settlement medium in the economy. When an individual or corporate entity transfers bank deposits to another entity at a different commercial bank, the transaction is finalized only when an equivalent value of reserves is transferred between the respective banks' accounts at the central bank. Thus, central bank reserves act as the underlying infrastructure of the financial system, maintaining public confidence in commercial bank deposits by ensuring their convertibility into state-backed fiat currency.

Broad money encompasses the funds held by households and non-financial corporations. In advanced economies, such as the United Kingdom, up to ninety-seven percent of the broad money supply exists exclusively in the form of electronic commercial bank deposits. These bank deposits are not backed by physical vaults of cash; rather, they serve as unsecured liabilities of commercial banks—promissory notes pledging to pay central bank money on demand. A critical operational factor is that central bank reserves cannot be "lent out" to the public. Reserves cannot exit the central bank's balance sheet (unless converted into physical cash) and can only be transferred between entities holding reserve accounts.

The structural reality indicates that broad money operates primarily as an unsecured liability of commercial banks—promises to pay central bank money on demand—while central bank reserves function as the underlying settlement infrastructure. Because reserves cannot leave the central bank's balance sheet except as physical cash, the aggregate quantity of reserves does not act as a direct, mechanical constraint on the volume of broad money that commercial banks can generate.

Counter-argument: If the volume of physical cash withdrawals by the public were to increase significantly, the assumption that reserves do not constrain broad money creation could be tested, as banks would need to convert electronic reserves into physical currency, directly reducing their reserve buffers and potentially limiting their capacity to maintain deposit liabilities.

COMMERCIAL BANKING

ENDOGENOUS MONEY • CREDIT EXPANSION
02

ENDOGENOUS MONEY CREATION

The operational reality of money creation aligns with the post-Keynesian theory of endogenous money. Commercial banks do not act simply as intermediaries lending out pre-existing savings. Instead, they function as the primary creators of the broad money supply, reacting to the economy's demand for credit. When a commercial bank approves a loan—such as a mortgage or a corporate credit facility—it does not transfer existing funds from a depositor's account to the borrower. Rather, the bank simultaneously generates a new asset and a new liability on its balance sheet.

The credit agreement constitutes an asset (the borrower's promise to repay the debt with interest). Concurrently, the bank credits the borrower's account with a deposit equal to the loan amount, establishing a new liability. Through this fundamental accounting action, new broad money is created ex nihilo. As some economists note, such deposits can be classified as "fountain pen money".

Commercial Bank Balance Sheet Assets Liabilities
Loan Issuance + Loan to Customer (e.g., $100,000) + Deposit Account (e.g., $100,000)

This endogenous creation process suggests that the money supply expands organically in response to credit demand from the real economy. In endogenous models, the causal relationship between investment and savings is inverted: credit availability enables investment expansion, which subsequently leads to a corresponding expansion of savings and bank deposits. Conversely, when a borrower repays the principal of a loan, the deposit account is debited, and the loan asset decreases, resulting in the permanent destruction of broad money. Banks also manage their balance sheets by issuing long-term debt instruments or equity; when non-bank entities purchase these instruments using bank deposits, the volume of deposit liabilities in the system contracts, similarly resulting in money destruction.

While commercial banks possess the operational capacity to create money, they face soft and hard budget constraints that restrict credit expansion. First, profitability and credit risk play a determinative role. Banks operate to generate shareholder value and will generate money only if a loan yields a sufficient risk-adjusted return. If the macroeconomic environment deteriorates, perceived credit risk elevates, and banks will contract lending irrespective of reserve availability, as real-economy credit demand acts as the primary constraint.

Second, regulatory frameworks, such as Basel III, mandate that banks hold a specific ratio of high-quality capital (Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital) against their risk-weighted assets. Tier 1 capital comprises equity capital and retained earnings, establishing a threshold for absorbing losses without ceasing operations. When a bank generates a loan, its risk-weighted assets increase. If it lacks adequate equity capital to absorb potential losses, it is legally restricted from extending the credit.

Third, liquidity and settlement limitations require banks to manage their balance sheets closely. Although banks initially create deposits and subsequently seek reserves, they still require central bank reserves to settle interbank transactions arising from these deposits. If a funded borrower immediately transfers their deposit to another bank, the originating bank must transfer an equivalent sum of reserves to the receiving bank. To maintain their reserve buffers, banks must attract deposits or borrow funds in the interbank market, incurring a cost of funding. The central bank governs the price of this liquidity (the key policy rate), thereby influencing commercial banks' funding costs and, consequently, the rates they charge borrowers.

Data suggests that the money supply expands organically as a function of real-sector credit demand, wherein banks simultaneously create loans and deposits. This endogenous expansion is primarily constrained by perceived macroeconomic credit risk, regulatory capital requirements mandating sufficient Tier 1 equity, and the cost of interbank settlement liquidity managed by the central bank.

Counter-argument: The assertion that real-sector credit demand is the primary constraint on endogenous money creation would be challenged if regulatory frameworks (such as Basel III capital requirements) or interbank settlement costs became so prohibitive that banks restricted lending even in an environment of high borrower demand and low perceived credit risk.

LIQUIDITY MANAGEMENT

REPO MECHANICS • IFRS 9
03

CENTRAL BANK OPERATIONS AND REPO MECHANICS

While commercial banks create broad money, central banks hold a monopoly on the creation of base money (reserves). Central banks expand or contract the supply of reserves to maintain control over short-term interest rates and facilitate the uninterrupted functioning of payment systems. The primary tool for systemic liquidity management is the repurchase agreement (REPO) and the reverse REPO. A REPO essentially functions as a collateralized loan. If the banking system requires supplementary liquidity to settle payments, the central bank injects reserves by purchasing high-quality assets (such as government bonds) from commercial banks, under an agreement that the banks will repurchase the assets on a specified date at a slightly elevated price (representing the interest rate).

Balance Sheet Event Assets Liabilities
Central Bank (REPO) + Claims on Banks (Securities) + Reserve Balances
Commercial Bank (REPO) + Reserve Balances
- Securities (Asset Swap)		 (No change in total assets)

Conversely, to absorb excess liquidity, the central bank conducts reverse REPOs, selling securities to banks and withdrawing reserves from the system. Prior to the Global Financial Crisis, the US Federal Reserve actively managed its balance sheet using daily fixed-quantity REPO operations to maintain the federal funds rate near its target. Subsequently, the transition to a "corridor" system, and later to a "floor" system (involving interest paid on reserves), altered liquidity management dynamics.

The accounting treatment of REPOs highlights the complex nature of financial risk transfer. Under International Financial Reporting Standard (IFRS) 9, transferring the legal title of an asset does not automatically trigger its derecognition from the transferring party's balance sheet. IFRS 9 utilizes a multi-step derecognition model that primarily assesses whether the transferring entity has retained "substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership". In a standard REPO, a commercial bank temporarily sells a government bond to the central bank in exchange for cash. However, because the commercial bank is contractually obligated to repurchase the bond at a fixed price, it retains exposure to the bond's market price fluctuations and credit risk.

Consequently, under IFRS 9, the commercial bank does not derecognize the bond. The bond remains on its balance sheet, and the received cash (reserves) is recorded as an asset, offset by a corresponding financial liability (a loan payable to the central bank). This contrasts with US GAAP (ASC 860), which relies more heavily on a control-based model and the concept of "legal isolation" in bankruptcy, though both frameworks generally treat standard REPOs as secured borrowings rather than outright sales.

The architecture of systemic liquidity management relies heavily on collateralized lending through REPO operations, wherein accounting frameworks such as IFRS 9 dictate that transferring legal ownership of an asset does not automatically trigger derecognition. Consequently, commercial banks retain the underlying market and credit risks of the pledged securities on their balance sheets, maintaining exposure to volatility despite the temporary influx of central bank reserves.

Counter-argument: The premise that commercial banks retain practically all risks associated with ownership under IFRS 9 in REPO transactions could be weakened if the contractual terms of the repurchase agreements were structured such that the central bank absorbed potential credit defaults or significant market price fluctuations of the underlying securities, which would necessitate derecognition by the commercial bank.

BALANCE SHEET DYNAMICS

QUANTITATIVE EASING • FISCAL RISK
04

QUANTITATIVE EASING AND THE FISCAL NEXUS

When conventional monetary policy reaches the zero lower bound, central banks employ unconventional balance sheet expansion, most notably Quantitative Easing (QE). The fundamental objective of QE is to inject broad money directly into the real economy and suppress long-term interest rates. A common misconception posits that QE involves distributing "free money" to commercial banks for lending. If a central bank purchases government bonds exclusively from commercial banks, the transaction represents a simple asset swap: the commercial bank exchanges a bond for central bank reserves. This increases base money but does not generate new broad money, as the commercial bank's liabilities to the public remain unchanged.

QE achieves its macroeconomic objective by purchasing assets from non-bank financial institutions (NBFIs), such as pension funds, insurance companies, and asset managers. Because NBFIs do not hold reserve accounts at the central bank, these transactions must be intermediated by commercial banks. When the central bank purchases a bond from a pension fund, it credits the reserve account of the pension fund's commercial bank, creating new base money. Simultaneously, the commercial bank credits the pension fund's deposit account, creating new broad money.

Entity Balance Sheet (QE via NBFI) Assets Liabilities
Central Bank + Government Bonds + Reserve Balances (Comm. Bank)
Commercial Bank + Reserve Balances + Deposit Account (Pension Fund)
Pension Fund + Deposit Account
- Government Bonds		 (No change)

This dual creation mechanism induces a portfolio rebalancing effect. The pension fund, now holding lower-yielding bank deposits instead of long-term bonds, is incentivized to allocate this liquidity into higher-risk assets, elevating asset prices and lowering borrowing costs across the economy. While QE proved highly effective in averting deflationary spirals, it fundamentally altered the fiscal dynamics of sovereign states. By purchasing fixed-rate, long-term government debt and financing it via floating-rate overnight central bank reserves, central banks effectively executed a massive debt swap on behalf of the consolidated state.

For example, the Bank of England's Asset Purchase Facility (APF) peaked at £895 billion, including £875 billion of government bonds. During a decade of low rates, the bank rate paid on newly created reserves was lower than the coupon income received from government bonds, generating substantial profits transferred to the UK Treasury. Initial estimates suggested the program could yield the Treasury a fiscal dividend between £35 billion and £78 billion. However, when inflation surged in 2022 and the Bank of England rapidly hiked the policy rate, the cost of remunerating commercial bank reserves eclipsed the fixed coupon income on the APF bond portfolio.

This dynamic transformed historical profits into severe cash losses. Because QE shifted a massive portion of sovereign debt from fixed-rate to floating-rate exposures, the Treasury was obligated to cover these losses, adding tens of billions to public debt servicing costs. The Institute for Fiscal Studies (IFS) proposed introducing a tiered reserve system to avoid paying the full rate on all reserves, estimating potential savings of £30 to £45 billion. This illustrates a second-order reality: QE intricately intertwines monetary policy implementation with public finances.

The central bank balance sheet is divided into monetary policy instruments (e.g., refinancing operations, REPO, QE) and autonomous factors (e.g., physical banknotes in circulation, government deposits, foreign exchange reserves). The application of IFRS to central banks introduces complexities regarding the valuation of foreign exchange reserves and monetary gold. Under IFRS 9, debt instruments held to collect contractual cash flows and for sale are measured at fair value through other comprehensive income (FVOCI). Unrealized gains and losses caused by exchange rate fluctuations and gold price movements are isolated in specialized capital accounts known as "Revaluation Reserves" to prevent the artificial inflation of distributable central bank profits. Data shows that central banks can operate effectively even with negative equity resulting from QE portfolio revaluation losses, as their primary liability is non-redeemable fiat currency.

Quantitative Easing intricately binds monetary policy implementation to state finances by converting fixed-rate long-term sovereign debt into floating-rate overnight reserve liabilities. As demonstrated by the Bank of England's Asset Purchase Facility, rapid monetary tightening via interest rate hikes immediately deteriorates the sovereign's fiscal position by transforming historical central bank profits into direct treasury losses, shifting the burden of duration risk onto the consolidated state balance sheet.

Counter-argument: The conclusion that QE inherently deteriorates the sovereign fiscal position during tightening cycles would be mitigated if the central bank implemented a tiered reserve remuneration system, as proposed by the Institute for Fiscal Studies, which would prevent the payment of the full policy rate on all commercial bank reserves and thus reduce the direct cash losses transferred to the treasury.

FUTURE INFRASTRUCTURE

DIGITAL CURRENCY • SYSTEMIC RISKS
05

THE THREAT OF RETAIL CBDC

The mechanics of money creation face a paradigm shift due to the impending implementation of retail Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs). Currently, the two-tier system strictly segregates base money (accessible only to banks) and broad money (accessible to the public). A retail CBDC would disrupt this architecture by granting households and non-financial corporations direct access to the central bank's balance sheet. If a citizen converts a commercial bank deposit into a retail CBDC, the commercial bank deposit liability is extinguished. The commercial bank must then transfer an equivalent sum of its central bank reserves to the central bank to "pay" for the CBDC issuance. The central bank consequently issues a direct CBDC liability to the citizen.

This process presents a structural threat to the endogenous money creation mechanism. As citizens transfer funds into CBDCs, commercial banks lose their cheapest and most stable funding source (retail deposits), while their liquid reserves are drained. To maintain their asset portfolios (long-term loans), commercial banks would be compelled to seek wholesale market funding or rely heavily on the central bank's discount window.

The implementation of a retail Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) introduces a structural shift that threatens the endogenous money creation mechanism. By allowing households direct access to central bank liabilities, a large-scale conversion of commercial bank deposits into CBDCs would drain liquid reserves and deprive commercial banks of their most stable funding source, potentially shifting the locus of credit allocation back to the centralized balance sheet of the state.

Counter-argument: The projection that CBDCs will centralize credit allocation and undermine endogenous money creation relies on the assumption of large-scale public conversion of commercial deposits into CBDCs; if households and non-financial corporations opt not to convert their funds into direct central bank liabilities, commercial banks would retain their retail deposit funding base and their capacity for decentralized credit expansion.
