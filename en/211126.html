POWER AND CAPACITY

SHENZHEN SUMMIT • GLOBAL SYSTEMS
01

SHENZHEN SUMMIT: POWER AND CAPACITY

When the leaders of the United States, China, and Russia sat together at a round table at Shenzhen's Qilin Villa for what the source describes as their first such meeting, Beijing's official statements and the joint declaration issued by the three capitals emphasized the dialogue's “pioneering significance,” the inadmissibility of nuclear war, and the stabilization of global energy flows. Beyond the ceremonial photographs, however, the report identifies substantial material and institutional strains. Less than two hours before Air Force One landed in China, the U.S. Air Force launched an intercontinental ballistic missile—an exercise the military described as routine and scheduled years in advance. In Washington, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) released a report acknowledging that the war with Iran had worsened a “chronic” shortage of key munitions in U.S. stockpiles already depleted by supplies sent to Ukraine.

At the same time, Wall Street presented a financial paradox. Anthropic conducted what the source calls the largest initial public offering on record, selling 980 million shares at $83 each for a total of $81.34 billion, including $79.38 billion from newly issued shares, and raising another $26 billion in a private placement with technology partners. At the opening of trading, the company's valuation exceeded $2 trillion; by the close, its fully diluted valuation was approximately $1.8 trillion, still substantially above the $1.5 trillion implied by the offering price. Yet the broader market fell: the S&P 500 lost 2%, recording a sixth consecutive declining session for the first time since 2024. Shares of Nvidia, the leading supplier of AI accelerators, fell another 2.7% after a 2.9% decline the previous day amid controversy surrounding Jensen Huang's resignation, his alleged connection to a suspected Chinese intelligence operative, and congressional anti-corruption inquiries into company donations to the White House.

The convergence of political, military, energy, and technological developments—from Shenzhen to Jerusalem, Canberra, and Brussels—suggests a common pattern in the source's interpretation. Displays of power by major states and an unprecedented concentration of capital in artificial intelligence may increasingly coexist with the physical limits of defense manufacturing, pressure on sovereign debt and energy systems, and weakening institutional safeguards in major democracies.

Counter-argument: The same evidence also shows functioning diplomatic channels, substantial new private-sector financing, and ongoing government efforts to expand production. Those developments do not remove the constraints identified in the report, but they limit any inference that institutional or industrial capacity has already failed across these systems.

02

DIPLOMACY, TWO WARS, AND MUNITIONS SHORTAGES

The 90-minute meeting of Xi Jinping, Donald Trump, and Vladimir Putin on the sidelines of the APEC summit illustrated the different meanings attached to the trilateral format by the three capitals. In the Chinese interpretation, supported by experts at Renmin University of China, the negotiations represented a proposal for “self-restraint” in a multipolar world: an alternative both to unipolar dominance and to a Yalta-style division into spheres of influence, grounded in respect for sovereignty and “red lines.” Moscow, by contrast, openly promoted the idea of a “new, modern Yalta,” treating its place at the table with U.S. and Chinese leaders as a breach in Western diplomatic isolation. The report notes, however, that Russia's economy is approximately one-eighth the size of China's and one-eleventh that of the United States, and that Russia has no comparable role in the AI race.

The American position revealed a further contrast between ceremony and leverage. According to the source, Xi personally greeted a foreign leader at the airport for the first time during his leadership, a gesture China had not made since Mao Zedong welcomed Nikita Khrushchev in 1959 and a response to Trump's September reception at Andrews Air Force Base. Trump, whose party had just lost elections for both chambers of Congress, emphasized the pageantry, honor guard, and leaders' personal rapport in an interview with Fox News: “They both like Trump. I can't say what happens after me.” The hosts' treatment of U.S. allies carried a different message. Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, whose statement a year earlier about possible intervention in a Taiwan crisis had strained relations with Beijing, was met at the airport by a former Chinese ambassador to Greece. Xi offered only a silent handshake at the reception, while the orchestra played the 1961 hit “Sukiyaki” (“Ue O Muite Arukou”), a song inspired by protests against the U.S.-Japan security treaty. Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. was greeted with “Anak,” a song about a repentant rebel asking his parents' forgiveness, prompting online discussion of a possible allusion to Manila's territorial dispute with China in the South China Sea.

The report argues that the leaders' most substantive point of convergence in the Shenzhen “peace declaration” concerned limits on supranational justice rather than disarmament. Trump pressed for reductions in nuclear arsenals, while Xi avoided binding commitments, stating that “a nuclear war cannot be won” and proposing crisis-response mechanisms. All three leaders instead emphasized state consent and official immunity, warning the International Criminal Court (ICC) against “abuse of jurisdiction.” The wording was diplomatically useful to Putin, who is subject to an ICC arrest warrant. It also aligned with the Trump administration's previous sanctions against the court over arrest warrants for Israeli leaders, including Benjamin Netanyahu.

Counter-argument: Agreement on the court's jurisdiction does not by itself establish a unified geopolitical strategy. The declaration's lack of binding nuclear reductions and the leaders' different interpretations of the meeting indicate that this was a narrower convergence than their public appearances might suggest.

While Shenzhen's declarations called for harmony, the wars under discussion exposed the limitations of summit diplomacy. From aboard Air Force One, Trump joined a Berlin meeting of the “coalition of the willing” by using the phone of his son-in-law Jared Kushner. The call included Volodymyr Zelenskyy, German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, French President Emmanuel Macron, and British Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Trump told the Europeans that Putin was tired of the war and ready for a deal, urging them to stop spending billions of dollars on Kyiv. At roughly the same time, Putin in Shenzhen appeared to contradict that assurance. Following a Ukrainian missile-and-drone strike on the 19-kilometer Kerch Bridge—completed in 2018 at a cost of $4 billion and reportedly causing the collapse of part of its four-lane roadway—Putin announced his withdrawal from consultations on a partial ceasefire covering energy infrastructure and the Black Sea and promised a “far more painful” response.

The negotiating framework discussed by Kushner and Steve Witkoff also faced obstacles. According to The Washington Post, American intermediaries were considering security guarantees for Ukraine modeled on NATO's Article 5 but without NATO troop deployments; automatic restoration of sanctions in the event of another attack; a special European Union accession process that could take up to 10 years; and even a hypothetical path to NATO membership in exchange for a referendum on withdrawal of Ukrainian troops from the remaining Kyiv-controlled part of Donbas. In practice, however, an October Kyiv School of Economics study reported that Russian airstrikes on Kyiv more than tripled in 2026, from 2.6 a day in the first half of the year to 9.8 in October. Monthly air-raid alert time rose from 32 hours in June to 172 hours in October, and households in the capital were without electricity for seven to nine hours a day. According to Reuters, Ukraine struck 15 of Russia's 35 largest oil refineries in November, reducing oil processing by 45%. Kyiv also reported a five-day slowdown in SpaceX Starshield satellite terminals along the front, complicating drone interception over energy facilities. Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called on Putin across the APEC table to “end the war immediately,” but his decision to appear in the group photograph with the Russian leader angered members of Australia's Ukrainian community, who recalled the deaths of 38 compatriots aboard flight MH17.

Counter-argument: The continued discussion of security guarantees, sanctions mechanisms, and EU accession shows that diplomatic options remain under consideration. The increase in air attacks, refinery strikes, and disruptions to communications nonetheless provides little evidence that a ceasefire was imminent at the time described.

The material limits of U.S. power were similarly visible in the Middle East. The U.S.-Israeli war against Iran, Operation Epic Fury, began on February 28, 2026, and, after an April ceasefire, developed into a prolonged war of attrition. By mid-November, it had cost the U.S. budget approximately $48.3 billion, excluding repairs to damaged equipment. The preceding week brought another escalation in the Persian Gulf: after Iranian strikes on U.S. facilities in Bahrain, home to the U.S. Navy's Fifth Fleet, on Friday, Sunday, and Tuesday morning, a U.S. fighter jet bombed a launch position on Qeshm Island. Tehran responded on Wednesday with its first drone strike in two months against Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and renewed attacks on Bahrain, while continuing strikes against tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. Western financial publications and Wall Street focused on this Gulf escalation, while Chinese and Israeli publications emphasized Shenzhen's commitments to keeping the strait open or internal disputes within Israel's security establishment.

A GAO report revealed significant management weaknesses behind Washington's military messaging. The Pentagon lacked a single overarching plan to replenish arsenals and compiled ammunition data from 37 disconnected systems, some updated partly by hand. Acting Secretary of the Navy Hung Cao told senators that the fleet had to “recode” expired Tomahawk missiles, likening the practice to sprinkling stale bread with water and microwaving it. This acknowledgment contrasted with earlier assurances from Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who had repeatedly dismissed reports of shortages of Patriot interceptors and other missiles as “fake news.”

The political cost of the war was also affecting the budget process on Capitol Hill. November election exit polls indicated that 61% of voters in Iowa and majorities in eight additional states won by Democrats considered the war with Iran a mistake. On Thursday, the Senate narrowly rejected a war powers resolution, 51–48, while three Republicans—Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins, and Rand Paul—joined Democrats in support. The day before, Republican majority leader John Thune suspended a party-line push for the White House's proposed $350 billion defense package. The proposal included $53 billion for emergency increases in Patriot, THAAD, and Tomahawk missile production of 680%, 2,200%, and 1,300%, respectively; $54.6 billion for drones; and more than 90% of a $17.9 billion allocation for the “Golden Dome” missile-defense system. Fiscal conservatives led by Rand Paul and House Budget Committee Chairman Jodey Arrington resisted adding unfunded spending to a $40 trillion national debt ahead of a possible government shutdown on December 12 and a reduction in the Republican Senate majority on December 15. Writing in The Washington Post, authors affiliated with Palantir warned that unless established suppliers such as RTX and Lockheed Martin faced greater competition—97% of missile funding still went to legacy systems requiring two to four years for delivery—even very large appropriations might not close the gap quickly enough.

Counter-argument: The proposed $350 billion package, explicit production targets, and acknowledgment of fragmented inventory systems show that the capacity problem is being addressed through policy and oversight. Whether those measures succeed depends on funding approval and the lengthy production schedules described in the same account.

03

ENERGY SHIFTS, COAL, AND TREASURY OUTFLOWS

Threats to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz accelerated the redirection of energy supplies and intensified pressure on global debt markets. In the Shenzhen declaration, the U.S., Chinese, and Russian leaders agreed to increase the production and supply of petroleum products, which was interpreted in Washington as a possible signal of easing energy sanctions on Russia. Before the summit, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth arrived in Caracas, less than 11 months after a January 3, 2026, U.S. special operations raid that resulted in Nicolás Maduro's capture and the deaths of about 80 Cuban and Venezuelan service members. Hegseth signed a military-partnership and counternarcotics agreement with interim president Delcy Rodríguez after a recent strike on a base belonging to Colombia's ELN group, amid a U.S. maritime campaign that had killed at least 240 people since September 2025 despite limited public evidence. He explicitly linked the pact to an August agreement under which U.S. company North American Blue Energy Partners would develop 17 oil fields with estimated reserves of about 65 billion barrels. In Hegseth's formulation, Venezuelan oil made the Strait of Hormuz “irrelevant to the Western Hemisphere.” Guatemalan presidential candidate Roberto Arzú, whose family has operated businesses in Venezuela for 40 years, argued that even pro-American politicians in the region were dissatisfied that Washington had removed Maduro while leaving the former political system in place for the sake of oil rather than securing free elections.

Counter-argument: New development agreements covering large estimated reserves do not by themselves establish that crude oil can be produced, transported, or substituted quickly enough to neutralize shipping disruptions in the Persian Gulf. The report's own account of regional criticism also shows that the political benefits of the Venezuela policy remain contested.

The Middle East crisis also exposed tensions within climate policy. International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates cited in the report projected that disruptions at Hormuz and industrial expansion in China and India would push global coal demand to a record 8.94 billion metric tons in 2026. IEA analysts forecast that Australia would overtake Indonesia by 2030 to become the world's largest coal exporter again, while Australian metallurgical coal exports to India would rise from 65 million tons in 2025 to 90 million tons by 2035. These forecasts complicated Canberra's position at the United Nations COP31 climate summit in Turkey, where Climate Minister Chris Bowen sought a final agreement amid 123 disputed formulations concerning climate finance. Ministers from Tuvalu and Fiji criticized the Albanese government's approval of nearly 40 coal and gas projects since 2022. In Australia, Rising Tide activists in kayaks again disrupted the Port of Newcastle after courts rejected a police ban, dismissed most earlier charges for insufficient evidence, and ordered police to pay legal costs. Canberra's proposal to require Queensland liquefied natural gas exporters to allocate up to 20% of output to the domestic market was stalled in the Senate amid industry criticism. Western Australia, meanwhile, secured an indefinite exemption from the bill, despite a state parliamentary report leaked the previous day detailing problems with its own 15% reservation system.

Counter-argument: The coal demand and export projections describe a possible market trajectory, not an irreversible outcome. The continuing COP31 negotiations, proposed gas reservation policy, and public challenges to coal projects indicate that energy policy remains subject to political and legal decisions.

Energy-related inflation is also putting pressure on public finances and investors' exposure to U.S. Treasury securities. Chinese rating agency CCXI revised its outlooks on France and the Philippines to “negative.” In France, the budget deficit was approaching 5.4% of GDP and public debt 119.3% of GDP ahead of the 2027 election. In the Philippines, the Middle East energy price surge increased subsidy costs, widened the current-account deficit to 4.4% of GDP, weakened the peso by 5.5%, and accompanied a 23.9% fall in foreign direct investment amid an infrastructure corruption scandal. September U.S. Treasury data showed aggregate foreign holdings of U.S. government debt falling $75.5 billion over the month to $9.24 trillion. Major sellers included France, down $31.4 billion; China, down $26.8 billion to $563.9 billion, its lowest level since July 2008; the United Kingdom, down $8.7 billion; and Japan, down $5.2 billion to $1.063 trillion. Belgium, by contrast, added $17.4 billion to reach $507.9 billion, and Canada added $3.1 billion to reach $467.7 billion.

Counter-argument: A net monthly decline in foreign holdings does not mean all major investors were withdrawing. The increases recorded in Belgium and Canada demonstrate that portfolio adjustments differed by country, while the report does not establish the individual motives behind every transaction.

In Australia, higher borrowing costs—after five Reserve Bank of Australia rate increases in a year—and inflation were raising recession concerns. The 0.8-percentage-point increase from January's 4.1% low exceeded the classic 0.5-percentage-point Sahm Rule threshold, although the RBA used a three-month average and a 0.75-point benchmark. Major-city home prices declined 8.1% over seven months. Counter-argument: The Sahm Rule comparison in the report uses the change from a January low, whereas the cited central-bank benchmark relies on a three-month average. The data therefore identify increasing recession risk but do not, on their own, establish that the RBA's preferred trigger has been met.

FINANCING • EXPORT CONTROLS • POWER GRIDS
04

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE: VALUATIONS AND CONSTRAINTS

The divergence between financial valuations and physical capacity was especially pronounced in artificial intelligence. Anthropic's public offering exceeded SpaceX's $75 billion IPO in June. At $83 per share, above the indicated $70–$78 range, the offering brought the company more than $105 billion in direct proceeds—$79.38 billion from new shares and $26 billion from a private placement involving Google, Microsoft, Nvidia, and AMD—against a gross transaction value exceeding $107 billion. At the initial price, each of the seven cofounders held a stake worth about $20 billion, while Ashton Kutcher and Guy Oseary's Sound Ventures saw its $435 million investment increase almost sixteenfold to $7 billion.

By the end of the trading day, however, Anthropic shares had retreated from an intraday high of $115 to $95–$97, while Micron fell 1.8%, CoreWeave 5.5%, and Nvidia 2.7%. October Federal Reserve minutes cited “rapidly increasing debt” associated with AI, off-balance-sheet lease obligations, and “circular financing,” in which chipmakers and cloud providers invest in AI laboratories and receive funds back through multiyear server rental agreements. Anthropic had already committed to nearly $900 billion in computing purchases over five to eight years; Counterpoint Research separately estimated $450 billion in commitments through 2030. These obligations were set against quarterly revenue of approximately $16 billion–$17 billion, an annualized run rate of $81 billion by late October, and continued operating losses. Company disclosures also noted a summer slowdown in enterprise demand for programming tools as customers switched to cheaper alternatives. Asian skeptics, including Shanghai investor Robin Du, questioned the durability of Western AI revenue growth and argued that Chinese open models cost one-tenth as much.

Counter-argument: A high valuation and large long-term computing commitments do not, by themselves, prove that a company's spending cannot be funded. The same figures show rapid revenue growth and substantial fresh capital, although the source leaves the timing of future cash flows and profitability unresolved.

China's AI market showed a different pattern. From its June peak, Shanghai's Star 50 index of AI chip manufacturers fell more than 40%, prompting local funds to reallocate toward established consumer companies such as Kweichow Moutai and Midea. Regulators also sought to counter panic-inducing social-media rumors, referred to as “short essays.” Alibaba, owner of the South China Morning Post, reported 53% growth in its AI cloud segment to 61 billion yuan and accelerated the migration of Qwen model training to its own T-Head processors. Management nonetheless warned that U.S. technology restrictions could slow its cloud business growth to 30%–45% in the following year. Beijing-based independent AI safety center Concordia found that, from mid-2025 to mid-2026, only five of China's 10 largest model developers disclosed the results of safety evaluations.

The technological contest intersected with disputes inside Washington through the Nvidia crisis. The source describes Jensen Huang's resignation after disclosures about his relationship with Alice Huang, the head of Singapore cloud company Megaspeed, which the U.S. Departments of Justice and Commerce suspected of illegally transferring chips to China; U.S. officials reportedly described her as a suspected Chinese intelligence agent. According to The Washington Post, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's department had for months delayed closing a loophole that allowed remote cloud access to Nvidia chips through companies such as Megaspeed, which had purchased billions of dollars' worth of processors. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and the National Security Council eventually pressed Commerce to adopt stricter rules. Democratic Representative Robert Garcia, expected to chair the House Oversight Committee, sent 12 preservation letters as part of a review of Nvidia's multimillion-dollar contributions to a fund for the White House presidential ballroom, whose cost exceeded $600 million. The donations reportedly preceded the company's permission to ship products to China and the end of a Commerce Department investigation. Acting Nvidia chief Colette Kress moved salary reviews from March to December to retain employees in China, while Bernstein analysts cut their share price target in half, from $400 to $200. Interpretations in the press differed: The Washington Post columnist Ramesh Ponnuru saw the controversy as evidence of Democrats moving toward a tougher China policy relative to Trump, while The Australian columnist Holman Jenkins suggested Huang had played a role in a deliberate two-track White House strategy to restrain China without provoking desperate cyberattacks or theft of AI model weights.

Counter-argument: The competing newspaper interpretations and the fact that some allegations remained under investigation limit conclusions about motive or coordination. The chronology of donations, export permissions, and regulatory actions warrants scrutiny, but it is not by itself proof of an improper exchange.

Physical constraints also threatened to slow global AI expansion. Natixis investment bankers warned that new U.S. export restrictions on third countries could cool the merger-and-acquisition boom in Southeast Asia, which attracted $236 billion in foreign direct investment in 2025 under “China+1” strategies—twice the amount received by China. To weaken China's concentration of approximately 70% of rare-earth mining and 90% of processing, and to support Trump's promise to “catch up” with Beijing within 10 months—a timetable experts in Shanghai and Washington called unrealistic—the U.S. Department of the Interior on Thursday sold the first deep-sea mining licenses covering 31.5 million acres of Pacific seabed near American Samoa to Wetstone for $6 million, despite a court finding involving a violation of the Endangered Species Act. The European Union faced a comparable trade-off: after moving away from Russian gas, Europe became dependent on Chinese equipment accounting for at least 80% of global solar-panel and lithium-battery production. Brussels consequently restricted Chinese hybrid-vehicle imports and prohibited Chinese inverters in EU-funded projects over concerns about the remote shutdown of power networks. China, however, did not hold an unconditional advantage in electricity costs. Regional planning imbalances, including the addition of 78 gigawatts of excess coal capacity in 2025, left industrial power tariffs in Guangdong and Jiangsu at 0.6–0.75 yuan per kilowatt-hour—roughly comparable to about 10 U.S. cents in Virginia. Cheaper electricity in Inner Mongolia, at 0.32–0.36 yuan per kilowatt-hour, was unsuitable for applications with stringent latency requirements.

Counter-argument: Energy and raw-material supply constraints do not affect every data center or AI application equally. The report's own contrast between coastal China and Inner Mongolia shows that network latency, geography, and local power prices—not just aggregate national generating capacity—determine practical competitiveness.

Public opposition compounded those constraints in Western markets. In U.S. local votes held alongside November elections, residents rejected data-center projects in 40 of 41 cases, prompting Senate discussion of the broad BAAJA bill. The proposal would require data centers to pay for grid expansion in return for faster transmission construction—the United States was building only 500 miles of major lines annually against a reported need for 5,000 miles—and substantial streamlining of environmental reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA). In Australia, protests derailed data-center projects by Goodman Group, Stockland, AirTrunk, and NextDC; NextDC abandoned a Melbourne site because of planned 20-meter transmission towers. Musicians and writers led by Elton John, Peter Garrett, and Charlotte Wood also blocked a government proposal to allow paid licensing of copyrighted content for AI training. Public anxiety increased after five consecutive days of technical failures in the United States affecting Microsoft email, nationwide flight operations, JPMorgan Chase, a classified National Security Agency network, and Apple Pay. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and the FBI rejected the cyberattack explanation, and Trump described the events as routine inconveniences. Senators Josh Hawley and Rick Scott, unnamed FEMA staff, and consumers who bought out 21 of 26 generator models on Costco's website remained unconvinced by official explanations. Separately, a 30-month study by universities in Hong Kong and Stockholm involving more than 26,000 Chinese schoolchildren found that AI use raised homework scores by 18% but reduced final-examination results by 18%–24%.

Counter-argument: The rejection of individual data-center proposals does not necessarily indicate opposition to all AI infrastructure, just as simultaneous technical outages do not establish a common cyber cause. The education study also points to differing effects on assisted assignments and independently assessed learning rather than a single measure of overall performance.

LAW AND INSTITUTIONS

INTERNATIONAL COURTS • POLITICAL OVERSIGHT
05

LEGAL DISPUTES AND INSTITUTIONAL PRESSURES

A landmark ruling by the International Court of Justice (ICJ) in The Hague, described in the source as the first finding that an entire state—Myanmar—was responsible for genocide against the Rohingya in 2017, became a point of conflict between different legal interpretations. The source cites about 10,000 deaths among a population of 815,000 and nearly one million refugees in Bangladesh. The judgment was reportedly adopted by nine votes to six under the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, adopted in 1948 and effective from 1951, a distinction that helps explain differing dates in news accounts. Asian and Western commentators viewed the ruling principally as a belated judgment on Min Aung Hlaing's regime, while noting that meaningful enforcement would depend on the United Nations Security Council.

Israeli observers examined the Myanmar decision in the context of South Africa's case concerning Gaza. The deadlines for written submissions had reportedly been extended to November 2027 and May 2029, pushing hearings toward the end of 2029. Israeli sources suggested Pretoria might be waiting for a change of U.S. administration. The Jerusalem Post military analyst Yonah Jeremy Bob and the newspaper's editorial staff argued that the court had lowered the evidentiary threshold: they characterized the deaths of 1.2% of an ethnic group, together with widespread destruction of housing, political rhetoric, actions by nonstate proxies, and United Nations agency reports given greater weight than government objections, as sufficient evidence for a finding of intent. The potential defense argument identified by Israeli commentators was the distinction between even serious negligence and genocide, including in relation to Israeli military acknowledgments of negligence in the World Central Kitchen aid convoy case and within approximately 150 other investigations.

Counter-argument: The legal reasoning cannot be reduced to a casualty percentage alone, even on the source's description, because the reported judgment considered multiple categories of evidence. The stated distinction between negligence and genocidal intent also shows why allegations in the Gaza proceedings would need to be assessed on their specific evidence rather than inferred directly from the Myanmar decision.

These legal disputes unfolded alongside fragile diplomatic and security arrangements across the Middle East. On Thursday, Israeli tanks completed their first raid into central Gaza since the October 2025 ceasefire to destroy a Hamas tunnel network under construction, without public objections from the United States; U.S. Central Command head Admiral Brad Cooper visited Israel Defense Forces headquarters the same day. On the Lebanese border, according to The Washington Post, France was advancing a plan to replace 7,500 United Nations Interim Force in Lebanon (UNIFIL) peacekeepers, whose mandate would expire at the end of the year, with a European contingent of 2,000–5,000 troops. Beirut and even Hezbollah reportedly supported the idea, while Israel opposed it. The source reports that the fighting since March had killed 4,300 people on Lebanese territory and at least 40 Israeli service members. At a Brussels forum, Syria's new leadership, previously associated with Hayat Tahrir al-Sham, presented itself to Europe as a partner against ISIS and captagon trafficking. It reported seizing 700 million pills and facilitating the return of three million refugees, while seeking $195 million in European aid and EU diplomatic pressure on Israel over raids in southern Syria. In neighboring Iraq, after U.S. troops withdrew on September 30, Iraqi aircraft were independently striking ISIS cells in Anbar.

The contrast between allied declarations and Israel's diplomatic friction became particularly visible in Sydney on November 19 at the inaugural Australian summit organized by The Jerusalem Post. The Israeli publication portrayed a forum attended by former National Security Agency director Admiral Michael Rogers and NAB chairman Philip Chronican as a demonstration of technological partnership and solidarity. Australian coverage instead emphasized an overt diplomatic dispute. A day before the conference, Foreign Minister Penny Wong imposed sanctions on three individuals, including Daniella Weiss, and four organizations, including the Nahala movement and, for the first time, a West Bank construction company. The penalties included fines of up to A$910,000 for individuals and A$3.6 million for companies. At the event, Israeli Ambassador Hillel Newman characterized the action as “megaphone diplomacy” against the transitional government and threatened reciprocal sanctions against “antisemitic elements” in Australia. Israeli President Isaac Herzog's wife, Michal Herzog, criticized boycott policies. The same day, philanthropist Steven Lowy reported a 97% increase in threats against his family, more than 15,000 incidents over a year, following testimony to a royal commission on antisemitism due to report on December 18. University of Sydney chancellor David Thodey apologized for allowing a slate using the slogan “globalize the intifada” to contest student elections. The Australian also published details of an Israeli military document attributed to Hamas that listed ABC freelance journalist Roshdi Sarraj, who died in 2023, under military number 852492 as a 12-year member and deputy unit commander. ABC and the Committee to Protect Journalists disputed the allegation, saying they had not been provided with the original supporting evidence.

Counter-argument: The Sydney summit demonstrates that diplomatic confrontation and continued institutional contact can occur simultaneously. The disputed document concerning the deceased journalist also illustrates the limits of drawing conclusions from contested evidence without access to the original records.

Domestic institutional problems contributed to political uncertainty in Israel and the United States. Israel's October election produced no majority in the 120-seat Knesset. Netanyahu's bloc won 53 seats: Likud 27, Otzma Yehudit seven, United Torah Judaism seven, Shas six, and Religious Zionism–Zehut six. Gadi Eisenkot's “change bloc” won 48: Yashar 21, Naftali Bennett's Beyahad 12, Yair Golan's Democrats eight, and Avigdor Lieberman's Yisrael Beiteinu seven. Two Arab parties won a record 19 seats, with the Joint List taking 11 and Ra'am eight. The opposition elected Matan Kahana speaker with support from Arab lawmakers and nominated retired judge Yosef Elron as state comptroller after the Supreme Court voided an earlier vote for Netanyahu's lawyer Michael Rabello because ballots had been photographed under compulsion. Yet Eisenkot's potential coalition remained divided. Bennett held a secret two-hour meeting with Netanyahu; Lieberman rejected an alliance with Arab parties and opened private talks with the Ashkenazi ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism party over preserving military-service deferments. This occurred despite a reported shortage of 12,000 combat troops in the IDF and economists' warnings about fiscal pressure in a country where nearly one-third of first-graders attended ultra-Orthodox schools without core secular subjects. Even local press assessments diverged: in The Jerusalem Post, Herb Keinon and Liat Collins correctly counted 13 ultra-Orthodox seats, while columnist Dan Perry wrote that there were 14 lawmakers.

Divisions within Israel's security leadership appeared more serious. The Washington Post and The Jerusalem Post reported that before the October 27 elections, Netanyahu had sought to arrange an operation against Iran through Mossad chief Roman Gofman, bypassing IDF Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir, who had not been invited to a meeting with Pete Hegseth at Ovda Air Base. The accounts differed on whether Gofman actually supported the plan despite objections from Zamir and Trump. Commentator Yaakov Katz also strongly criticized Shin Bet director David Zini over pre-election arrests and warrantless searches involving Arab Ra'am activists, reportedly based on an unsubstantiated tip from a former prime minister's office employee transmitted through Netanyahu's chief of staff, Ido Norden.

Counter-argument: The fragmented Knesset does not preclude a new coalition, as the election of a speaker and appointment process for state comptroller demonstrate. Conflicting accounts of the proposed Iran operation and the unsubstantiated allegations concerning Ra'am activists also prevent definitive conclusions about the intentions of individual security officials.

In Washington, a related institutional struggle was unfolding ahead of a transition to Democratic control of Congress, with Hakeem Jeffries already nominated for House speaker. Alongside investigations concerning World Liberty Financial's cryptocurrency business, the reported funding of Donald Trump Jr.'s wedding by Russian oligarch Umar Kremlev, and the withholding of Epstein-related files, congressional Democrats renewed scrutiny of arrangements involving 10 major law firms that had promised almost $1 billion in free legal services to the administration under the threat of presidential sanctions. Kirkland & Ellis alone reportedly provided $11.8 million in work. An alimony-related court dispute also exposed alleged informal lobbying channels in the White House. Longtime Trump adviser Jason Miller, who appeared in the Oval Office as an “unpaid employee” without filing required ethics and conflict-of-interest declarations, reportedly increased his income from $879,000 to $15 million through two private firms in the year ending September 2026. Bank records cited in the account showed payments from the Indian government ($1.65 million, accompanied by meetings with Bessent and Lutnick), from Romania, and from Paramount Skydance ($420,000 in March and June, with the final payment made nine days before the Justice Department approved its $111 billion merger with Warner Bros. Discovery). A court summary prepared by the plaintiff from transfers without named senders also listed an offshore entity in Mauritius ($3.35 million) and the Brooklyn Nets, owned by Joseph Tsai, cofounder of Alibaba, a corporation reportedly placed on a Pentagon blacklist (about $1.5 million).

Counter-argument: Financial transfers, meetings, and regulatory decisions occurring close together are grounds for examining disclosure and conflicts of interest, but timing alone does not demonstrate that official actions were purchased. The source itself notes that several payments appeared in a court summary of transfers that did not identify their senders.

Taken together, the geopolitical statements and the military, fiscal, and corporate figures in this report suggest a widening gap in late 2026 between the international projection of power and the practical capacity to exercise it. In Shenzhen, the largest states presented themselves as capable of reshaping global arrangements, while their options remained constrained by aging cruise missiles, limited grid capacity, borrowing costs near 5%, and vulnerable maritime chokepoints.

Counter-argument: Material constraints narrow strategic options but do not make states incapable of acting. The report describes continuing diplomatic initiatives, defense appropriation proposals, energy agreements, and institutional oversight processes that could alter some of those limits.

The report argues that the near-term direction of world politics and financial markets may depend less on the December G20 summit in Miami than on four concrete decisions and risks: whether the U.S. Congress can overcome debt-related opposition and replenish defense stockpiles before the possible December 12 government shutdown; whether markets can absorb roughly $900 billion in AI infrastructure commitments amid expensive credit and Chinese price competition; whether a winter escalation of attacks on energy infrastructure in Eastern Europe and the Persian Gulf can be avoided; and whether institutions in Washington and Jerusalem can restore effective internal checks before disputes over legitimacy reduce their ability to make strategic decisions.

Counter-argument: The four decision points are scenarios and dependencies, not inevitable outcomes. The report identifies several possible responses—including appropriations, negotiations, and renewed oversight—that could change the trajectory before those risks materialize.
