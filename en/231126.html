THE AXIS OF AUTOCRATIC COOPERATION

The principal geopolitical argument of The Economist’s November 21, 2026 issue is that the diplomatic theater surrounding the Shenzhen meeting of Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin should not be confused with a settlement of the global balance of power. The publication contrasts the summit with the 1945 Yalta meeting. The original conference was conducted by three wartime leaders deciding the future of Europe; the Shenzhen encounter, by contrast, offered a high-profile display of engagement without evidence that its participants had reconciled their competing objectives. The president of the United States was seeking a display of authority after his party’s midterm reversals, the Russian president an opportunity to reinforce his status alongside larger powers, and China an occasion to demonstrate diplomatic centrality.

The Economist argues that a more consequential development is the practical convergence of four governments: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. It emphasizes that these states do not constitute a conventional alliance. They differ sharply in economic weight, governing ideology, institutional structure and relationships with world trade. China is a major manufacturing and technological economy; Russia relies more heavily on military and resource capabilities; Iran and North Korea face severe constraints. Their common interest, in the publication’s assessment, lies in protecting their governing systems while complicating Western strategic planning. The pattern predates the current summit: American defense officials had identified the danger of cooperation among these states as early as 2018.

Military assistance is the clearest form of cooperation described in the reporting. Russian attacks in Ukraine use Iranian drone technology and Chinese-made components; Iranian engineers have studied Russian experience adapting unmanned systems under battlefield conditions. The Economist estimates that North Korea has dispatched about 16,000 soldiers to support Russia in exchange for money and military technology. China is portrayed as avoiding direct military participation while collecting operational information about drone warfare. The publication also reports that Iranian missiles benefit from Chinese chemical precursors used in solid fuel, Russian intelligence and Chinese satellite information. These are different types of support, but each may reduce the recipient’s dependence on Western-controlled supplies.

Economic links reinforce the military relationship. The magazine says that more than 95% of Russia’s trade with China is now settled in yuan or rubles, compared with roughly 25% five years earlier. It describes Russian oil flowing to China and North Korea, Iranian crude reaching China through sanctions-evading shipping networks, and North Korean workers helping address labor shortages in Russian factories. The publication estimates that the so-called shadow fleet moving sanctioned oil has expanded by 1,500% since 2022. Chinese payment systems give the other governments alternatives to Western financial channels, while Russian and Chinese veto power at the United Nations can obstruct initiatives aimed at their partners.

These connections could make geographically separate crises harder for Western governments to manage. The Economist considers the possibility that a confrontation involving Taiwan might coincide with disruption initiated by Russia, Iran or North Korea elsewhere, including in cyberspace. Simultaneous pressures would force the United States and its allies to allocate forces, weapons, intelligence capabilities and political attention across multiple theaters. The article also identifies an indirect benefit for Beijing when American aircraft carriers are committed to the Persian Gulf rather than the Pacific, or when Western resources are devoted to defending Ukraine instead of reinforcing Taiwan.

Counter-argument: The magazine itself distinguishes these states from a unified military command. China has benefited from globalization, depends on European customers, and is hurt by the higher oil prices associated with an Iran-related crisis. It has reasons to avoid a wider war in Europe or uncontrolled instability near the Korean Peninsula. Their incentives can align against American influence without becoming identical.

The week’s news illustrates the distance between diplomatic engagements and security developments. At the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation meeting in Shenzhen, the leaders of the United States, China and Russia held trilateral talks after bilateral discussions. Before arriving, Trump attended meetings in Manila, including talks with Australia, India and Japan, the partners in the Quadrilateral Security Dialogue. Meanwhile, Japan protested an alleged intrusion by two Russian military aircraft near Okinoshima, and Taiwan reported sightings of Russian aircraft near its east coast. Ukraine struck Russian infrastructure and partially damaged the Kerch bridge; American representatives described movement in peace negotiations even as Moscow disputed European participation in the talks.

The magazine’s defense reporting treats the maritime dimension as particularly important. Its editorial on the Royal Navy notes a 40% increase in Russian naval activity around British waters over the previous year. Britain must sustain its submarine-based nuclear deterrent while protecting communications, undersea infrastructure and North Atlantic routes. The submarine mission consumes roughly a quarter of the British defense budget, leaving limited resources for conventional fleet expansion. The Royal Navy is therefore experimenting with a hybrid force of crewed, uncrewed and autonomous vessels, with some functions coordinated through artificial intelligence.

Under that concept, expensive multipurpose warships would be supplemented by smaller platforms that perform specialized tasks. Proposed Common Combat Vessels are expected to cost about £200 million ($260 million) each, compared with approximately five times as much for a high-end frigate and more than £2.5 billion for an air-defense destroyer. A larger number of cheaper hulls could improve geographic coverage, disperse risks and open procurement to commercial shipbuilders. Britain’s naval planners see a potential parallel with the way NASA’s use of commercial suppliers helped transform the American space sector.

Counter-argument: A hybrid fleet has not been tested at the scale envisaged. Smaller autonomous craft may have less endurance and less armament than conventional warships, and North Atlantic defense still depends on effective coordination with the United States, France and Norway. Operating rules, command responsibilities and responses to attacks on uncrewed vessels remain difficult questions. Lower unit costs therefore do not automatically translate into equivalent strategic capability.

The Economist’s broader warning is not that another twentieth-century military bloc has already been formed, but that overlapping trade, technological, intelligence and defense relationships are changing the choices available to each government. Some forms of cooperation can be inexpensive and deniable while still imposing significant planning costs on opponents. Its 2027 analysis adds a further uncertainty: artificial intelligence is changing the speed at which military and cyber capabilities develop, potentially leaving national-security institutions less time to assess and respond to simultaneous threats.

Counter-argument: The same source stresses that open political systems retain advantages in innovation and alliance-building, and that Beijing has reasons to preserve a functioning international trading order. Whether strategic cooperation becomes a durable axis will depend on these constraints as well as on the partners’ shared antagonism toward Washington.

CHIP CONTROLS, ESPIONAGE, AND DIGITAL FRAGILITY

The Economist’s semiconductor investigation describes a gap between the formal restrictions that Washington places on advanced processors and the practical routes by which Chinese developers continue to acquire computing power. The United States has restricted exports of increasingly capable chips since 2020, tightened rules in 2022 and 2023, and added high-bandwidth memory to its controls in 2024. Yet the publication reports that chip shipments through intermediaries, remote access to data centers outside China, and efforts to recruit skilled engineers have limited the effectiveness of those measures.

The immediate controversy concerns Nvidia, the American chip designer, and Megaspeed, a Singapore-based cloud company. The magazine reports that Megaspeed became a major buyer of Nvidia processors in Southeast Asia and that some equipment appears to have been directed to China or used to supply computing services to Chinese customers. The story arose amid the resignation of Nvidia’s chief executive Jensen Huang and allegations concerning his relationship with a Megaspeed executive. These accusations and the associated claims of export violations require distinction from established findings; the report describes investigations, denials and disagreements among American agencies rather than a completed adjudication.

The scale of possible circumvention is material. SemiAnalysis estimates that computing equipment equivalent to approximately 1.5 gigawatts, or around one million restricted Nvidia chips, has reached China through illicit channels. The Institute for Progress estimates that smuggled chips provide around 22% of China’s AI data-center capacity. The same institute puts remotely accessed computing capacity at approximately one-quarter of the total. Those are estimates from different methods and categories, not figures that can automatically be added together as a measure of unique chips.

A large share of the remote capacity is located in Southeast Asia. Following Singapore’s restrictions on data-center construction because of land and power constraints, projects expanded in Malaysia. According to the investigation, Malaysian data-center capacity has grown approximately one hundredfold in five years, and the country has 55 large facilities with another 62 in development. Taiwan’s high-end semiconductor exports, after excluding shipments to the United States and Mexico, are heavily concentrated in Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. American cloud companies, including Oracle, operate infrastructure in the region that serves Chinese technology customers, raising a policy question about the distinction between ownership of restricted equipment and remote use of it.

High-bandwidth memory creates another compliance challenge. Before restrictions on sales to China were introduced in late 2024, Huawei is estimated to have stockpiled about 13 million HBM stacks, enough for roughly 1.6 million advanced AI processors. The Economist describes an alleged technique in which HBM is assembled with older chips whose combined technical specifications do not cross export thresholds, then separated after arrival. Historical investigations also include claims of equipment being shipped through shell companies and repackaged to disguise its destination. The reported co-founder of Supermicro accused in one such case denies involvement.

China’s domestic industrial effort is the second part of the story. Semiconductor production depends not only on the acquisition of equipment but also on tacit knowledge held by experienced engineers. The magazine cites cases involving former employees of Samsung and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), while carefully noting that particular allegations are disputed or subject to criminal proceedings. In one South Korean case a former Samsung specialist was sentenced to seven years in prison after prosecutors said proprietary DRAM manufacturing knowledge had been transferred to a Chinese rival. Taiwan prosecutors separately allege that a former TSMC manager copied 21 documents; the files were reportedly recovered before any technology was transferred.

Progress on equipment is uneven. Chinese suppliers provided less than 10% of purchases of chipmaking tools in China in 2021, but the research firm Bernstein estimates that their share will exceed one-quarter in 2026, representing approximately $16 billion in equipment. Chinese-owned fabrication plants had acquired around 340 older deep-ultraviolet lithography systems from ASML by early 2026, with more than 180 purchased during 2024 and 2025. These machines can be used to manufacture chips around the seven-nanometer class or somewhat smaller, although the costs and defect rates are less favorable than with the most advanced alternatives. Analysts cited by the magazine expect China to require about 10–15 years to master the hardest aspects of extreme-ultraviolet technology.

Counter-argument: The investigation does not establish that American restrictions have become irrelevant. The magazine notes that China still struggles to manufacture certain advanced memory and lithography systems at scale, that Malaysia has tightened permitting, and that domestic substitutes may incur higher costs and lower yields. Slowing technological development remains possible even if complete exclusion is not.

Disagreements inside the American government have complicated enforcement. The Economist reports a dispute between Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick over how aggressively to close loopholes, particularly those involving remote computing and Southeast Asian intermediaries. The Commerce Department introduced further restrictions covering advanced chip shipments to Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore and Thailand. Critics of tighter controls argue that restricting access too aggressively can stimulate an accelerated Chinese substitution effort, while enforcement advocates regard continued access as directly undermining national-security policy.

Cybersecurity offers a parallel example of an intervention intended to reduce one risk creating another. The publication describes major American service disruptions in November: Microsoft suffered a global outage on November 12; air-traffic control communications failed the following day; large banks experienced problems on November 16; and the National Security Agency reported disruption to a classified communications system on November 17. Officials offered differing explanations, but the magazine found little evidence connecting the outages to a coordinated cyberattack or autonomous AI systems acting independently.

In several cases, mundane software and network failures were sufficient explanations. The Federal Aviation Administration attributed the aviation disruption to a newly installed router whose data traffic overwhelmed another site, during upgrades from copper to fiber connections. Microsoft said an older software patch interacted with a temporary capacity shortage and caused more than 80% of servers to fail. Sources connected to JPMorgan Chase and the NSA linked their own incidents to software changes. The Economist also cites the possibility that fast-moving AI-assisted security audits are pushing organizations to deploy fixes faster than older infrastructure and experienced staff can safely manage.

Microsoft reportedly repaired around 4,000 documented software vulnerabilities in total between 2022 and 2025, compared with more than 5,000 during 2026 alone. Anthropic’s Mythos model, provided under controlled access in its Glasswing program, is described as especially capable of discovering and exploiting vulnerabilities. Experts quoted in the publication warn that faster detection can overwhelm patch-management teams, and that workforce reductions may have weakened institutional knowledge of critical systems.

Counter-argument: The same analysis suggests that a surge in defensive patching may improve long-term security as vulnerabilities are removed. Moreover, no clear causal link has been established between AI-driven security work and every reported outage. Some failures are attributable to routine hardware upgrades or delayed interactions among existing systems.

Longer-term cyber risks remain substantial. The publication reports that attackers associated with North Korea stole approximately $6.3 billion from Binance in October, while more advanced offensive models are becoming broadly available. The strategic problem is therefore not only how much processing power rival countries possess, but how rapidly organizations can secure the networks, software dependencies and supply chains on which that processing power depends.

POLITICAL REALIGNMENT AND INSTITUTIONAL TRUST

The Economist treats the November 2026 American midterm elections as a major disruption to the political assumptions that governed Donald Trump’s second term. Its special annual outlook, The World Ahead 2027, describes a large electoral repudiation of the administration following dissatisfaction with the Iran war, fuel prices and allegations of corruption. The resulting balance of power increases the importance of congressional scrutiny and gives the Democratic Party opportunities in states previously regarded as difficult territory.

In its reporting from Denver, the publication draws a comparison with Barack Obama’s 2008 political coalition. A new group of Democrats who began their careers as organizers and volunteers in that period is now contesting important offices. The magazine names James Talarico in Texas and Rob Sand in Iowa among its examples and identifies Pete Buttigieg as another figure shaped by that generation. It argues that their rhetoric often combines an appeal to move beyond entrenched partisanship with a more explicitly populist focus on prices, wages and the distribution of economic opportunity.

The 2027 outlook places these changes in the context of the approaching post-Trump succession. Vice President J.D. Vance is described as an early favorite for the Republican nomination, but he faces the difficult task of representing an administration while distinguishing himself from its unpopular decisions. The publication notes his efforts to distance himself from the Iran war and describes the possibility of challenges from other Republican figures. Democrats, meanwhile, must decide whether their midterm gains can be translated into a durable national coalition rather than a temporary protest vote.

Counter-argument: Midterm results and early succession polling do not determine a presidential election. The Economist acknowledges that Trumpism has organized Republican politics for more than a decade; candidates on both sides still face uncertain coalitions, electoral geography and disputes over how to govern. A weaker presidency does not necessarily produce a coherent alternative policy agenda.

The first immediate institutional issue is oversight. A Democratic-controlled Congress could impose new conditions on technology and defense procurement and examine administrative decision-making more closely. In the magazine’s analysis, the semiconductor dispute involving Nvidia and Megaspeed has already produced friction between the Treasury and Commerce departments. Political competition may alter the timing of export-control legislation, the extent of scrutiny of corporate access to the White House and the practical meaning of national-security exceptions.

Domestic institutions face a separate test in public health. The Economist reports that an arm of the World Health Organization declared on November 17 that endemic measles transmission had been re-established in the United States for the first time since 1999. Cases had been recorded in 45 states in 2026, with 11 reported measles-related deaths, compared with three over the entire period from 2000 through 2025. The publication attributes the resurgence in part to falling vaccination rates, disrupted routine care and the spread of vaccine misinformation. It also criticizes policies and statements by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. that could further undermine confidence in immunization.

The epidemiological stakes extend beyond case counts. The article notes that measles can produce serious complications among very young children and may weaken immunity to unrelated infections. Vaccination campaigns had previously brought sustained endemic transmission under control. Its return illustrates how a public institution can lose a hard-won protective capacity even when a proven preventive technology exists.

Counter-argument: An increase in reported transmission does not mean the entire public-health infrastructure has ceased functioning. The same source records the historical effectiveness of vaccine campaigns and the continuing availability of immunization. Restoring participation and confidence remains a possible response, though the publication judges it difficult.

The publication’s commentary on legal norms adds another dimension to institutional trust. A column about Secretary of State Marco Rubio questions the administration’s increasingly dismissive rhetoric about international courts, multilateral commitments and the prohibition on territorial conquest. It contrasts the traditional American defense of a rules-based order with statements presenting power and sovereignty as sufficient grounds for unilateral action. The magazine also describes rival ambitions within the administration, including tensions between Rubio and Vance over Iran policy and succession positioning.

Counter-argument: The Economist notes that American governments have long interpreted international law through the lens of national interests and have not accepted International Criminal Court jurisdiction over American citizens. The issue is therefore not whether the United States ever resisted supranational authority, but whether the current administration’s rhetoric and conduct narrow its capacity to negotiate credible commitments.

Beyond the United States, the 2027 forecast anticipates a period of political inertia within the European Union. Elections in France, Italy and Poland, alongside the consequences of Spanish voting, are expected to make leaders more reluctant to accept difficult compromises. Two policy questions stand out: financing Ukraine after a €90 billion package agreed in 2025 proves insufficient for the relevant period, and negotiations over the European Union’s 2028–2034 budget. The magazine also anticipates pressure from right-wing populist movements and debate about the treatment of frozen Russian assets.

Counter-argument: The EU’s elections are a source of possible delay, not evidence that decisions are impossible. The publication frames the outcomes as uncertain and dependent on leaders’ domestic mandates. Electoral pressure could also produce greater clarity about which arrangements have public backing.

The common theme across American and European institutions is the tension between political accountability and administrative capacity. Elections can check executive power; they can also generate uncertainty in security spending, health policy, fiscal reform and technology regulation. The Economist’s material suggests that 2027 will test not only who holds office but how quickly governments can rebuild confidence in rules and processes after prolonged political confrontation.

FISCAL PRESSURES AND ECONOMIC VULNERABILITIES

The Economist’s late-November reporting presents financial stress as a problem shared by very different economies. In rich countries it concerns sovereign borrowing, budget credibility and the prospect of higher interest costs. In emerging economies it includes fragile banks, narrow tax bases, exposure to energy shocks and pressure on household income. The causes are not interchangeable, but the underlying constraint is similar: institutions must finance existing obligations while economic growth and public confidence remain uncertain.

Its forecast for 2027 reports International Monetary Fund estimates of fiscal deficits amounting to 8.5% of GDP in the United States, 5.4% in France, 3.8% in Britain and 4.3% in Germany for 2026. The Economist’s own fiscal calculations suggest that stabilizing public debt would require an adjustment exceeding 5% of GDP in America and 4% in France, with adjustments of roughly 2.5–3.5% in Britain, Germany and Canada. Such figures are analytical estimates sensitive to assumed growth, bond yields and government revenue; they should not be read as enacted policy measures.

France receives particular attention because electoral uncertainty has coincided with a widening spread between French and German borrowing costs. The magazine anticipates that campaigning for the 2027 French presidential election will make deficit reduction politically difficult. In the United States, the 2028 campaign could encourage rival candidates to promise tax cuts or spending without explaining their financing. The outlook cites the possibility that Social Security financing problems will intensify toward the end of the next presidential term.

The publication also discusses an earlier American Treasury effort to buy long-term government bonds while issuing more short-term debt. It argues that this approach may temporarily change market demand for maturities without resolving the underlying fiscal imbalance. Policymakers may be tempted to seek central-bank support if yields rise further; investors will be watching whether a combination of falling exchange rates and rising borrowing costs signals reduced confidence in government debt.

Counter-argument: The outlook acknowledges that sovereign bonds can still rally during market stress. A reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and lower oil prices could relieve inflation and interest-rate pressure, giving governments additional time. The timing and scale of any debt-market repricing are therefore uncertain, and the headline deficit does not by itself determine a funding crisis.

Bangladesh illustrates how a different balance-sheet problem can spread through an otherwise successful development economy. The magazine reports that nonperforming loans represent approximately one-third of the country’s bank loan book, compared with 1.7% in India and 5% in Pakistan. The banking sector’s capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratio is described as negative 3.8%, and depositors at several institutions have struggled to withdraw funds. These weaknesses follow years in which political connections influenced bank management and lending, according to officials and researchers interviewed by The Economist.

A 2025 change in loan-classification rules made the size of the problem more visible. Bangladesh had previously allowed borrowers to make small initial repayments and reschedule debt in ways that kept many troubled loans out of the official nonperforming category. The government adopted a 90-day overdue threshold, closer to international practice; the reported nonperforming share subsequently reached about 33%. This means a rise in the published ratio reflects both actual financial stress and the recognition of losses previously obscured by regulatory treatment.

The economic consequences are measurable. Private credit growth has fallen from around 10% in 2025 to approximately 4.5%, the lowest rate in 33 years. The Economist cites a growth forecast of 3.7% for 2026, compared with approximately 6.5% before the 2024 political upheaval. The central bank has provided about $6.2 billion, equivalent to 1.2% of GDP, in emergency assistance. Moody’s estimates that adequate recapitalization might require resources amounting to 10% of GDP. Bangladesh collects about 7% of GDP in tax revenue and runs a deficit around 3.3% of GDP, limiting its ability to fund a rescue independently.

The strain extends to the garment industry. Energy interruptions linked to the war in the Gulf closed factories, while higher living costs contributed to wage demands and strikes. The publication says inflation has remained above 8% since mid-2022 and that poverty may rise for a third consecutive year. Bangladesh has asked for and received a three-year postponement of its graduation from the United Nations’ least-developed-country category, an acknowledgment that it needs time to strengthen institutions and prepare for changes to trade preferences.

Counter-argument: The Economist stresses that Bangladesh’s problems are not equivalent to an imminent balance-of-payments collapse. Remittances and garment exports continue to generate foreign earnings, and the recognition of bad loans is a necessary step toward repair. The country has also recorded large gains in education, health and income since independence. Its banking crisis threatens that progress but does not erase it.

In China, social insurance and local government debt expose a separate set of weaknesses. The Economist reports that more than 320 million people were aged 60 or older at the end of 2025, or about 23% of the population, compared with 16% a decade earlier. The working-age population contracted by approximately 60 million over that period. Mandatory contributions can be substantial: formally employed workers pay at least 10% of wages and employers approximately 25% of payroll. Beijing is trying to improve compliance partly to strengthen a safety net that could support consumer spending, but higher deductions can reduce current disposable income and provoke opposition.

The property downturn has weakened the revenue model of Chinese local authorities. The magazine recounts the November 9 protest of a man who drove an excavator into the facade of a government service building in Fengdong, near Xi’an. His motive was not established; residents suggested unpaid construction work or compensation disputes. The surrounding development includes unfinished towers and vacant buildings. A state-owned development group associated with the area had reported nearly 3.7 billion yuan, around $550 million, in overdue debt. According to S&P, more than two-thirds of local-government financing vehicles in the first half of 2026 did not earn enough to cover interest payments.

Counter-argument: The report does not present a single protest as proof of a broad uprising. It also notes attempts to develop new technology-related businesses and a government effort to impose longer-term accountability for official borrowing. These may change incentives, although neither is likely to reproduce the earlier windfall from land sales quickly.

Elsewhere in Asia, demographic pressure is emerging through Thailand’s exceptionally low fertility and India’s agricultural emissions. The Economist notes that India harvested approximately 154 million tonnes of rice in the previous year, about 28% of the global total, and exported approximately 22 million tonnes, or 40% of world rice exports. Flooded rice paddies also produce methane, which makes food-security policy inseparable from climate adaptation. Latin American economies are competing to host AI data centers with relatively low construction costs and renewable energy, yet must address electricity transmission, local demand and regulatory delays. Brazil’s electricity supply is described as about 85% renewable and Chile’s as 68%; neither share alone guarantees reliable power at data-center sites.

Counter-argument: Emerging economies have different sources of resilience. Food exports, hydropower, skilled labor, remittances and favorable energy costs can support growth. The common analytical lesson is that these advantages depend on functioning banks, grid connections and public institutions rather than on resource endowments alone.

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE • CAPITAL MARKETS • 2027
05

THE 2027 OUTLOOK: AI CAPABILITY AND FINANCIAL RISK

The World Ahead 2027, published by The Economist alongside its November 21 issue, identifies two forces likely to interact throughout the coming year: the weakening of Donald Trump’s political dominance after the midterms and the accelerating capabilities of artificial intelligence. In the publication’s editorial forecast, new models have begun to solve mathematical and scientific problems previously considered out of reach, while the political reaction to AI is shifting from curiosity toward scrutiny over safety, jobs, electricity consumption and control of infrastructure.

The magazine cites an announcement that an unreleased OpenAI model had solved more than 370 previously unsolved mathematics problems. It argues that successes in mathematics and code are early indications of changes that could extend to materials science, medical research, robotics and automated laboratory work. Its predictions include a wider use of personal AI agents, additional cities adopting driverless transport, the first practical deployments of more capable humanoid robots and organizational change as companies incorporate autonomous software into more business functions.

Counter-argument: The forecasts are not observations of completed economic transformation. The editorial explicitly rejects the idea of an immediate disappearance of all office work, even while anticipating substantial disruption. Translating benchmark achievements into reliable, affordable production systems remains a separate challenge, especially where physical infrastructure and human oversight matter.

Artificial intelligence is also affecting the balance of international power. The Economist describes a technology that changes the time available for decisions, the geography of strategic dependencies and the distribution of military capabilities. It notes that frontier laboratories can release more than ten new models a month, compared with the decades that historical international arms-control arrangements required to negotiate. Governments operate through slower processes of consultation, legislation and appeals, making rapid technical development difficult to supervise.

The underlying supply chain is geographically dispersed even as the most prominent models are concentrated in the United States and China. American firms design advanced chips; Japanese companies supply important chemicals; Dutch firms provide lithography systems; South Korean manufacturers produce memory; and Taiwanese factories make advanced processors. Countries seeking AI capabilities may have to choose among related hardware, software and security standards. The magazine describes Washington’s Pax Silica initiative and China’s Digital Silk Road as competing efforts to shape those dependencies.

Counter-argument: A global supply chain also creates mutual constraints on attempts to divide technology into rigid blocs. Even China, despite major investment in domestic capacity, depends on specialized foreign inputs. Countries may attempt to diversify suppliers rather than commit exclusively to one national stack.

The financial scale of the industry creates a second uncertainty. The Economist reports that Anthropic’s November 2026 public offering and associated private financing raised about $107 billion, with the company valued at roughly $1.5 trillion before trading. Its model Claude Code helped it win business in programming and enterprise automation. The magazine cites Goldman Sachs projections of approximately $53 billion in Anthropic revenue during 2026, compared with an OpenAI forecast of $35 billion, while cautioning that the companies account for sales through cloud partners differently and are not fully comparable.

Forward projections underscore how much growth investors are assuming. Goldman Sachs is cited as anticipating around $636 billion in annual Anthropic revenue by 2030; William Blair projects roughly $414 billion; and OpenAI has reportedly targeted about $446 billion for the same year. These estimates vary widely but are all much larger than current sales. The Economist notes that OpenAI’s Codex product has grown and that both companies are attempting to move beyond coding tools to general-purpose knowledge-work agents. Its account also describes intense competition in enterprise pricing.

The special report on AI forecasts heavier spending on model training, post-training and infrastructure. It cites Anthropic’s October prospectus as anticipating approximately $750 billion in computing expenditure in coming years. A possible cycle of AI-driven software improvement could raise both technical capability and the consumption of expensive computing power. At the same time, Chinese laboratories are putting downward pressure on model prices. The magazine highlights DeepSeek’s free model-running software and Stanford research suggesting that smaller models can perform as well as cloud systems on more than 80% of evaluated tasks.

Counter-argument: A strong growth trajectory is not sufficient proof that the most optimistic revenue projections are achievable. Lower model prices, locally run AI and rising infrastructure costs could benefit customers while compressing provider margins. Equally, cheaper access and expanding capabilities could enlarge the market. The publication treats the distribution of gains between users, providers and hardware suppliers as unresolved.

Political resistance is an increasingly important constraint. The Economist reports that 44 of 45 state and local ballots seeking restrictions on data centers passed during the American midterms, and that the new Texas governor promised a moratorium. The publication anticipates negotiations in which local jurisdictions demand tax revenue, infrastructure funding or other benefits from developers. It also expects greater federal attention to AI safety when the newly elected Congress convenes. The policy dispute concerns not simply whether AI should expand, but who bears the costs of power generation, transmission and local disruption.

The cybersecurity dimension gives that debate urgency. The special report describes powerful AI systems capable of identifying exploitable software vulnerabilities and warns that offensive capabilities may spread faster than defensive patching. Anthropic’s Mythos is presented as an example of a model considered too dangerous for unrestricted release. According to The Economist, leaders attending an AI conference in 2026 expressed serious concern about the possibility of a future cyber incident causing $100 billion in damage or 10,000 deaths. These numbers represent assessments of potential harm, not measured outcomes or established forecasts.

Counter-argument: The magazine also observes that the same models can discover weaknesses before attackers exploit them. Widespread defensive adoption could improve software security, provided organizations maintain experienced teams and reliable update procedures. The net effect is therefore conditional on deployment and governance rather than fixed by capability alone.

Energy, economics and geopolitics remain interconnected in the publication’s other forecasts. Its Middle East outlook expects fewer direct confrontations than in 2026 but cautions that this would not necessarily amount to durable peace. Its fiscal analysis warns that budget pressures in the United States and France may constrain government choices, even if lower oil prices temporarily ease inflation. European elections could slow collective responses to Ukraine, trade and a new long-term EU budget. China’s expected 2027 Communist Party congress is portrayed as likely to consolidate Xi Jinping’s authority while leaving questions about succession, economic reform and external relations unresolved.

The business press material also shows how the AI boom alters corporate structure. A column on spin-offs cites successful separations including GE Vernova and Sandisk, as well as more troubled reorganizations involving Honeywell and other conglomerates. It describes companies trying to isolate data-center and technology assets for investors while warning that repeated restructurings impose costs and do not automatically create economic value. The resulting corporate landscape may become simpler within individual businesses while remaining complex for shareholders and regulators.

Counter-argument: The column provides both successful and disappointing cases, so corporate separation cannot be treated as inherently destructive or inherently valuable. The case for a restructuring depends on the assets, management, debts and market conditions involved.

The Economist’s overarching 2027 assessment is that technical progress could accelerate even as political and financial tolerance for the associated risks declines. Greater capabilities may coexist with more competition, lower prices, regulatory constraints, cybersecurity incidents and volatile valuations. Geopolitical rivals may gain new sources of leverage, but the international production system remains intertwined. The balance of outcomes will depend less on any single headline model or stock-market debut than on infrastructure delivery, institutional adaptation and the economics of deployment.

Counter-argument: The publication does not present the most dramatic scenarios as certainties. It recognizes that competition may make AI more accessible, that sensible regulation could reduce some risks, and that existing supply-chain dependencies can discourage confrontation. The central forecast is a widening range of possible outcomes rather than an inevitable crash or an inevitable technological breakthrough in every sector.
