Export Controls, Sanctions, and Defense Spending

While markets were focused on the leadership crisis surrounding the departure of NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang, volatility in semiconductor shares, and preparations for the Federal Reserve's December meeting, there was unusually concentrated activity in U.S. regulatory measures, Treasury sanctions, and U.S. and EU defense budgets. Between November 23 and 25, 2026, the U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) added seven companies in Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand, including three Megaspeed group entities, to the Entity List under a presumption of license denial. At the same time, the U.S. Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) blocked more than two dozen entities associated with Iran's Rahbar shadow banking network and identified nearly $15 billion in potential shadow financing through the United Arab Emirates (UAE), mainland China, Hong Kong, Singapore, and the United Kingdom. Within 48 hours, the Council of the European Union and the U.S. Department of War approved or allocated up to €45.0 billion in assistance to Ukraine, including up to €31.7 billion for defense-industrial capacity, and $3.301 billion in announced U.S. defense contracts.

The concentration of announcements across four official channels (Federal Register, OFAC/Treasury, EUR-Lex, and War.gov) within the 72-hour period of November 23–25, 2026, may indicate structural changes in supply chains and the allocation of public funding rather than merely a short-term news cycle. The November 25 BIS rule restricts transit channels in Southeast Asia, building on the November 19 rule controlling remote access to AI computing resources. Combined with the BIS suspension of its 50% affiliate rule, due to expire January 10, 2027, these developments could create substantial compliance barriers for non-U.S. data centers, foundries, outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) providers, and correspondent banks, while conferring regulatory advantages on U.S. cloud companies and direct contractual support on the U.S. and European-Ukrainian defense industries.

Announcements clustered in the same period do not establish a coordinated policy or a causal link among the policy decisions. The announced contracts cover only part of military procurement, and the nearly $15 billion represents potential financing identified by Treasury rather than a confirmed amount of completed illicit transactions.

The rules include licensing exceptions, transit provisions, and transition periods; they do not amount to a universal prohibition on all overseas computing or trade.

Market implications

Markets facing greater restrictions: transit and end recipients of advanced AI computing capacity (more than 12 million aggregate TPP) connected to Macau, Country Group D:5, and the seven Entity List entities in Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand, with licensing also introduced for Indonesia and Brazil; non-U.S. foundries and OSAT providers working with unauthorized chip designers; Iran's Rahbar shadow networks and their banking and shipping channels in the UAE, Hong Kong, Singapore, Turkey, the Marshall Islands, and the British Virgin Islands; and 101 companies in Russia's defense-industrial network subject to EU sanctions.

Recipients of public funding or regulatory support: U.S. defense and space contractors (The Boeing Co., Amentum Technology Inc., Sea Box Inc.) through direct FY2027 contracts ($3.301 billion in announced contracts on November 23–24) and autonomous-system, counter-unmanned aircraft system (C-UAS), and critical-material programs (6K Inc., $250 million; BAE Systems); European and Ukrainian defense joint ventures in unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs), air defense, electronic warfare (EW), and radar (up to €31.7 billion for Ukraine's defense industry under Decision 2026/2623 and €100 million under EPF Decision 2026/2618); and U.S.-headquartered data centers and semiconductor customers exempted from the new licensing requirements for shipments to five diversion-risk countries.

A licensing requirement or exemption does not by itself establish the realized loss or gain of market share. Contract awards, approved funding ceilings, and conditional financing also represent different stages of commitment.

90-day risk: three successive BIS deadlines—December 23, 2026 (expiration of the transit provision for the Megaspeed listings); January 10, 2027 (expiration of the suspension of the 50% affiliate rule); and approximately February 17, 2027 (end of the 90-day licensing window for commercial recommendation systems in foreign data centers)—coincide with the January 5, 2027, deadline for comments on polysilicon and specialized pharmaceutical imports and the possibility of U.S. Treasury secondary sanctions against non-U.S. banks.

Whether the three regulatory deadlines produce cumulative effects depends on subsequent extensions, licensing decisions, and enforcement, including any actual use of secondary sanctions.

How Regulation Is Reshaping Investment

AI export controls and semiconductor investment

BIS rules published on November 9, 19, and 25, 2026 and the Treasury’s November 23 announcement illustrate the tightening of U.S. controls. From November 9 to 25, advanced-chip shipments to five diversion-risk countries (Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil) became subject to licensing, with exemptions for U.S.-headquartered companies. Remote data center access restrictions (more than 12 million aggregate TPP) were established for entities in Macau and Country Group D:5, alongside licensing presumptions for foundries and OSAT providers (3A090.a). Seven companies in Malaysia, Singapore, and Thailand, including three Megaspeed entities, were added to the Entity List under a presumption of denial. The U.S. Treasury identified Singapore (approximately 2.6 billion) in the context of shadow flows through the UAE.

Quarterly holdings reported for Appaloosa, Duquesne, NVIDIA, and Renaissance Technologies in November 12–16 Form 13F-HR coverage, together with AMD and PLTR Form 4 transactions from November 16–20 and NVDA transactions from Q3, suggest changes in investment preferences. The November 12–20 disclosures reflect, in the case of Form 13F, holdings as of September 30, 2026. Funds reduced or fully liquidated stakes in fabless competitors and foundry and component businesses (AMD and AVGO down 100% at Appaloosa; AMD, LRCX, ARM, and SNDK down 100% and TSM down 94.5% at Duquesne). Capital was reallocated toward memory and storage (MU, $1.33 billion at Appaloosa and purchases by RenTech; STX, $405.64 million, and WDC at Duquesne) and cloud AI providers (CRWV and NBIS at NVIDIA). In PLTR, Duquesne's entry coincided with Peter Thiel's $122.25 million purchase.

Asian foundries and OSAT contractors serving unauthorized chip designers, non-U.S. data center operators in Southeast Asia and the Middle East (facing stricter know-your-customer requirements and licensing within a 90-day window), and NVDA in connection with corporate governance and upcoming December U.S. Senate export-control hearings may face pressure. U.S.-headquartered cloud AI providers explicitly exempted from licensing for the five countries under export control rule, memory and storage providers (MU, STX, WDC), and defense analytics software (PLTR) could receive support. The timing suggests a possible association rather than a causal relationship. The quarterly disclosures represent September 30 holdings, before NVDA's November 11 CEO departure and the November 19/23 BIS rules. Duquesne's reduction in TSM and exit from AMD, with zero exposure to NVDA, thus preceded the regulatory developments, while Appaloosa's 31% increase in NVDA exposure in Q3 may face repricing risk ahead of January 10, 2027 (BIS 50% affiliate rule) and publication of fourth-quarter 13F filings on February 16, 2027.

A prior portfolio decision may reflect ordinary investment considerations rather than anticipation of export rules. The fourth-quarter 13F reports and subsequent licensing outcomes are necessary to assess whether the proposed correlation persists.

Defense spending, space infrastructure, and software investment

U.S. defense announcements and European Union decisions 2026/2618, 2026/2623, and 2026/2574 describe substantial defense commitments. From November 16 to 25, 2026, the United States allocated $3.301 billion from FY2027 funds through recorded Air Force, special operations, Army, and Space Command contracts (The Boeing Co., $3.016 billion; Sea Box Inc., $216.36 million; Amentum Technology Inc., $68.28 million for Cheyenne Mountain SFS and Peterson SFB), plus $250 million in credit support for 6K Inc. and deliveries of more than 25,000 C-UAS systems. The Council of the EU approved up to €31.7 billion for Ukraine's defense industry and €100 million under the EPF for accelerated joint ventures in UAVs, air defense, EW, and radar, while imposing sanctions on 101 Russian defense-industry companies.

Institutional and insider disclosures between November 16 and 23, 2026, included Form 13F-HR (November 16), Form 4 (November 20), and Schedule 13D (November 23). During that period, Appaloosa reportedly increased SpaceX exposure by 1,626% (an estimated $518 million). Ronald Burkle's OA3 group reached a 10.6% stake in space integrator MNTS with an activist agenda (a $1.24 million Form 4 transaction and Schedule 13D). Duquesne's third-quarter purchases of PLTR coincided with Peter Thiel's $122.25 million transaction.

Russian defense-industrial companies subject to direct asset freezes under EU Regulation 2026/2574 and manufacturers outside European-Ukrainian joint ventures or U.S. space security infrastructure may face pressure. The U.S. aerospace and satellite sector (The Boeing Co., Amentum Technology Inc., SpaceX, MNTS), European drone, air defense, and EW manufacturers, and defense software providers (PLTR) could receive support. Over 90 days, this may create a more predictable flow of public funding because FY2027 funds are already legally committed under U.S. contracts and Council Decision 2026/2618 calls for contracts to be concluded as quickly as possible, with eligible expenditures beginning November 20, 2026.

The reported SpaceX investment remains an estimate and does not establish comparable investment trends among direct government contractors. Approved funding and signed contracts also do not establish the timing of realized revenue for every company in the segment.

Iran sanctions and energy investment

The connection between the sanctions and energy investment remains weak and indirect. Against the backdrop of OFAC's blocking of more than two dozen Rahbar network entities and the U.S. Treasury's November 23 identification of nearly $15 billion in potential oil and shipping flows through the UAE, China/Hong Kong, and Singapore, Wisevoter reported alleged statements by Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps regarding three tankers in the Strait of Hormuz and Brent and WTI prices of $86.31 and $79.69, respectively. U.S. corporate disclosures confirm a 39.98 million-share stake in oil and gas company Granite Ridge Resources in a November 24–25 Schedule 13D/A and the retention of Chevron and Occidental Petroleum among Berkshire Hathaway's top ten portfolio positions in its Q3 13F. The ownership disclosures do not establish a direct shift in fund or insider capital caused by the November sanctions round.

The Granite Ridge ownership filing confirms a stake, not necessarily new purchases in response to sanctions. Berkshire’s holdings predate the November sanctions round, and the reported tanker statements and oil prices are not independently verified.
