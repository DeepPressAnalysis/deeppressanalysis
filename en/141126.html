01

THE COLLAPSE OF TECHNOCRATIC UNTOUCHABILITY AND THE ANATOMY OF THE MEGASPEED CASE

On the morning of Thursday, November 12, 2026, the 33-year era of Jensen Huang's sole leadership at Nvidia Corporation ended in a rapid departure. The company's co-founder—who transformed a California gaming graphics card developer founded in 1993 at a roadside Denny's diner with $40,000 in capital into the foundation of the global artificial intelligence revolution with a peak market capitalization of $5.8 trillion—resigned. The formal trigger was the disclosure of two extramarital relationships involving the chief executive: first with Alice Huang, head of the Singaporean cloud provider Megaspeed International, whom US intelligence agencies regard as an agent of the Ministry of State Security of the People's Republic of China, and subsequently with his own subordinate, Kimberly Powell, head of Nvidia's healthcare division. Yet behind this corporate drama—which has already cost Nvidia between $1.8 trillion and $2 trillion in market capitalization from its October peak, including a loss of roughly $500 billion to $600 billion in a single week—lies a systemic crisis of far greater scale.

A comparison of facts from business, political, and intelligence circles shows that Huang's fall became the point of intersection for three fundamental processes shaping global politics and economics in November 2026. First, it exposed the vulnerability of the financial pyramid of artificial intelligence, in which Nvidia functioned as a quasi-central bank lending to its own customers and guaranteeing their multibillion-dollar obligations. Second, the investigation into Megaspeed's activities uncovered the paralysis of the US export control system, where the geopolitical containment of China became subordinated to transactional lobbying, donations to White House infrastructure projects, and bureaucratic infighting within the Donald Trump administration. Third, the collapse of the untouchability of technology titans coincided with a resounding defeat for the Republican Party in the November 3 congressional midterm elections—a defeat driven by American public fatigue over the protracted war with Iran, a new wave of inflation, and the unchecked expansion of energy-intensive data centers.

As a result, the global system simultaneously confronted a power vacuum at its most critical technology corporation, a reallocation of authority in Washington, and a sharp tightening of coercive diplomacy from the Persian Gulf to the front lines in Ukraine. Beneath the facade of public statements regarding national security and energy independence lies an intricate web of interdependencies in which American chips feed Chinese technology giants through Malaysian and Singaporean structures, Russian diesel doubles its seaborne export volumes through informal White House deals, and the war in the Middle East paradoxically accelerates the global transition to Chinese green technologies faster than any United Nations climate summits.

The public chronology of the Nvidia chief executive's fall began on November 8, when Jensen Huang sent an apologetic email to employees acknowledging marital infidelity, and the company's board of directors announced an internal investigation retaining the law firm of former federal prosecutor Joon Kim to examine whether the chief executive had become a target of foreign intelligence. Initially, the board of directors—most of whose members had worked with Huang since the 1990s and which until Thursday did not even maintain a chairman position—sought to retain him in his post, stating there were no signs that commercial secrets or national security had been compromised. As recently as October, Nvidia representatives maintained that their chief executive's contacts with Alice Huang represented standard customer communication, and the corporate chief himself assured that the company had accounted for every chip delivered to Megaspeed. The board's position collapsed only on the evening of November 11, when reports regarding his link to the suspected Chinese intelligence operative were followed by disclosures of an affair with Kimberly Powell, head of the company's healthcare division, which constituted a direct violation of corporate norms within the firm itself.

However, an investigation by The Wall Street Journal demonstrates that Jensen Huang's relationship with Alice Huang was by no means a chance acquaintance between supplier and buyer at industry conferences in Taipei in June 2024 and May 2025. The future head of Megaspeed—a Hong Kong native who previously worked for Chinese state broadcaster CCTV—was introduced to the Nvidia chief by her own husband, Chinese financier and Shanghai Hexi investment firm founder Kan Wei, whom Jensen Huang had known for roughly a decade through gaming industry investments. For two years, Kan Wei not only knew about his wife's affair with the head of the world's most valuable company, but also openly discussed their closeness with business partners, half-jokingly referring to his wife as Jensen Huang's personal assistant. The family tandem of Kan Wei and Alice Huang, who had previously been held liable by Chinese regulators for violations during a pharmaceutical company acquisition in 2018 (with Kan Wei himself detained in Shanghai in 2026 in a stock market manipulation case before recently being released), used personal access to Nvidia's chief executive to build a multibillion-dollar scheme to circumvent US sanctions.

The mechanism of this scheme reveals a direct link between the Singaporean startup and the top echelon of China's technology sector. In March 2023, shortly after the Joe Biden administration prohibited the export of advanced AI processors to China in October 2022, the couple acquired the Singaporean subsidiary of the Chinese gaming company 7Road Holdings and renamed it Megaspeed International. A key player in Megaspeed's launch was the Chinese e-commerce giant Alibaba, which acutely needed remote access to Nvidia-based computing capacity outside China. Megaspeed presented guarantees to Nvidia's management that Alibaba would become its anchor client, and by November 2023 it obtained privileged status as an Nvidia cloud partner with priority access to scarce accelerators. According to estimates by Culper Research analysts and customs statistics from Big Trade Data, in less than three years Megaspeed and its Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix purchased more than $4.6 billion worth of Nvidia equipment. Part of these servers were indeed placed in Malaysian data centers to serve Alibaba, but another part, in violation of US export controls, was physically shipped to mainland China, including to the Shanghai cloud provider Century Litong, linked to Kan Wei's former business partners. While Alibaba leased American chips through the Singaporean intermediary, its own semiconductor unit T-Head, as noted by The Economist, released a next-generation processor in September 2026 and officially filed this week for an IPO in Hong Kong to raise capital for its own computing ecosystem.

Nvidia's own financial reporting indicates that the company was aware of the vulnerability of the Singapore route long before the public scandal. An analytical breakdown by The Wall Street Journal points out that Nvidia claims to have placed Megaspeed on its internal stop-list in June 2025. In the fiscal quarter ended July 2025, Singapore accounted for $10.2 billion, or 22 percent of Nvidia's total global revenue, growing 81 percent year-over-year. Yet in the very next quarter ended October 2025—precisely when US federal investigators intensified their probe into Megaspeed—Nvidia abruptly changed its methodology for geographic revenue accounting. Instead of the billing address, the company began recording revenue according to the headquarters location of buyers, many of whom purchased chips as part of complete server systems from US corporations Dell and Super Micro Computer. As a result of this accounting reclassification, Singapore instantly disappeared from Nvidia's revenue breakdown, and the bulk of previous Singaporean sales were reclassified as US-related revenue, depriving investors of the ability to track the dynamics of Asian re-exports. Furthermore, according to sources, Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter personally intervened in the US Commerce Department's investigation of Megaspeed in the summer of 2025 and sought to determine whether the Trump administration was involved in Alice Huang's detention in Singapore.

Even more serious contradictions surfaced at the interstate level. On Thursday, November 12, Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs published an unprecedented statement shedding light on why the investigation against Megaspeed had reached a dead end. Singaporean authorities reported that they began inspecting the company for false declarations regarding the end recipients of servers back in February 2025, and in June 2025 questioned Alice Huang and seized her passport. In February 2026, however, Singapore was forced to return her documents and allow her to leave the country, issuing only a warning and barring her from re-entry for providing inaccurate information about her role in managing the firm; Taiwan imposed a similar entry ban under its anti-infiltration law. Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs directly cited the lack of assistance from US law enforcement agencies as the reason for closing the case: starting in September 2025, Singaporean police repeatedly sent requests to the US Department of Justice and the FBI asking for materials from the American investigation into server manufacturer Supermicro, but received only extremely limited information. In response to publications about this demarche, a US Justice Department spokesperson in a comment to The Washington Post referred only to the March arrest of Super Micro co-founder Wally Liaw on charges of conspiring to smuggle chips into China, whereas the FBI and the White House left Singapore's statements unanswered.

The causes of this institutional paralysis are rooted in the political rapprochement between Jensen Huang and the Donald Trump administration. After Trump's return to the White House, Nvidia's chief executive launched a large-scale campaign to weaken Biden-era export restrictions, convincing the president that preserving American dominance required keeping Chinese developers dependent on American chips. As The Wall Street Journal reconstructs in detail, an acute split emerged within the administration. Officials from the National Security Council, the State Department, the Pentagon, and the Treasury Department viewed Huang's position as a security threat: after his very first meeting at the White House, one adviser remarked that Huang would sell chips directly to the People's Liberation Army of China if the government allowed him to. In April 2025, however, Trump fired several skeptical National Security Council staffers, and Huang and his wife attended a private dinner at the Mar-a-Lago residence costing $1 million per person. The Nvidia chief's main allies in the administration were White House artificial intelligence adviser David Sacks, who repeated Huang's arguments at meetings, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick.

The result of this alliance was a revision of the export control regime on commercial terms. In July 2025, Trump allowed Nvidia to resume shipments of H20 chips to China. In October 2025, ahead of the summit between Trump and Xi Jinping in Busan, South Korea, Nvidia donated $25 million toward the construction of a new White House ballroom, and Trump himself also purchased shares in the chipmaker. Although Secretary of State Marco Rubio and hardliners managed to block Huang's attempt to obtain permission to export the newest Blackwell processors to China, on December 8, 2025, Trump approved direct sales to China of powerful H200 chips in exchange for transferring 25 percent of the revenue from these sales directly to the US federal government budget.

This financial-political nexus explains why the White House left intelligence warnings without consequence. According to The Economist, The Wall Street Journal, and The Washington Post, Marco Rubio and CIA Director John Ratcliffe knew about Huang's connection to the suspected Chinese intelligence operative last year, and in the spring of 2026 a classified memorandum on the threat posed by Alice Huang was prepared under Rubio's leadership. Moreover, on May 13, 2026, during an Air Force One refueling stop in Alaska en route to Beijing, Rubio personally reminded Trump of this intelligence immediately before Jensen Huang boarded the plane to join the government delegation. Despite the fact that four agencies—the State Department, the Pentagon, the Department of Energy, and the Treasury Department—supported placing Megaspeed on the federal blacklist and clashed with Howard Lutnick in the Situation Room and the Oval Office (the Commerce Department itself denies the fact of such clashes), the agency head delayed introducing restrictions for months. As emphasized in a Washington Post editorial and articles in The Economist, additional attention in upcoming congressional investigations will be drawn to the fact that the investment bank Cantor Fitzgerald, formerly led by Lutnick and now run by his sons, disclosed a large stake in Nvidia shares, although the bank explains that it holds these securities on behalf of clients as part of asset management.

Notably, the effort to defend the Nvidia chief's position was waged not only in the corridors of Washington, but also through coordinated information campaigns. According to research by the nonprofit organization Midas Project published by The Washington Post, immediately after Huang's confession on the social network X, a network of at least 400 fake accounts was activated, defending Nvidia's chief executive and promoting continued shipments of advanced chips to Chinese firms. These bots deliberately left replies under posts by journalists, former officials, the House Select Committee on China, and Senator Elizabeth Warren. An analysis of digital footprints showed that nine such accounts were on a list that the Israeli newspaper Haaretz had previously linked to a network of the Israeli PR firm Koios, which conducted a campaign on behalf of Qatar, although Koios itself denies involvement in the campaign surrounding Nvidia. Now Huang has been summoned to a closed briefing with the Republican chairs of three House committees on November 18, while the Congress transitioning to Democratic control in January—including Senators Elizabeth Warren, Chris Coons, Richard Blumenthal, and Republican Tim Scott—is preparing large-scale hearings into the relationships between tech giants and the Trump administration.

AI FINANCIAL ARCHITECTURE & DEBT LIMITS

CAPITAL MARKETS • ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
02

FINANCIAL ENGINEERING OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE: CIRCULAR LENDING AND WALL STREET'S DEBT LIMIT

The appointment of chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim head of Nvidia is intended to calm Wall Street ahead of the publication of the company's quarterly earnings next week, yet Huang's departure calls into question the very financial model that underpinned the artificial intelligence investment boom. Although President Trump on Thursday called for former Apple chief Tim Cook—who stepped down as CEO in August 2026 after 15 years of leadership—to be appointed in Huang's place, while among real contenders in the industry observers name CUDA software platform creator Ian Buck, Arm head Rene Haas, and AMD chief Lisa Su, the core problem lies in the fate of Nvidia's gigantic investment portfolio.

Under Huang, who managed the company without a formal organizational chart through weekly meetings with 60 direct reports, Nvidia turned into the chief venture investor and credit guarantor for the entire AI industry. According to the Financial Times, based on statistics from PitchBook and Dealroom, by the end of July 2026 the volume of Nvidia's equity investments and commitments reached $124 billion. Since the beginning of the year, the corporation and its venture unit completed more than 120 deals—averaging one every two and a half days. In total, Nvidia's transactions include $55 billion for planned acquisitions and team purchases (including the acquisition of the Hugging Face platform for $12.9 billion and Groq), $23 billion in investments in shares of public companies (among them $5 billion in Intel, $2 billion each in CoreWeave, Nebius, Marvell, Coherent, and Lumentum, $3.5 billion in MediaTek, as well as $1 billion each in Nokia and Naver), and over $60 billion directed into more than 200 startups, including $30 billion in OpenAI, $10 billion in Anthropic (including a private placement in parallel with its IPO scheduled for November 19), $5 billion in Ilya Sutskever's Safe Superintelligence, and $2.5 billion in Thinking Machines Lab.

Even more serious questions among analysts are raised by the practice of off-balance-sheet commitments and so-called circular financing, whereby Nvidia provided capital or guarantees to customers so that they would purchase its own graphics processors. By the end of July, Nvidia had committed to buying back $36 billion worth of unused computing capacity from young cloud providers ("neoclouds"), allowing them to raise loans against these contracts to buy Nvidia chips. In addition, the corporation signed data center lease agreements worth $20 billion (including a 3-gigawatt campus in Texas subleased to Lambda, which supplies capacity to Anthropic) and provided credit guarantees of up to $105 billion covering OpenAI's lease and energy payments for the construction of a 4.25-gigawatt campus in Ohio being developed by SB Energy with support from SoftBank in exchange for a 20-year exclusive use of Nvidia equipment. Together with Wall Street financial groups, Huang was developing a plan to use Nvidia's balance sheet to mobilize more than $500 billion in external capital for chip financing. The scale of these deals grew so rapidly that at the end of August the company added a new warning to its quarterly report regarding the financial and counterparty risks of its guarantees, and Colette Kress herself, as noted by The Wall Street Journal, slowed down the parameters of the agreement to support OpenAI's data center in August after a negative market reaction.

In assessing where a revision of this model will lead, leading economic publications arrive at different conclusions. Financial commentators at The Wall Street Journal believe that Huang's resignation could prove beneficial for Nvidia's own shareholders: much as Tim Cook after Steve Jobs's departure in 2011 directed Apple's cash reserves toward share buybacks and dividends (delivering a total return of over 2,700 percent over 15 years versus 750 percent for the S&P 500 index), new management at Nvidia will be able to abandon risky customer financing and direct the $261 billion in free cash flow projected by FactSet analysts over the next four quarters toward payouts to shareholders, especially since the company's stock is currently trading at its lowest multiple in decades of less than 12 times projected annual earnings. For the rest of the ecosystem, however, such a step threatens a liquidity squeeze: according to UBS analyst Tim Arcuri, winding down Nvidia's financial programs could withdraw $50 billion or more from the industry at a moment when interest rates are rising. In trading on November 12, the market already demonstrated this divergence: while Nvidia shares closed down 4.3 percent and shares of closely linked cloud operator CoreWeave fell 7 percent, quotes for competitors Broadcom and AMD, as well as the largest hyperscale data center operators, rose.

A broader macroeconomic analysis conducted by The Economist points to a fundamental gap between capital expenditures on AI and current revenues from the technology. According to the publication's calculations, which are close to estimates by Columbia University economist Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh and researchers at the Stanford Institute for Economic Policy Research, to ensure a normal rate of return on planned data centers, total AI industry revenue must reach roughly $3 trillion per year by 2030—equivalent to nearly a tenth of the entire wage bill across OECD countries ($35 trillion). Today, the combined annual revenue of all model developers is estimated at only about $200 billion, of which Anthropic accounted for an annualized revenue run rate of $81 billion at the end of October, and OpenAI accounted for $50 billion at the end of September. Moreover, modeling based on alternative indicators of corporate spending (data from Ramp cards and the TickerTrends service) shows that three-month revenue growth rates for AI developers slowed from 80–90 percent in the spring to 12 percent in October 2026.

Unlike classic software of the cloud subscription era, which had a near-zero marginal cost of copying and high switching costs, the business of language models requires continuous expenditures on electricity and expensive chips for every query, while switching between providers using tools like OpenRouter comes down to changing a single line of code. Furthermore, advanced American models lead Chinese counterparts (such as Kimi K3.1 Pro, comparable on the Epoch Capabilities Index to April's US flagship model GPT 5.5 Pro) by only six months, and a study by the hedge fund Bridgewater based on tools from the startup Thinking Machines showed that a specially fine-tuned small Chinese model outperformed leading models from Anthropic and OpenAI in processing financial documents. Outside of programming, corporate adoption of AI is proceeding unevenly: while venture-backed companies spend an average of about $100 per employee per month on AI, ordinary firms spend only about $10, and Goldman Sachs in October banned non-engineering employees from using AI coding tools, citing weak productivity growth and cybersecurity risks.

The attempt by technology corporations to accelerate monetization through autonomous AI agents—such as Muse from Meta, Dots from OpenAI, Grok Bot from SpaceX, and Gemini Spark from Google—has run into computational and financial barriers that are analyzed in detail in The Wall Street Journal and the Financial Times. On one hand, continuous background operation of agents requires enormous server resources: according to estimates by analysts at Deutsche Bank and the startup Vals AI, running one active Muse agent on a dedicated virtual machine costs $25 to $50 per month, which caused Meta to limit its free launch to US territory, OpenAI to open access to the Dots agent only on plans starting at $100 per month, and Elon Musk to admit that the cost of servicing Grok Bot is excessively high relative to revenue. On the other hand, endowing AI agents with the function of independently paying for purchases has provoked resistance from the banking sector. The largest US credit card issuer, JPMorgan Chase, began blocking transactions by AI bots to which clients had passed their card details, and jointly with Citigroup, Bank of America, and Capital One restricted agents' access to deposit accounts due to fears of data leaks, rising chargebacks and fraudulent purchases in fake online stores, as well as the risk of an instantaneous outflow of deposits to other banks. As BBVA board chairman Carlos Torres Vila notes in the Financial Times, due to the lack of dedicated communication channels, AI agents are forced to log into systems posing as humans using user passwords, which requires the urgent legislative introduction of verified digital credentials ("Know Your Agent") and special agent tokens currently being developed by payment company Stripe on the basis of the Link wallet jointly with Visa and Mastercard.

Against this backdrop, a telling divergence arises between The Economist and the Financial Times regarding how dangerous a probable downturn in the AI sector could become for the global financial system. The Economist argues that even in the event of a stock market crash in the style of the 2000 dot-com bust, a deep financial crisis modeled on the 1873 railroad crash is unlikely: accumulated capital in the AI industry has been financed predominantly through equity and cash flows of technology giants (debt and lease obligations of the five largest data center owners amount to about 30 percent of their book value, or just 6 percent of market capitalization), and the bulk of US capital expenditures goes toward imported equipment like chips, which makes Taiwan extremely vulnerable (whose annual economic growth reached 14 percent), but limits the direct dependence of US GDP on the AI boom to roughly 0.3 percent.

However, Financial Times financial commentator Toby Nangle points to a hidden bottleneck in the structure of the corporate debt market. Last week Goldman Sachs raised its estimate of capital expenditures on AI infrastructure in 2025–2031 from $7.6 trillion to $8.5 trillion, projecting that 35 percent of this amount—about $3 trillion—will be financed through debt. Although the $10.2 trillion US investment-grade corporate bond market is capable of digesting such a sum in aggregate, the mandates of institutional asset managers and insurance companies strictly cap the share of a single issuer in a portfolio at 1 to 2 percent, which corresponds to $100 billion to $200 billion of debt per corporation. According to S&P Global Ratings data cited by the Financial Times, borrowers such as Amazon, Oracle, Meta, and SpaceX (the latter, according to a report by The Washington Post, having just announced plans to invest more than $74 billion in two AI supercomputer campuses in Texas and Arizona) are already moving toward reaching or exceeding this 2 percent limit. This means that credit spreads for technology giants are beginning to widen not because of a deterioration in their solvency, but due to the saturation of bond fund portfolios.

The first consequences of this overheating are already reflected in the balance sheets of the largest investors. Masayoshi Son's Japanese group SoftBank, whose market capitalization has fallen by more than half from its June peak amid a decline of over 50 percent in the shares of its chip developer Arm and Intel Corporation, the postponement of OpenAI's listing to 2027, and Chinese regulators blocking the purchase of ABB's robotics division, reported a quarterly net loss of $7 billion on November 10. Of this amount, $3.7 billion came from writing off contracts for reserving gas turbines and power equipment for American data centers, while quotes for SoftBank's five-year credit default swaps reached a four-year high, reflecting a 31 percent probability of default on debt obligations. Simultaneously, October 2026 became the worst month since the start of the pandemic for global hedge funds (credit fund Arini Capital lost 19.7 percent, Millennium's main fund lost 2.3 percent), which by November 9 had reduced the share of AI instruments in their portfolios from 47 percent to 32 percent. In search of protection from a technology sector correction, investors began shifting capital to markets unrelated to AI hardware production: as noted by The Economist and the Financial Times, the main center of attraction has become India, whose Nifty 50 index has grown 11.6 percent since the beginning of October, and on November 5—amid a 5.5 to 6 percent drop in the Nasdaq index—recorded the largest single-day foreign capital inflow in several years of $1.6 billion to $1.7 billion.

US POLITICAL REALIGNMENT & MACROECONOMIC PRESSURE

MIDTERM ELECTIONS • INFLATION & ENERGY
03

THE US ELECTORAL EARTHQUAKE: DATA CENTER REVOLT, INFLATIONARY SHOCK, AND COALITION FRACTURES

Stock market instability surrounding the technology sector proved directly linked to the political upheaval in the US midterm elections on November 3, 2026, where artificial intelligence became a significant voting factor for the first time. According to an analysis by The Economist, American voters living near data center construction sites shifted toward the Democratic Party over the past year by roughly 5 percentage points more strongly than voters with similar demographic characteristics in other districts. Out of 41 local referendums on restricting data center construction, proponents of restrictions won in 40 cases. In gubernatorial elections, Democratic candidates advocating a pause or limits on new server complexes won in states that, according to calculations by research firm Cleanview, account for 41 percent of all planned data center capacity in the country (or, according to an estimate by SemiAnalysis analyst Maya Barkin, 25.6 gigawatts of not-yet-started projects scheduled for commissioning in 2028, equivalent to nearly half of all current US data center capacity). The most tangible blow to the industry was the unexpected election of Democrat Gina Hinojosa as governor of Texas, where roughly a third of American data center construction projects are concentrated, as well as the victory of Democrat Cindy Holscher in the Kansas gubernatorial race and the success of candidates with similar platforms in Ohio and Georgia.

The scale of the nationwide Republican defeat, which Florida Governor Ron DeSantis compared to a "meteor strike," exceeded pollsters' expectations: the average leftward voting shift compared with the 2024 presidential election was about 14 to 15 percentage points, and in several southern states reached 20 points or more. Democrats regained a majority in the House of Representatives and control of the Senate, bringing their representation in the upper chamber to 55 seats (with vote counting continuing in Alaska, where the Democrat also holds a narrow lead) after victories in Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, Texas, South Carolina, and Mississippi.

The latter two campaigns became symbols of the changing electoral map of the American South. In Mississippi, former prosecutor Scott Colom defeated incumbent Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith—who in 2023 had personally blocked his appointment to a federal judgeship—by a margin of more than 4,500 votes; Colom became the first Black senator from the state since Reconstruction and the first Democrat in that post since 1982. In South Carolina, as described in detail in a report by The Wall Street Journal, pediatrician Annie Andrews won the Senate seat held for more than twenty years by Lindsey Graham, who died in July 2026 after winning the June primary. After Graham's death, Donald Trump secured the nomination of his sister Darline Graham, which overlapped with an acute conflict in the local party organization between supporters of the MAGA movement and traditional Republicans; ultimately Andrews, who focused on the cost of living and was backed by a joint television ad featuring former governors—Republican David Beasley and Democrat Jim Hodges—won even in conservative Greenville County, where Trump had won by a 22 percentage point margin in 2024. The only major Republican stronghold remained Florida, where thanks to a 1.5-million lead in registered voters and support from the Cuban community, Republican Byron Donalds won the gubernatorial election against Democrat David Jolly, although even there the party lost its supermajority in the legislature. Nationwide, according to The Washington Post and The Wall Street Journal, Democrats won a majority of governorships for the first time in twenty years, won majorities in 12 state legislative chambers, stripped Republicans of supermajorities in 13 chambers, and formed six new "trifectas" (simultaneous control of the governorship and both legislative chambers) in Arizona, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin.

The main driver of this vote was voter discontent over the rising cost of living amid trade tariffs and the war with Iran. Statistical data published this week show the persistence of inflationary pressure in the United States. The Consumer Price Index in October rose 0.4 percent for the month, while the core index excluding food and energy increased 0.3 percent for the third consecutive month (the Bureau of Labor Statistics did not calculate an official annual figure due to the government shutdown in October of last year, but the median and trimmed-mean CPI indices calculated by the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland rose 3.7 and 4.5 percent year-over-year). As Wall Street Journal economic commentator Greg Ip notes, expectations that price shocks from tariffs and the war with Iran that began in February would quickly fade are not being borne out, as price increases are spreading across a wide range of goods and services. As a result, market participants expect another interest rate hike by the Federal Reserve in December, and Federal Reserve Board Governor Christopher Waller has already allowed for the possibility of raising the key rate by 0.5 percentage points at once.

The prospect of monetary policy tightening pushed the yield on 10-year US Treasury bonds to 5.10–5.12 percent, with the yield on two-year notes at 4.86 percent. This led to a deep slump in the US real estate market reported by The Wall Street Journal and The Washington Post: because 30-year mortgage rates remain above 7.11–7.19 percent, existing-home sales in October fell 5.5 percent for the month (and 8.8 percent year-over-year) to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 3.75 million units—the lowest level since August 2010. At the same time, homeowners' reluctance to sell their homes and lose old low loan rates is squeezing supply and keeping the median sales price at a record mark of $416,700, causing the monthly payment when buying a median home to reach $2,468—85 percent more than current homeowners pay. Simultaneously, American workers faced rising medical costs: according to consulting firm Marsh data cited by The Economist, the cost of employer-sponsored health insurance policies covering 157 million Americans will grow by an average of 8 percent in 2027 (the largest jump in more than twenty years, driven by hospital consolidation, demand for GLP-1 class obesity drugs, and hospitals' use of AI billing tools), while the cancellation of federal subsidies under the Affordable Care Act (ACA) sharply increased the cost of individual insurance plans.

Against the backdrop of economic problems, voters rejected radical initiatives from both sides of the political spectrum. Conservative organizations spent at least $183 million on campaign advertising on transgender themes, which did not save Republicans from defeat, although in all four states (Arizona, Washington, Colorado, and Nebraska) where the question of banning transgender students from participating in girls' school sports competitions was put to referendums, the restrictions were approved by a majority of votes even as Democratic candidates won (meanwhile, The Washington Post covered the publication in JAMA Pediatrics of a five-year study by scientists at the University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center finding that nearly 97 percent of 376 surveyed young people who started hormone therapy in adolescence do not regret their decision). On the other hand, in California, Proposition 40—an initiative backed by labor unions and Senator Bernie Sanders to introduce a one-time 5 percent tax on billionaire wealth, opposed by technology entrepreneurs and Governor Gavin Newsom—was defeated. As Wall Street Journal columnist Joseph Sternberg notes, California voters saw in this measure an attempt to compensate for the inflationary ballooning of the US national debt (which has exceeded $40 trillion) by taxing unrealized capital gains on financial assets, which over time would inevitably affect middle-class savings. At the same time, in California, progressive politician Jane Kim was elected state insurance commissioner, promising to replace the crisis-ridden FAIR Plan pool with a mandatory $3 billion to $6 billion state wildfire insurance program for all homes and to introduce a profit cap for private insurers, raising alarm among industry representatives just beginning to recover from the destructive wildfires in Los Angeles County in January 2025.

Even on the issue of power supply for data centers, legislative practice in Washington is already diverging from campaign rhetoric. While Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa is softening her tone, assuring companies of the state's friendliness toward business, the US Senate this week by a majority of 73 votes to 25 backed the advancement of the Bipartisan American Energy Affordability and Jobs Act, prepared by Democratic Senators Martin Heinrich and Sheldon Whitehouse and endorsed by Donald Trump. As The Washington Post reports, the bill is designed to accelerate permitting for energy projects and power transmission lines to supply AI data centers, obligating computing center operators to bear the bulk of grid costs. However, this step provoked a split among Democrats: former Vice President Al Gore, Indigenous organizations, and environmentalists stated that the document undermines key provisions of the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA).

Within both parties, the election results triggered a struggle to change course. In the Republican Party, as publications in the Financial Times, The Economist, and The Wall Street Journal show, several fault lines emerged at once. First, ideologues of the MAGA wing—including Heritage Foundation head Kevin Roberts and Texas Congressman Chip Roy—blamed the defeat on the party's alliance with Silicon Valley billionaires led by Elon Musk (who spent about $225 million on the campaign in Texas), stating that voters rejected the imposition of energy-intensive data centers and technocrats' talk of replacing jobs with universal basic income. Second, media figures on the populist flank (Tucker Carlson, Steve Bannon, and his co-host Natalie Winters) alongside Vice President JD Vance attributed the failure to the war in Iran, inflation, the activities of Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and unconditional support for Israel, whereas representatives of the party's traditional wing Mike Pence and Mitt Romney criticized Trump's economic populism, growing budget deficits, and immigration service methods. Third, differing strategies manifested in congressional party leadership elections: while Republicans in the Senate re-elected moderate South Dakota Senator John Thune as their leader, in the House of Representatives, following Speaker Mike Johnson's departure, Freedom Caucus co-founder Jim Jordan was elected minority leader. Moreover, while The Economist views Jordan's victory as entrenching a hardline Trumpist course in the lower chamber, Wall Street Journal columnist Kimberley Strassel assesses it as a chance to unite the caucus and return to a conservative economic agenda.

A struggle for leadership has also unfolded in the Democratic Party: Maryland Senator Chris Van Hollen announced his candidacy against 75-year-old Chuck Schumer in the upcoming December 1 Senate majority leader election, calling for a renewal of party leadership. Simultaneously, future chairs of key congressional committees—Senators Elizabeth Warren and Richard Blumenthal, as well as Congressman Robert Garcia—sent an official request on Wednesday to President Trump's chief fundraiser Meredith O'Rourke, demanding by November 25 donor lists and information on the collection of more than $800 million for the construction of the White House ballroom and other presidential projects from corporations like Capital One, SoftBank, and Lockheed Martin, which subsequently received favorable decisions from federal agencies. Secretary of State Marco Rubio also came under close press scrutiny: a Washington Post investigation showed that he flew at least 23 times on military aircraft to Gainesville, Florida, including to ten University of Florida football games featuring his son Anthony, with 15 trips classified as personal and costing the treasury more than $1.2 million.

Donald Trump's own reaction to losing Congress combines signals of readiness for compromise with a demonstrative hardening of position. Having stated on November 6 in a CBS interview that his immigration policy may have been "too harsh" and that he would like to reach an agreement with Democrats on immigration, Trump appointed hardline deportation ideologue Stephen Miller as the new White House chief of staff just an hour later. Problems within the accelerated hiring campaign for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) officers became the subject of a Washington Post investigation: a review of the biographies of roughly 300 out of 1,500 identified new agents showed that at least 19 of them had previously faced criminal charges or internal disciplinary proceedings, including five use-of-force cases and six firings or resignations under threat of dismissal, while the Department of Homeland Security allowed recruits to be sent on assignments before completing full background checks. And on Thursday, November 12, Trump threatened to immediately double tariffs on goods from Mexico due to "migrant caravans" allegedly moving toward the border—even though, according to statistics from the US Department of Homeland Security itself, the number of apprehensions on the Mexican border for fiscal year 2026 dropped to 98,700 people (the lowest since 1968 amid an OECD-recorded overall 10 percent drop in legal permanent migration to developed countries in 2025), Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum stated that not a single caravan had approached the US border since 2025, and the US Supreme Court earlier this year already ruled unlawful the president's use of emergency economic powers to impose tariff duties.

GEOPOLITICAL CONFLICTS & SECURITY ARCHITECTURE

MIDDLE EAST • UKRAINE & EUROPEAN SECURITY
04

THE IRANIAN STALEMATE, INFRASTRUCTURE CYBERWARFARE, AND COERCIVE DIPLOMACY OVER UKRAINE

The domestic political defeat of the Republicans was directly reflected in Washington's military and diplomatic strategy in the Middle East. The military campaign against Iran launched by the United States and Israel in February 2026—in the first 24 hours of which US forces using Palantir's analytical AI system Maven Smart System struck more than 3,000 targets (and, according to Bloomberg, the system's use was accompanied by a mistaken strike on a school in Iran in February)—grew into a protracted conflict. Iran disrupted shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, attacking an average of one oil tanker per day, and fired thousands of missiles and drones at Persian Gulf states and Jordanian territory; in Jordan itself, despite a 150 percent increase in cargo transshipment through the port of Aqaba and GDP growth of nearly 3 percent, discontent over the war and government repression were accompanied by a surge in extremism, manifested in a militant attack on a funeral procession outside a church in Karak province on October 23, where nine people were killed.

In Washington, the war became a source of acute political and budgetary tension. According to exit polls in the November 3 elections, more than seven out of ten voters who voted against Republicans named the war in Iran an important or decisive factor in their decision. On November 10, outgoing Republican Congressman Thomas Massie initiated a vote in the House of Representatives to impeach War Secretary Pete Hegseth, accusing him of unlawfully starting a war, ignoring a June congressional resolution on troop withdrawal, as well as actions in Venezuela, Yemen, and strikes on vessels in the Caribbean Sea. Hegseth—who this week also drew press attention by awarding a medal to a sentinel at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, Specialist Thomas Iglesias, after a viral video appeared in which the guard sharply demanded that the secretary and his entourage maintain silence on Veterans Day—escaped impeachment by a margin of just seven votes because 11 Democrats missed the session, some of them stuck in traffic returning from an Elect Democratic Women event. Meanwhile, as former Pentagon budget director Elaine McCusker notes in The Economist, military operations have led to a serious cash shortfall: even if Congress passes a continuing resolution to extend funding by December 11, the defense department will have $400 million per day less at its disposal than last year, and $1.7 billion per day less than provided for in the $1.5 trillion defense budget requested by Trump.

Trying to find a diplomatic exit from the conflict, Vice President JD Vance—who even before the start of the February campaign had advocated inside the administration for a diplomatic solution to the Iranian nuclear problem—jointly with special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner and with mediation by Qatar, established contact with the secretary of Iran's supreme security body, Mohsen Rezaei. The negotiations, preliminary rounds of which took place in Tehran and Doha before the elections, envisaged unblocking the Strait of Hormuz and limiting the Iranian nuclear program; as recently as Monday, November 9, after a White House meeting with Qatari Prime Minister Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, Trump stated that military operations against Iran were "effectively concluded," which contributed to a decline in oil quotes.

However, on Thursday, November 12, Trump publicly shut down his vice president's diplomatic initiative. In a post on Truth Social, the president stated that the United States "does not need a deal with Iran" because it already controls "hundreds of billions of dollars of their money and their strait," left the naval blockade in place, and instructed Vance to focus all his attention on investigating "corruption and crime in Democrat-run cities and states." While Washington Post columnist Marc Thiessen supported the rejection of talks, urging Trump to completely destroy Iranian military, energy, and transport infrastructure and achieve regime change following Ronald Reagan's Cold War strategy, experts and diplomats from Persian Gulf countries interviewed by the same publication—including UAE presidential adviser Anwar Gargash and head of the Middle East Council on Global Affairs in Qatar Khalid Al-Jaber—warned that maintaining the blockade without a peace treaty condemns the region and the global economy to protracted instability and high energy prices. On Trump's statements, Brent crude oil rose 3.3 percent on Thursday to $95.82 per barrel.

The continuation of the conflict is accompanied by an expansion of Iranian cyberattacks against civilian infrastructure in Western countries, covered in articles by The Wall Street Journal, The Economist, and The Washington Post. In mid-October 2026, the hacking group CyberAv3ngers, linked to the Shahid Kaveh unit of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps cyber command, carried out a series of intrusions into internet-connected programmable logic controllers (PLCs) of refrigeration systems in hundreds of supermarkets in France and nine other European countries. By remotely raising the temperature in refrigerators to 49 degrees Celsius (120 degrees Fahrenheit), the hackers spoiled tens of millions of euros worth of food and caused at least 15 fires, leaving traces in the Persian language in the software code. French Foreign Minister Jean-Noel Barrot called the attack an act of "unprecedented aggressiveness," while acting head of the US cybersecurity agency CISA Nicholas Andersen pointed to similar attacks against water treatment facilities in seven US states and the computer networks of two tankers heading to Texas (Iran's ambassador to France Mohammad Amin-Nejad denied Tehran's involvement). According to Shalev Hulio, head of the Israeli cybersecurity firm Dream, Iranian hackers have transitioned to using artificial intelligence models to automatically search for zero-day vulnerabilities and analyze released patches. In articles published in The Economist and The Wall Street Journal, former US Deputy National Security Adviser and Andreessen Horowitz partner Anne Neuberger notes that in October AI tools discovered a critical vulnerability hidden for years in a programming language powering about 70 percent of the world's websites, after which an information leak led to immediate attacks. Neuberger urges Congress to extend the 2015 Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act before December 11 and obligate key operators of power grids, water utilities, telecommunications, payment systems, and hospitals to use AI and digital twins for preemptive elimination of flaws in industrial equipment.

Simultaneously, the Iranian war accelerated a crisis in US-Israeli relations, examined in detail in publications by the Financial Times and The Economist. On election day, November 3, Donald Trump caught Israeli military representatives arriving in Washington to discuss extending the ten-year military aid memorandum (signed under Barack Obama for the period through 2028) off guard, stating in an interview that the United States would stop providing Israel with its annual $3.8 billion military subsidy at the end of this year. Trump explained that aid to Israel had become a losing issue in political campaigning and noted on CBS the emergence in the United States of strong dislike toward Israel, while Vice President JD Vance, backed by Donald Trump Jr., the day after the elections directly linked the Republican defeat to the war in Iran and the nature of relations with Israel.

As former US diplomat Dennis Ross, Democratic Senator Peter Welch, and former Israeli Prime Minister Ehud Olmert point out in conversations with the Financial Times, a perception has solidified in American society (where, according to polls, 60 to 62 percent of citizens negatively assess the policies of the Israeli government) that Benjamin Netanyahu bet exclusively on the Republicans and drew the United States into a war with Iran that led to the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and rising fuel prices. The largest pro-Israel lobby AIPAC, which spent about $70 million in the midterm elections through its United Democracy Project fund (half of this sum was directed to Michigan, where sharp critic of military aid to Israel Democrat Abdul El-Sayed won anyway), abandoned the practice of officially endorsing candidates two weeks before the vote. For the Israeli budget, the termination of American payments will create a $7 billion deficit over the next two years alongside commitments for another $10 billion after 2028 for already ordered weapons.

The cooling from Washington coincided with a political crisis inside Israel itself and a severe humanitarian situation in the Gaza Strip. After Netanyahu's coalition lost its majority in the Knesset elections on October 27 (and the government's legal adviser rejected attempts by the Likud party and the leadership of the Shin Bet security service to call into question the vote count in Arab districts), President Isaac Herzog on November 8 handed the mandate to form a government to former IDF Chief of Staff and leader of the centrist Yashar party Gadi Eisenkot. Eisenkot expects within the allotted six weeks to form an "emergency minority government" of four Jewish opposition parties controlling 48 of the 120 seats in parliament, with external support without formally including Arab or ultra-Orthodox parties in the cabinet, but his efforts are complicated by maneuvers from Yisrael Beiteinu party leader Avigdor Lieberman and calls from Netanyahu himself to create a national unity government under his former leadership.

In the Gaza Strip itself, where more than 1,600 people have been killed by Israeli strikes since the ceasefire agreement concluded with Trump's participation took effect in October 2025 (and the total number of Palestinians killed since October 2023 has exceeded 74,000–74,500), the situation deteriorated sharply. On the night of Thursday, November 12, the Israeli military without an evacuation warning struck the surgical wing of Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis; the target was named as Hamas Khan Younis Brigade commander Muhammad Abu Ismail, but the strike killed at least six people, including 52-year-old surgeon Dr. Rizk Abu Shaira and patients. During these same days, severe winter storms and rains flooded tent camps for displaced persons, affecting hundreds of thousands of people, and a landslide on a sandy slope south of Deir al-Balah collapsed onto tents, killing a woman and three children. The Board of Peace established by Trump in July, which prepared 66 Gaza reconstruction projects worth at least $250 million that remain blocked due to Netanyahu's refusal to allow the deployment of international forces or a new Palestinian police force for Hamas disarmament, issued public criticism of Israeli authorities, calling the volumes of winter shelter and humanitarian aid deliveries insufficient.

The Trump administration's drive to achieve quick foreign policy results before Congress transitions to Democratic control in January 2027 also shaped the course of negotiations surrounding Russia's war against Ukraine. On November 10 and 11 in Abu Dhabi, US special envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner held the first direct talks between Russian and Ukrainian representatives in nine months. The six-hour meeting ended only with agreement on a major prisoner-of-war exchange, while the head of the Russian delegation, Vladimir Medinsky, refused to meet with high-ranking representatives of France, Germany, the United Kingdom, and Italy who had arrived in the UAE capital. On Thursday, November 12, Witkoff and Kushner held talks with Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin, after which they traveled to Dublin for a meeting with Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky and European leaders gathered under the chairmanship of European Council President Antonio Costa.

A comparison of diplomatic reporting from the Financial Times, The Washington Post, The Wall Street Journal, and The Economist shows that although Witkoff and Kushner publicly stated in Moscow that Europeans would join the next round of talks, Washington and Moscow have effectively begun discussing a revision of the entire European security architecture. Speaking in Dublin, Volodymyr Zelensky warned allies that the talks between the United States and Russia now concern not only Ukraine, but also the creation of a new security framework between Europe and Russia. Russian presidential aide Yuri Ushakov confirmed Moscow's readiness to immediately begin negotiations with Washington on a "new architecture of indivisible security" and reported that Putin would continue discussing the Ukrainian issue with Trump at a scheduled meeting in China next week. The Kremlin's demands include NATO's renunciation of eastward expansion, the cessation of exercises and withdrawal of alliance troops and weapons from the eastern flank, as well as Ukraine's neutrality and a reduction of its army, whereas Kyiv insists on Western support to maintain a defensive army of at least 800,000 troops and security guarantees analogous to Article 5 of the NATO charter. Moreover, at the Kremlin meeting Putin categorically rejected the possibility of deploying European peacekeepers in Ukraine after peace is concluded, stating that any foreign contingents would become a "legitimate military target" for Russia and lead to an immediate resumption of war, and that revising European security must be discussed before hostilities cease. Antonio Costa and Estonian Prime Minister Kristen Michal rejected the Russian demands, with Michal characterizing the attempt to condition Europe's admission to the negotiating table on agreeing to discuss NATO's security architecture as a dangerous "trap."

However, the most telling evidence of the methods used to exert pressure on Kyiv during this diplomatic round came in reports by the Financial Times and The Wall Street Journal regarding technical communications restrictions and informal fuel deals. On Thursday, November 12, Volodymyr Zelensky stated that Ukrainian troops on the front line had for several days been facing "systematic speed restrictions" on Starshield satellite communications terminals—the military service of Elon Musk's SpaceX funded by the Pentagon. The operating speed of terminals over the front line was capped at a threshold of 161 kilometers per hour (100 miles per hour). Because Starshield terminals are mounted on drones, this restriction seriously hampered the interception of high-speed Russian jet-powered drones (according to the Institute for the Study of War, during a similar restriction last month the share of downed jet drones dropped from 77 percent to 74 percent) and, according to a military source for The Wall Street Journal, virtually halted strikes by medium-range Ukrainian drones against Russian logistics on one sector of the eastern front. Senior Ukrainian officials told the Financial Times that they view the introduction of the speed limit as an attempt to put pressure on Kyiv and strengthen Moscow's negotiating position during the American envoys' visit to the Kremlin.

In parallel, a Financial Times investigation uncovered the consequences of a fuel deal between Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin. Seeking to lower global and domestic diesel prices ahead of the midterm elections, Trump on October 9 announced the lifting of US sanctions on Russian fuel exports and simultaneously pressured Ukraine, forcing Kyiv to suspend drone strikes on Russian oil refineries for two weeks (strikes resumed only on October 30). According to analytics company Kpler, this pause allowed Russian refineries to quickly restore production and double seaborne diesel exports from 135,000 barrels per day in September to 271,000 barrels per day in October 2026. Yet not a single shipment of this fuel ever reached the United States: after New Jersey Governor Mikie Sherrill threatened tough sanctions against port terminal operators in New York Harbor for accepting Russian cargoes, two tankers changed course, the tanker Agioi Fanendes remained adrift off the US East Coast awaiting a buyer, and the main flow of Russian diesel went to buyers in Brazil and Turkey.

At the same time, an analysis of the situation at the front and in the economy presented by The Wall Street Journal and The Economist explains why the Kremlin is in no hurry to make compromises. On the front line, 2026 has been the most unsuccessful year for Russian troops since 2022: attempts to encircle Ukrainian fortified areas in the Donetsk region failed, and the number of Russian soldiers killed since the start of the full-scale war, according to Rosstat data obtained by hackers linked to Ukraine and the Belarusian opposition, exceeded 450,000 (taking into account unrecorded losses since mid-2025, analysts estimate the total number of dead at nearly 600,000). Nevertheless, Putin counts on turning the tide by the spring of 2027 through two factors. The first is the destruction of Ukrainian energy and urban infrastructure: Ukraine enters the winter with the most severe damage to its power grid of the entire conflict and an acute shortage of interceptor missiles for Patriot systems, whereas Russia holds a stockpile of 700 ballistic missiles and produces about 110 such missiles per month.

The Kremlin's second calculation is tied to the budgetary and political crisis in European countries, which after the termination of direct American military and financial aid became Ukraine's main donors. As an analysis by The Economist shows, the European model of social protection—which accounts for 50 percent of global social spending with 7 percent of the planet's population and 25 percent of global GDP—has run into an acute shortage of funds amid the rising cost of servicing public debt. Ten EU countries, including France, Italy, and Belgium, fell under the European Union's excessive deficit procedure. In Belgium, where the budget deficit in 2026 reached 5 percent of GDP with public debt at 112 percent of GDP, Prime Minister Bart De Wever submitted his resignation on November 6 due to the inability of the five-party coalition to agree on a budget for 2027. In the Netherlands, as The Washington Post reports, the first minority government since World War II, led by centrist Rob Jetten, found itself on the verge of collapse just nine months after formation when the coalition member right-wing People's Party for Freedom and Democracy (VVD), led by Defense Minister Dilan Yesilgoz-Zegerius and Finance Minister Eelco Heinen, opposed a tax deal between their partners and the left-wing opposition, warning of a budget hole of more than $442 million. In Germany, Chancellor Friedrich Merz stated that the current scale of the welfare state (having reached 30 percent of GDP with social contributions at 42 percent of gross wages) is no longer affordable for the country given the need to reduce the budget deficit by more than 100 billion euros by 2030. In Italy, whose public debt exceeded 138 percent of GDP, an attempt by Giorgia Meloni's cabinet to introduce a 26 percent tax on unrealized capital gains for wealthy residents leaving the country and to extract an additional 7 billion euros from banks over two years (by raising the corporate tax on bank profits from 27.5 to 30.5 percent and spreading the use of 20 billion euros in tax credits over ten years) provoked a harsh response from the financial sector: the head of the country's largest bank, Intesa Sanpaolo, Carlo Messina, at a Senate banking committee hearing on November 12 compared these measures to South American practices and warned that Italian banks might reduce their investments in national public debt. In Spain, Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez's Socialist government, trailing the right-wing People's Party and Vox in polls ahead of the November 29 general election, faced the first nationwide union strike in 14 years over housing costs and the severe political consequences of two derailments on the Madrid–Seville high-speed rail line in January and October 2026, in which a total of 115 people died following the 170th failure of the year of a computerized train control system operating since 1992.

An especially tense situation has developed in France, where interest payments on public debt will grow from 79 billion euros in 2026 to 91 billion euros ($102 billion) in 2027, and the fourth prime minister in three years, Sebastien Lecornu, is trying to push through parliament a draft budget with 43 billion euros in savings (including a nominal freeze on most pensions and civil servants' salaries) to reduce the deficit from 5.4 to 5 percent of GDP. Amid street protests, the front-runner in polls ahead of the spring 2027 presidential election remains Marine Le Pen (polling at about 35 percent in the first round and promising to wind down funding for Ukraine), followed by left-wing leader Jean-Luc Melenchon (about 17 percent), while on November 9 multiple Olympic judo champion Teddy Riner unexpectedly entered the presidential race, capable, according to pollsters, of attracting the votes of up to 22 percent of voters. It is precisely on Le Pen's possible rise to power in France and the strengthening of the Alternative for Germany party, which advocates resuming purchases of Russian gas, that Moscow is counting in its strategy of prolonging the war.

In response to these risks, European countries are trying to accelerate the development of their own defense technologies and retain the American military presence while simultaneously countering intelligence and cyber operations. As The Wall Street Journal and The Economist report, in the United Kingdom near Gloucester, as a result of an operational sting by British intelligence services, 41-year-old US Army Lieutenant Colonel Brandon Tyner, who served in the personnel division of the headquarters of NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps, was arrested on suspicion of espionage for Russia (meanwhile, American military aircraft had earlier been withdrawn from the British bases of Mildenhall and Fairford amid threats from Iranian operatives). In Germany, according to the Financial Times, the Bundeswehr temporarily suspended the operation of drones from leading defense startup Quantum Systems (valued this year at 7 billion euros and having more than 200 employees and three joint ventures in Ukraine) after discovering a cyberattack in late October during which attackers distributed a malicious file through the update server of the QBase Tactical mission planning system. Seeking to reduce Europe's dependence on American military systems and Palantir, another German defense startup—Munich-based Helsing, valued at $18 billion—this week in Berlin unveiled its own AI reconnaissance and target selection platform Clarity, developed under the leadership of former Palantir engineer Robert Fink and tested in Ukraine, while British defense concern BAE Systems, amid an increase in its order backlog to 33 billion pounds, raised its free cash flow forecast for the second time this year to 3 billion pounds. On NATO's eastern flank, Romanian President Nicusor Dan in an interview with the Financial Times reported that after the reduction of US troop numbers in the country from 1,700 to 1,172, Bucharest in talks with US Under Secretary of Defense Elbridge Colby offered Washington to share the costs of maintaining the American contingent (following the example of Poland, which offered in September to pay $15,000 per year for each American soldier at the planned permanent base "Fort Trump"), recalling that Romania, amid Russian drones falling on its territory and commercial vessels being hit in the Black Sea, allowed the US Air Force to use the Mihail Kogalniceanu air base for operations against Iran when other EU capitals refused. Meanwhile, in the United Kingdom itself, new Labour Party Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who replaced Keir Starmer, ahead of a summit with the EU on November 20 openly raised the question of the country's possible return to the European Union and protection of freedom of movement for the first time since 2020 amid forecasts that net migration to the UK for 2026 could turn negative for the first time since 1993 with a net outflow of 54,000 people, whereas Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch and Nigel Farage's Reform UK party demand withdrawal from the European Convention on Human Rights, which threatens to collapse basic agreements with Brussels on trade and the status of Northern Ireland.

MULTIPOLAR ADAPTATION & SYSTEMIC HORIZON

GLOBAL SOUTH • TRANSACTIONAL ORDER
05

PARADOXES OF MULTIPOLAR ADAPTATION AND THE SYSTEMIC HORIZON OF TRANSACTIONAL UNCERTAINTY

While the United States and Europe are focused on domestic political reshuffles and regional wars, China is preparing to host the leaders of nearly twenty states, including Donald Trump and Vladimir Putin, at the APEC summit in Shenzhen next week. The totality of materials across four publications shows that Beijing approaches the summit in a dual position: the party leadership's concern over internal socioeconomic contradictions is combined with a powerful export surge, technological import substitution, and the creation of an independent financial and logistical infrastructure.

On the domestic front, the Chinese Communist Party leadership notes the growth of ideological and social challenges: on November 3, the People's Daily published an article by the party's chief ideologue and fourth member of the Politburo Standing Committee Wang Huning explaining the concept of "three even more formidable challenges" put forward by Xi Jinping at the October Central Committee plenum. Wang Huning directly warned of the danger that the party could fall victim to historical changes due to inequality between regions and sectors, population aging and decline, the social consequences of introducing artificial intelligence, and the sprawl of the gig economy, which already encompasses 320 million people outside traditional mechanisms of party control, in connection with which the Central Committee demanded stronger party supervision over technology companies. In the economy, the consequences of the downturn in the real estate market (where up to 80 percent of Chinese household wealth was held at the 2021 peak) and weak consumer demand (retail sales growth in September was just 1.1 percent year-over-year) led households in 2025 to direct at least 100 billion yuan ($15 billion) of savings toward opening hundreds of thousands of budget food service and tea shop franchise outlets (such as the chains Mixue, Busy Ming, Cotti, and Yangguofu), whose business model is built not on collecting royalties, but on selling raw materials and equipment to franchisees at minimal margins. Simultaneously, to replenish the budget, Chinese authorities launched a tough tax campaign against wealthy citizens: as the Financial Times reports, after setting an October 22 deadline for declaring foreign assets and introducing a 20 percent tax on offshore trust income (with audits of transactions going back to 2020), China's State Taxation Administration in October alone collected more than 120 billion yuan ($18 billion) from residents' offshore trusts, increasing total income tax receipts by 90 percent year-over-year.

In the foreign economic sphere, Chinese industry, by contrast, demonstrates rapid expansion. In October 2026, the dollar value of China's merchandise exports grew 35 percent compared with last year thanks to large-scale shipments of semiconductors and artificial intelligence-related products, while China's cumulative year-to-date trade surplus exceeded $1 trillion back in October—a month ahead of last year's schedule. Despite American restrictions, China's largest contract chipmaker SMIC, according to The Wall Street Journal, more than doubled its net profit in the third quarter to $428.4 million on record quarterly revenue of $3.19 billion (up 34 percent): because leading global foundries led by TSMC concentrated capacity on producing advanced AI processors (allowing Taiwanese server assembler Foxconn to increase quarterly net profit by 34 percent to $2.36 billion), a shortage of mature-node chips for power management systems and peripheral data center nodes emerged on the global market, thanks to which SMIC raised its gross margin to 29.9 percent and increased its share of the global contract manufacturing market to 5.4 percent, approaching Samsung Electronics (5.9 percent).

Moreover, as The Wall Street Journal found out, Beijing has begun preparations to create its own alternative to the Western marine insurance market led by Lloyd's of London. Taking into account the experience of Western sanctions against Russian oil shipments and the jump in war-risk insurance costs due to hostilities in Iran (where insurance for a single passage of a supertanker carrying two million barrels of oil through the Strait of Hormuz reaches $17 million versus $250,000 in safe areas), Chinese maritime authorities are holding talks with state companies China Re, PICC, the Hong Kong war risk insurance pool, and banks on forming a $15 billion insurance and reinsurance pool by 2029 with dollar settlements conducted through Hong Kong banks. Major Western shipowners, including the head of Greece's Capital Group Evangelos Marinakis, have already stated their readiness to insure vessels in the Chinese center provided rates are competitive. Simultaneously, Beijing is strictly using its own monopoly on critical raw materials: amid a diplomatic conflict with Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi over her statements regarding a possible forceful response by Tokyo in the event of an attack on Taiwan, China during the first nine months of 2026 cut exports to Japan of yttrium oxide—a rare-earth component essential for manufacturing jet engines and semiconductors—by 98 percent, excluding Japan from a temporary agreement on rare-earth metals reached with the United States. In turn, Washington and its Pacific allies are building up military and industrial integration: as a report by The Economist from Western Australia and an article by South Korea's ambassador to the United States Kang Kyung-wha in The Washington Post show, within the framework of the AUKUS alliance Australia and the United States are investing $5.6 billion in upgrading the HMAS Stirling base south of Perth, where starting next year—for the first time outside the United States in 53 years—four American Virginia-class nuclear submarines will begin basing (even though meeting obligations to sell submarines to Australia will require American shipyards to increase vessel delivery rates from 1.2 to 2.3 units per year by 2031, while South Korea under an agreed $350 billion investment package is directing $150 billion into American shipbuilding and $200 billion into US energy and high technology, including a 6.47-gigawatt gas power plant project in Texas for $22.3 billion and a program to build eight nuclear reactors worth up to $120 billion).

However, the largest unintended consequence of geopolitical conflicts and Chinese industrial strategy has been a turning point in the dynamics of global greenhouse gas emissions—a process in whose analysis data from The Economist and reports by the Financial Times from the UN COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey fit into a single picture. According to estimates by the Global Carbon Budget consortium and the Finnish research center CREA, global carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels in 2026 will decline by 0.4 percent (after average annual growth of 0.8 percent over the previous seven years), and the main factor behind this decline was a 3.1 percent year-over-year reduction in carbon dioxide emissions in China in the third quarter of 2026—the largest quarterly drop since the pandemic, whose volume equals the entire annual emissions of the United Kingdom.

As an analysis by The Economist shows, this reduction resulted not from diplomatic declarations in Antalya, but from a combination of Chinese investments in electrification and the energy shock from the US and Israeli war against Iran. In China itself, the share of coal-fired plants in electricity generation fell below 50 percent for the first time in the first half of 2026 thanks to the commissioning since 2022 of 1,000 gigawatts of solar and more than 300 gigawatts of wind capacity (although the transition to market pricing for solar plants since June of last year and grid constraints led to a more than twofold year-over-year drop in new solar capacity additions in the first nine months of 2026), while the share of electric vehicles and plug-in hybrids in September reached two-thirds of passenger car sales and half of heavy truck sales, causing state corporation Sinopec to forecast a nearly 4 percent decline in oil consumption in China in 2026 for the third consecutive year. Outside China, the blockade of the Strait of Hormuz and the jump in Middle Eastern oil and gas prices sparked explosive demand for cheap Chinese energy equipment in developing countries: in the first nine months of 2026, exports of Chinese solar panels and components to Southeast Asia grew by nearly 60 percent in capacity terms (whereas shipments to Europe fell by 11 percent), and the Philippines in May overtook Pakistan to become the world's second-largest buyer of Chinese solar panels. Simultaneously, Africa in 2026 is installing a record 17 gigawatts of solar capacity (about 100,000 panels per day at a retail price of up to 20 cents per watt), and in countries such as Zimbabwe, South Africa, and Namibia, about 75 to 80 percent of new installations account for private companies and households moving away from unreliable state power grids.

At the COP31 conference in Antalya itself, where nearly 93,000 participants registered, as the Financial Times reports, the agenda is being set by power grid and energy companies (National Grid, Octopus Energy, SSE, Schneider Electric) promoting the "35 by 35" target benchmark—increasing the share of electricity in global final energy consumption from the current 23 percent to 35 percent by 2035. Meanwhile, the heads of the largest oil and gas corporations—Darren Woods of ExxonMobil and Patrick Pouyanne of TotalEnergies—did not attend the summit, and a Financial Times editorial draws attention to the fact that due to resistance from oil-producing states, the "35 by 35" draft contains no binding quotas on the share of renewable energy sources amid the weakening in the EU of the ban on selling internal combustion engine cars after 2035 and UK plans to lower its electric vehicle sales quota for 2030 from 80 to 60 percent. Moreover, according to a closed European Commission memorandum seen by the Financial Times, ExxonMobil Corporation in October through subsidiaries in Belgium, Luxembourg, and the United Kingdom filed a confidential arbitration claim against the EU demanding a suspension of obligations for oil and gas companies to create carbon dioxide storage infrastructure under the European Net-Zero Industry Act, invoking the twenty-year sunset clause of the 1994 Energy Charter Treaty, from which the EU and the UK withdrew last year.

Finally, this week's events demonstrate acute sociopolitical contradictions in the countries of Latin America and Southeast Asia, where direct state intervention in managing resources and financial institutions—a trend that The Economist, in a review of a book by Cornell University historian Nicholas Mulder, calls the return of the "confiscatory state"—is generating unexpected crises. In Venezuela, ten months after American commandos captured President Nicolas Maduro on January 3, 2026 and brought him to the United States on drug trafficking charges, a US State Department report published on November 12 (analyzed by The Wall Street Journal and The Washington Post) revealed a striking discrepancy between Donald Trump's statements about multibillion-dollar US revenues from Venezuelan oil and the actual movement of funds. Of the $12.26 billion that entered a US-controlled account at the US Treasury between January and September 30 from the sale of Venezuelan oil, gold, minerals, and fertilizers, more than 98 percent of the funds were returned to Venezuela's economy through foreign exchange operations of the local central bank (only $168.56 million remained at the disposal of US authorities at the end of September). Inside Venezuela itself, however, as The Washington Post describes, redirecting electricity to the needs of recovering oil production led to daily power outages of 8 to 16 hours and water disruptions, which sparked more than 1,100 protest actions in September (an average of 37 per day—the highest for September since 2008), in response to which interim President Delcy Rodriguez announced an early start to the Christmas season from November 1, ordering festive illuminations lit in blacked-out cities. The consequences of the Venezuelan redistribution also affected Trump's own former circle: according to The Wall Street Journal, the president's former personal lawyer Alina Mildon (Habba) and her husband, Turkish entrepreneur Turhan Mildon—whose Maduro-era Venezuelan cement and shrimp assets came under scrutiny at the insistence of the US administration, while Habba's own $50 million contract with the family of a pardoned Venezuelan billionaire drew sharp criticism from Trump—twice failed in recent months to provide funds to close deals on purchasing estates in Palm Beach County, Florida worth $65 million and $72.5 million, losing their initial deposits.

A turnaround closely linked to the Venezuelan crisis occurred in Cuba. As a Wall Street Journal investigation showed, after the cessation of Venezuelan oil shipments and Trump's introduction in January 2026 of an energy blockade of the island (since the start of which only one Russian tanker has entered Cuban ports), a February US Treasury Department authorization for fuel exports to the Cuban private sector turned the United States into the island's main fuel supplier. Through South Florida companies, more than 1.6 million barrels of gasoline and diesel worth over $230 million have already been shipped from American ports to Cuba in small container tanks, keeping private trucks and home generators running amid the energy crisis, although part of this fuel inevitably ends up at gas stations of the state company CUPET.

At the same time, in Indonesia and Paraguay, a split in ruling coalitions led to a direct political crisis. In Indonesia, on November 11–12, 39-year-old Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka—the eldest son of former President Joko Widodo (Jokowi)—resigned after the country's Supreme Court ruled on October 30 that he had entered university without the high school diploma required by law. While a note in The Wall Street Journal cites only Jokowi's restrained words about the normality of political dynamics and continued support for Prabowo Subianto's cabinet, materials in The Economist and the Financial Times reveal the depth of a conflict threatening a forceful confrontation between the army and the police. A week before Gibran's resignation, on November 3, General Listyo Sigit Prabowo, who had just stepped down as head of the national police and was a longtime associate of Jokowi dating back to his work in the municipal administration of Solo, was arrested on charges of illegally wiretapping President Prabowo Subianto's telephone. Because the police traditionally relied on Jokowi's patronage while the army is loyal to former special forces general Prabowo Subianto, on the evening of the arrest the president urgently deployed between 20,000 and 21,000 troops into Jakarta. The political conflict, amid a drop in the president's approval rating from 81.2 to 44.5 percent and investor doubts about his economic policy, hit financial markets: Indonesia's stock index became the worst in the world in 2026 (falling 39.3 percent in dollar terms amid warnings from the MSCI agency), and the rupiah exchange rate on November 12 dropped to a historic low of 18,269 rupiah per dollar. And in Paraguay, where a record 50,000 foreigners applied for residency in the first nine months of the year, regulators' seizure on November 3 of the fast-growing digital bank Ueno (where accounts of 3 million out of the country's 7 million residents, $850 million in state funds, and $520 million in pension funds were blocked) led to the rapid impeachment and house arrest of President Santiago Pena and Central Bank head Carlos Carvallo (former chairman of Ueno), after which power passed to new President Pedro Alliana and a cabinet formed from supporters of former head of state and Colorado Party leader Horacio Cartes. (In neighboring Colombia, new right-wing populist President Abelardo de la Espriella, nicknamed "The Tiger," who faced a devastating earthquake three days after his inauguration that claimed more than 300 lives and caused over $13 billion in damage, amid a budget deficit projected at 9.4 percent of GDP in 2027, is negotiating a $20 billion loan with the IMF and trying to push a tough tax reform through Congress ahead of a nationwide strike called by the left-wing opposition for November 19).

The events of mid-November 2026, brought together into a single analytical perspective, record the moment when accumulated imbalances of the technology boom, debt markets, and transactional geopolitics simultaneously passed into a phase of open correction. In an economy where the ten largest companies in the artificial intelligence sector account for nearly 40 percent of the S&P 500 index's capitalization and the share of corporate equities in the net wealth of American families has reached a record 38 percent (exceeding the dot-com era peak by a quarter), the personnel catastrophe atop Nvidia and questionable chip supply routes through Southeast Asia reverberate instantaneously both in Wall Street portfolios and in the offices of national security committees.

The key pattern uniting these processes is that the world's leading powers and corporate monopolies have turned the very uncertainty of rules into an instrument for managing dependencies. In the United States, the Donald Trump administration uses the unpredictability of trade tariffs, a selective approach to export controls (where bans on shipments of advanced semiconductors alternate with individual permits in exchange for 25 percent budget remittances, while the number of enforcement actions by the Securities and Exchange Commission under Paul Atkins against public corporations fell to a sixteen-year low of 16 cases per year amid the departure of nearly 20 percent of the agency's staff), and abrupt shifts of course in military conflicts as a way to keep trading partners, allies, and business in constant dependence on White House decisions. In China, the Communist Party leadership mirrors this by applying politicized control over rare-earth metal exports, rapidly building autonomous circuits for chip production and marine insurance, and strengthening party supervision over the economy, promoting at external venues like the APEC summit in Shenzhen the concept of a Sinocentric order under the slogan of a "community with a shared future for mankind."

However, the November turning point of 2026 marked strict objective limits to this model. Inside the United States, voter discontent over inflation, the protracted war in Iran, and the unchecked growth of energy-intensive data centers led to the Republicans losing both chambers of Congress and a majority of governorships, which starting in January 2027 launches a wave of congressional investigations into deals between the executive branch and technology corporations. On global capital markets, the artificial intelligence industry's need for trillions in borrowing ran into strict risk concentration limits in bond portfolios, the high cost of computing, and the return of inflationary pressure keeping Federal Reserve interest rates and mortgages at multi-year highs. As a result, both technology giants, having lost their chief lobbyist in the person of Jensen Huang, and political leaders in Washington, European capitals, and Beijing will have to operate in a reality where previous mechanisms of circular subsidization and unilateral coercion are beginning to experience systemic failures.
