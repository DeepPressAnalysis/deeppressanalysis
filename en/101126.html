GLOBAL CAPITAL FLOWS, INFRASTRUCTURE BOTTLENECKS, AND AI REPRICING

As of November 9–10, 2026, global capital markets are repricing three interlocking structural shifts: the sudden imposition of state-level political constraints on U.S. artificial intelligence infrastructure, a de-escalation in U.S.-Iran military rhetoric that has broken the war premium in crude oil prices, and acute fiscal and supply-chain attrition across the European and Eurasian theaters. Across these developments, capital is rotating out of highly leveraged, single-theme compute hardware and physical goods retail into vertically integrated technology operators, regulated energy and grid infrastructure, European cross-border banking consolidators, and experiential services. While equity indices rebounded on Monday, November 9—with the S&P 500 rising 2.31% to 7,547.27 and the Nasdaq 100 gaining 3.3% by midday as Brent crude tumbled to a 10-week intraday low of $92.80 per barrel—underlying credit and equity markets are actively penalizing balance sheets with concentrated key-person governance, unhedged debt leverage, or exposure to local utility ratepayer backlashes.

Beneath the headline volatility, the primary economic mechanism at work is the forced internalization of previously externalized costs. Technology hyperscalers and independent power producers can no longer treat municipal water supplies and regional electric grids as frictionless public goods after the November 2026 U.S. midterm elections placed roughly 80% of the national data-center development pipeline—according to BCA Research estimates—under Democratic state governors, many of whom campaigned on construction moratoriums. Simultaneously, in sovereign finance and international trade, dominant actors—Germany in European Union budget negotiations, the newly elected U.S. Senate majority overseeing the Federal Reserve and defense appropriations, and resource-producing states in Africa—are using institutional veto points to extract fiscal burden-sharing, block monetary politicization, and capture a larger share of commodity value chains.

The November 3, 2026 U.S. midterm elections transferred executive and legislative control of key U.S. states to Democratic governors and lawmakers who campaigned on pausing or restricting artificial-intelligence data-center construction. Specifically, Democrats flipped a dozen legislative chambers, increasing their state legislative trifectas (control of both legislative chambers and the governorship) from 16 to 22 states while reducing Republican trifectas from 23 to 17, and captured the governorship and state House in Texas alongside statehouse gains in Georgia, Ohio, and Michigan. Consequently, analysts at BCA Research estimate that roughly 80% of the U.S. data-center development pipeline is now located in states governed by Democrats, up from approximately one-third prior to the election. Because monthly U.S. data-center construction spending had surged nearly 80% since the start of the year (according to the Commerce Department) while straining municipal water supplies and local electric grids, the prospect of state-level permitting moratoriums forced an immediate market reassessment of AI compute capacity growth and capital expenditure timelines for 2027, with tech investor Brad Gerstner estimating that up to 35% of planned 2027 U.S. data-center construction is now at risk.

Equity and credit markets aggressively repriced hardware suppliers, independent power producers, and leveraged technology investors. On Thursday, November 5, a concentrated AI stock selloff erased a record $2.5 trillion in market value from the Nasdaq Composite. Over a two-day post-election window, memory-chip maker Micron Technology plunged 23%, neocloud data-center operator CoreWeave fell 19%, and chipmakers Nvidia and Broadcom each dropped 12% (with Nvidia, Broadcom, and Dell Technologies falling 9.3%, 10%, and 15% on Thursday alone), dragging the PHLX Semiconductor Index down 12.15% for the week. Although the Nasdaq 100 rebounded 3.3% by midday on Monday, November 9 on lower oil prices and bargain hunting, the structural cost of capital for unbuilt compute projects has risen.

In the power generation sector, Vistra—the largest electricity generator in Texas—saw its shares fall 19% between Tuesday and Thursday before a 10% partial recovery on Friday. Vistra's forward earnings multiple has compressed by about 44% year-over-year to 12.5 times earnings, compounded by a more than 20% year-to-date decline in Texas wholesale forward power prices (according to a Jefferies report) following an earlier data-center permitting pause by outgoing Texas Governor Greg Abbott.

The repricing is most acute for leveraged balance sheets. Shares of Japanese conglomerate SoftBank Group—which is heavily exposed to Texas data-center projects and has committed $65 billion to OpenAI—have fallen approximately 60% since June, matching a 60% decline in its core chip-design holding, Arm Holdings. Analysts estimate that falling asset values have pushed SoftBank's loan-to-value ratio (the proportion of its net debt relative to the market value of its investment holdings) from 15% at the end of June to between 18% and 22%, approaching its 25% ceiling. SoftBank's five-year credit default swaps (the annual insurance cost against bond default) widened sharply following its record $11 billion junk bond issuance in September, and people familiar with the matter indicated that the planned listing of its U.S. energy infrastructure subsidiary, SB Energy, at a $50 billion valuation risks being put on hold. Meanwhile, wealth management clients holding concentrated AI positions—where the top 10 stocks account for roughly 40% of the S&P 500's value and Nvidia alone represents ~7%—are accelerating allocations into options-based diversification and tax-loss harvesting vehicles; assets under management at BlackRock's SpiderRock Advisors have quadrupled since May 2024 to over $16 billion.

Vertically integrated technology giants (Alphabet, Amazon, SpaceX/Tesla) that self-fund capital expenditures from internal cash flow and control their own chips, power agreements, and software applications gain a competitive moat as external debt financing tightens for smaller rivals. Application-layer data analytics firms (Relx, Sage, Experian, London Stock Exchange Group) previously sold off as "AI losers" rebounded sharply—helping UK-based Finsbury Growth & Income Trust post a nearly 13% net asset value gain in October versus 1.1% for its benchmark—as investors rotated into businesses deploying AI tools to grow customer revenue. U.S. residential home builders (D.R. Horton, PulteGroup, Lennar) saw shares jump 8.1%, 7.2%, and 7.1% respectively on Wednesday on expectations that slower data-center construction will ease inflationary pressures and bring down 30-year fixed mortgage rates from last month's 7.4% peak. Overseas data-center jurisdictions and Chinese AI developers also stand to benefit as hyperscalers consider moving greenfield capital expenditures abroad and as a U.S. construction slowdown lowers global component costs. Conversely, non-integrated AI frontier labs and neoclouds (OpenAI, Anthropic, CoreWeave, Nscale, SB Energy) reliant on external equity/debt markets and third-party infrastructure face higher financing costs and compute bottlenecks; OpenAI senior leaders have already begun internal discussions on resetting 2027 data-center capacity expectations. Independent merchant power producers (Vistra, NRG Energy, Constellation Energy) face depressed wholesale forward power prices and stricter grid-interconnection standards rather than unconstrained demand premiums. High-yield credit investors and margin lenders—including holders of SoftBank's $11 billion junk debt and banks lending against pledged technology shares (where some 80% of SoftBank's Arm stake may be pledged as collateral, though only ~10% of available borrowing was drawn as of June)—absorb the credit downside if AI equity valuations fall further. Concentrated retail and high-net-worth portfolios at firms like Latigo Peak Partners ($18.6 billion AUM), where longtime clients hold nearly 20% in a single stock (Nvidia, Broadcom, Dell), carry roughly four times the volatility of a diversified benchmark. Hyperscale data-center developers historically externalized peak grid upgrade costs and municipal water depletion onto local residential utility ratepayers—a cost transfer that state governments and voters are now moving to block.

State-level electoral politics is functioning as a physical bottleneck on capital deployment. Voters in Texas, Georgia, Ohio, and Michigan penalized incumbent politicians over localized electricity price inflation and municipal water scarcity tied to AI infrastructure. However, conversations between hyperscaler government-relations executives (such as Meta Platforms President Dina Powell McCormick) and incoming state transition teams indicate that newly elected governors do not intend to permanently forfeit billion-dollar tax and construction inflows. Instead, state executives are positioned to use moratorium threats as bargaining leverage to extract financial concessions—such as requiring developers to meet higher grid-connection standards, compensate local communities, or expand existing brownfield sites rather than greenfield builds. This event represents a collision between industrial policy, energy realignment, and physical infrastructure limits. For three years, equity markets priced AI growth as a frictionless software scaling law. In reality, frontier AI is a heavy-industrial sector constrained by electrical trade labor, water cooling, and local utility rate regulation. Furthermore, the divergence between cash-rich hyperscalers and debt-dependent challengers highlights a classic cycle of vertical integration and market consolidation during a capital-tightening phase, even as political shifts raise the longer-term prospect of antitrust scrutiny against vertically integrated tech empires.

Meta and OpenAI are evaluating shifting new greenfield data-center construction outside the U.S. or into arid desert regions, while expanding capacity primarily at existing U.S. data-center sites where surrounding communities already receive job and tax benefits. In capital markets, if Anthropic's IPO roadshow (launching this week at a targeted $1.5 trillion valuation to raise up to $100 billion) or SB Energy's $50 billion listing falters due to infrastructure delays, OpenAI's upcoming funding round (targeting a $1.25 trillion valuation) and delayed 2027 IPO will face heightened valuation pressure. Finally, HSBC Chief Asia Economist Frederic Neumann noted that while a U.S. data-center slowdown creates a near-term export headwind for Asian electronics suppliers, it simultaneously opens component capacity and lowers data-center hardware costs for build-outs in China and the rest of Asia. The U.S. AI boom has hit a state-level regulatory and utility-rate wall that is transferring pricing power from compute-hungry startups and merchant power producers to state regulators and self-funded tech conglomerates.

On Sunday, November 8, Nvidia's board of directors opened a formal internal investigation—expected to be led by independent director and former Cooley LLP CEO Stephen C. Neal alongside outside legal counsel—into reports that CEO Jensen Huang was the target of a foreign espionage operation after Huang admitted to an extramarital affair with Alice Huang (Huang Le). U.S., Singaporean, and Taiwanese intelligence officials suspect Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based data-center firm Megaspeed International, of sharing information with China's Ministry of State Security (MSS) while pitching Chinese investors on Southeast Asian data-center projects utilizing Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs).

On Monday, November 9, Nvidia shares fell as much as 8% in early trading—wiping out nearly $350 billion in market value at one point—before closing down 5.7% to 6.2% even as the broader Nasdaq 100 rallied 3.3%. Having already ceded its position as the world's most valuable company to Apple in late October, Nvidia ($4.5 trillion market capitalization, representing almost 7% of the S&P 500) acted as a major drag on passive index portfolios. Investors immediately rotated capital into competing AI chip designers to hedge Nvidia's governance risk, sending shares of Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) up 12.6% and Cerebras Systems up 5% on Monday. The probe also jeopardizes hundreds of millions of dollars in Southeast Asian cloud financing: in early 2025, Alice Huang and business partner Kang Wei sought several hundred million dollars from China-based investors for a Southeast Asian data-center project anchored by Chinese tech giant Alibaba (which lent funds to Megaspeed's Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix via Singaporean subsidiary Apex Enterprise Solutions in June 2024), explicitly boasting to prospective investors that they had "no problem securing GPUs" from Nvidia.

Rival semiconductor designers (AMD, Cerebras) and custom-silicon hyperscalers (Alphabet, Amazon) capture equity inflows and customer diversification interest as enterprise buyers seek to reduce single-vendor dependency on Nvidia. U.S. national security hawks and export-control regulators gain decisive political leverage to override industry lobbying and tighten restrictions on overseas AI chip access. Meanwhile, Nvidia shareholders and CEO Jensen Huang (who owns a 3.6% stake worth >$150 billion, received $36.3 million in total compensation last year including ~$4 million for personal security, and holds no special super-voting shares) face severe reputational damage, loss of lobbying influence in Washington, and fiduciary duty scrutiny under Delaware corporate law. Southeast Asian "neocloud" intermediaries (Megaspeed International) and Chinese cloud renters (Alibaba) face heightened risk of being placed on the U.S. Commerce Department's Entity List, which would legally prohibit U.S. companies from supplying them. AI startups backstopped by Nvidia and passive S&P 500 index investors carry the systemic downside; Nvidia has provided financial backstops, loan guarantees, and equity investments running into hundreds of billions of dollars across dozens of AI startups and data-center providers. Nvidia's board of directors—by operating as the only S&P 100 company with no designated board chair (per a 2025 Fenwick report), maintaining an average director tenure of 18 years (more than twice the S&P 500 mean according to Spencer Stuart, with five directors serving ~30 years), disclosing no formal CEO succession plan, and allowing 60+ executives to report directly to Huang—externalized extreme "key-person" single-point-of-failure risk onto public shareholders.

Until this week, Jensen Huang leveraged a close personal relationship with President Trump—accompanying him on state visits to China and the UK—to lobby for permissive export rules allowing Nvidia to sell older-generation AI chips to China and supply Southeast Asian data centers. Although Trump defended Huang on Monday as an "honourable guy," the scandal neutralizes Huang's lobbying influence. Republican House China Committee Chair John Moolenaar, three House Republicans, and Democratic Senator Chris Coons (a leading candidate to chair the Senate Foreign Relations Committee in January 2027) have all called for congressional briefings or investigations. Under current U.S. export controls, Chinese firms are barred from owning advanced Nvidia GPUs but can legally lease compute capacity from offshore data centers in Southeast Asia; congressional investigators and Commerce Department officials (who recently deliberated adding Megaspeed and several dozen firms to the U.S. Entity List) now have strong political backing to close this offshore leasing channel. This event sits at the intersection of technological decoupling, supply-chain fragmentation, and systemic corporate governance risk. As advanced semiconductors have become instruments of national security, commercial distribution channels in third countries (such as Singapore and Malaysia) have turned into contested battlegrounds for state intelligence agencies. Simultaneously, Nvidia has grown to systemic financial scale ($4.5 trillion valuation, generating >$7 million in annual revenue per employee across 42,000 staff) while retaining the flat, founder-dependent governance structure of an early-stage company. The U.S. Commerce and Justice Departments, alongside authorities in Singapore, have been investigating Megaspeed for over a year over alleged chip smuggling and are now positioned to add Megaspeed and affiliated Southeast Asian entities to the U.S. Entity List. In parallel, institutional investors and lawmakers in the 2027 Congress are likely to pressure Nvidia's board to adopt bank-style systemic contingency planning—appointing an independent Chair, establishing a de facto number-two executive (President or Chief Operating Officer), and auditing GPU allocation commitments. The espionage investigation into Nvidia's CEO transforms a personal governance lapse into a national security catalyst that threatens to sever Chinese firms' offshore leasing access to U.S. AI chips and force institutional succession controls onto a $4.5 trillion market bellwether.

Following severe Republican losses in the U.S. midterm elections—which exit polls and party strategists linked to inflation and higher fuel prices from the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran launched on February 28, 2026—President Trump told CNN on Sunday, November 8 that renewed U.S. military strikes on Iran are "highly unlikely" so long as diplomatic peace talks progress. Concurrently, newly appointed White House Chief of Staff Stephen Miller advocated internally for winding down the Iran conflict. This political pivot triggered an immediate unwinding of the geopolitical supply-disruption premium in global crude oil markets, even as Iran-aligned militias and Yemen's Houthi rebels executed new physical strikes against Saudi Arabian oil infrastructure and Gulf shipping.

Global crude oil benchmarks fell sharply across both futures and physical spot markets. On Monday, November 9, global benchmark Brent crude fell as much as 5.6% intraday to a 10-week low of $92.80 per barrel before trading around $93.87 per barrel. U.S. marker West Texas Intermediate (WTI) dropped 3% on Monday to $84.00 per barrel, extending a prior weekly plunge of $13.29 per barrel (-12.76% to $90.83 on Friday, November 6)—the steepest weekly oil price drop since April 2020. Crucially, Dated Brent—Europe's physical oil price for immediate delivery—fell to $92.25 per barrel on Monday, down 32.4% from its October 5 peak of $136.39 per barrel (when front-month Brent futures stood at $103.40), making immediate physical oil cheaper than oil for January delivery. This shift into contango (a market structure where prompt physical supply trades below future delivery contracts) reflects a drop in maritime shipping costs and immediately lowers feedstock costs for European refineries. The oil price drop eased headline inflation fears, catalyzing Monday's equity rebound across the S&P 500 (+2.31%), Eurofirst 300 (+1.30%), Nikkei 225 (+2.33%), and Taiwan's Taiex (+4.9%, which was also supported by government curbs on short selling announced Friday).

European refineries and energy-intensive manufacturers benefit immediately from physical Dated Brent falling to $92.25 per barrel and lower maritime freight rates. Global airlines and travel operators (IAG, American Airlines, Ryanair, Booking Holdings, Marriott, Hilton) gain relief from jet fuel and petrol price spikes as the travel sector builds on nearly $50 billion in market value gains among top hotel groups in 2026. The European Central Bank (ECB) and U.S. consumers also gain flexibility; ECB contender Klaas Knot stated that if energy prices remain near current levels, the ECB (which raised borrowing costs in two 25-basis-point steps since June to 2.5%) will see little need for higher interest rates, while U.S. households see relief from a war-driven ~$1.50-per-gallon gasoline spike that cost typical families ~$20 per week. Conversely, oil service companies and upstream producers suffered sharp equity losses; the PHLX Oil Service Index plunged 12.25% last week, and Chevron shares dropped 10.65% (-$22.91 per share to $192.61), pulling down the Dow Jones Industrial Average by 136.17 points. Saudi Arabia's non-oil diversification economy is also taking direct damage: repeated Houthi missile and drone strikes—including 20 attacks in 10 days on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport in early October (killing at least 17 and injuring >300), Sunday's strike on the Saudi Aramco tower ("Villas in the Sky") in Riyadh's King Abdullah Financial District, and Monday's strike on the East-West oil pipeline—forced financial district employees to work remotely and caused the cancellation or postponement of major events including the flagship Future Investment Initiative ("Davos in the Desert"). Maritime insurers and tanker operators continue to bear physical destruction risk in the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea; the UK Maritime Trade Operations agency reported that a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz was hit by an unknown projectile on Saturday after another tanker was struck exiting the Gulf on Thursday. Iran and regional proxy militias (Yemen's Houthis and Iraqi militias) externalize security, insurance, and business-disruption costs onto Gulf states and international shipping while facing diminished risk of direct U.S. military escalation.

Domestic U.S. electoral incentives have overridden military escalation. Pre-war polling reviewed by The Wall Street Journal showed 62% of Americans unwilling to accept more than 100 U.S. combat deaths, and midterm voters punished the ruling party over energy-driven inflation. Furthermore, incoming House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries pledged to use the new congressional majority in January 2027 to stop the war in Iran. As Energy Aspects analysts Amrita Sen and Daniel Sternoff noted, Tehran has little incentive to offer new diplomatic concessions to Trump when Iranian leaders know the incoming U.S. Congress may move to cut war funding within months. This dynamic illustrates the feedback loop between energy realignment, inflation pressure, and sovereign electoral constraints. In an era of elevated public debt and 3.7% U.S. consumer price inflation, Western electorates have minimal tolerance for energy supply shocks. Military interventions that disrupt maritime chokepoints (such as the Strait of Hormuz) rapidly transmit into domestic gasoline prices and mortgage rates, forcing political reversals before military objectives are achieved. Because the U.S.-Israeli war with Iran effectively shut down the Strait of Hormuz, Saudi Arabia relies heavily on its East-West pipeline to transport crude from eastern oilfields to the Red Sea. Although Monday's fire on the East-West pipeline in the Madinah region was extinguished without halting operations (following a temporary shutdown in September after an Iraqi militia drone strike and an October Houthi attack on a pumping station), sustained strikes on the pipeline could abruptly reverse the oil price decline. Concurrently, U.S. pressure to wind down the regional war coincides with Israeli President Isaac Herzog granting centrist challenger Gadi Eisenkot (who secured 67 Knesset endorsements to Benjamin Netanyahu's 46 after United Torah Judaism abstained) a four-to-six-week mandate to form a national emergency government, shifting Israeli policy toward potential coalition compromises on military conscription and economic stabilization. Oil traders are pricing in an electoral end to U.S. offensive strikes on Iran, lowering global inflation pressure, but physical crude supply remains vulnerable to asymmetric militia attacks on Saudi Arabia's sole pipeline bypass to the Red Sea.

The Democratic victory in the November 2026 U.S. Senate elections (securing 54 seats, with two close elections pending that could reach 56) removes President Trump's ability to confirm partisan nominees to the Federal Reserve's seven-member Board of Governors for the remainder of his term. Because presidential appointees to the Fed board require Senate confirmation, the election result halts the White House's campaign to assemble a board majority committed to lowering interest rates. With executive pressure neutralized, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) remains anchored to inflation-fighting monetary policy. Under Chairman Kevin Warsh (confirmed in May 2026 with only one Democratic vote, from Sen. John Fetterman), the Fed raised interest rates in September 2026—its first rate hike since 2023—to combat persistent inflation (with the September U.S. Consumer Price Index up 3.7% year-over-year and Core CPI up 2.6%) and held the federal-funds target rate steady at 4.00%–4.25% in October. Investors expect another rate increase in December 2026, and Polymarket prediction markets price a 97% probability of zero Fed rate cuts in 2026. Consequently, sovereign and private borrowing costs remain elevated: the 10-year U.S. Treasury yield closed at 4.858% on Friday, November 6 and rose to 4.91% on Monday, November 9, while the 2-year Treasury yield sits at 4.520% and the U.S. Treasury Long Index yields 5.270%. High benchmark yields keep 30-year fixed mortgage rates at 7.18%–7.23% (5-year adjustable-rate mortgages at 6.32%), commercial bank prime lending rates at 7.00%, and ICE BofA High Yield corporate bond yields at 7.588%.

Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and sitting institutional governors (Jerome Powell, Lisa Cook, Philip Jefferson, Michael Barr) gain an institutional Senate buffer against White House removal threats or forced dovish appointees. U.S. dollar holders and fixed-income savers benefit as the WSJ Dollar Index rose 0.46% on the week to 97.52 (US Dollar Index at 101.85–102.28) and cash accounts earn 3.7% to 4.5% yields without central bank financial repression. Floating-rate private credit providers, such as publicly traded Business Development Companies (BDCs), continue to generate high-single-digit dividend yields from floating-rate loans to middle-market companies. Conversely, interest-rate-sensitive debtors—including commercial real estate borrowers, equity and mortgage Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs), and prospective homebuyers—face prolonged refinancing and affordability stress as Treasury yields remain near 5%. The Trump administration loses its primary institutional lever to lower sovereign debt-servicing costs or stimulate rate-sensitive sectors ahead of 2028. The U.S. Treasury and federal taxpayers absorb higher sovereign debt-service costs as the Treasury auctions 3-year notes, 10-year notes, and 30-year bonds this week at yields between 4.5% and 5.3% following a $284 billion October monthly federal budget deficit. Federal fiscal authorities, by running multi-trillion-dollar structural deficits (having raised the debt ceiling by $5 trillion in 2025) and tariff regimes that stoke price pressures, externalize the burden of macroeconomic stabilization onto the Federal Reserve, which must keep borrowing costs elevated for private borrowers. The White House has attempted to create a vacancy on the seven-member Fed board by seeking to fire Biden-appointed Governor Lisa Cook (who dissented in favor of a rate hike in October) over mortgage-fraud allegations she denies, scheduling a White House hearing for December 2026 after the Supreme Court allowed her to remain in her seat in June. However, because Trump has appointed only three of the seven governors (Warsh, Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman, and Christopher Waller) and only three of the 12 voting FOMC members (after temporary governor Stephen Miran stepped down in May following six dissents for easier policy), firing Cook no longer yields a majority. A Democratic Senate can simply refuse to confirm any replacement, leaving the seat empty. Furthermore, Senate Democrats now hold confirmation veto power over who succeeds Philip Jefferson when his term as Fed Vice Chair expires in September 2027.

This development reinforces monetary-fiscal divergence and institutional checks on fiscal dominance. When sovereign debt levels and fiscal deficits expand alongside supply-side inflation shocks, executive branches face strong incentives to capture central banks to suppress real interest rates. Anglo American shareholders offload a structurally challenged luxury commodity business to focus on supply-constrained copper essential for electrification, while the Global Diamond Consortium, Oman's sovereign wealth capital, and producing states acquire the premier diamond marketing brand at a deeply discounted valuation (~$4 billion). However, Botswana's sovereign balance sheet and taxpayers—who already produce 70% of De Beers' diamonds and own 50% of local mining joint venture Debswana—are increasing their fiscal concentration in natural diamonds just a year after the IMF warned Botswana to diversify its economy away from the diamond sector. President Boko did not disclose how Botswana will finance the additional 15% stake; if the downturn in natural diamond demand proves structural rather than cyclical due to synthetic lab-grown substitution, Botswana will face heightened fiscal and foreign-exchange pressure. Anglo American is taking a massive valuation haircut to exit natural diamonds for copper, transferring ownership—and the structural risk of lab-grown diamond substitution—onto Botswana and its neighboring producer states.
