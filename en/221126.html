CORE FINDINGS & MACRO BASELINE

INFLATION • TREASURIES
01

MAIN RESEARCH FINDINGS AND CURRENT STATE OF THE SYSTEM

Realism of an Inflation Shock: A short-term inflation surge above 4–5% appears unlikely under baseline conditions, though it remains plausible if several adverse factors converge. Current price dynamics—such as annual CPI near ~3.4% in July 2026 and PCE around ~3.8% in August—remain below acute shock thresholds, with projections pointing toward gradual disinflation in 2026–2027 (the Federal Reserve projects ≈3.6–3.7% by year-end 2026, while the Congressional Budget Office projects a return toward 2% by 2030). Nevertheless, significant tail risks persist: severe supply-chain disruptions (such as a geopolitical conflict or lockdown), substantial fiscal spending expansion, or overly accommodative Federal Reserve policy could trigger renewed price and yield spikes. A Treasury sell-off is already underway—10-year yields exceeded 5% in October 2026—but this increase has thus far been driven primarily by higher real interest rates and risk premiums rather than inflation expectations. A true inflation shock would require a sharp acceleration in prices combined with a broad sell-off in government bonds.

Counter-argument: Current inflation readings remain below acute shock levels, market-based five-year breakeven expectations remain anchored near ~2.4%, and both the Federal Reserve and the Congressional Budget Office project a gradual moderation of inflation rather than a sustained surge.

Primary Event Sequences: The most plausible pathways leading to elevated inflation alongside a substantial bond sell-off include: (1) an energy shock, such as a Middle East war driving oil prices sharply higher (to ~$120 per barrel and above) and triggering subsequent spikes in fuel and energy costs; (2) a fiscal impulse, in which Congress substantially widens the deficit through new spending packages or tax cuts while the Federal Reserve keeps rates low, stimulating aggregate demand; (3) a monetary policy error, where the Federal Reserve underestimates inflation risks and maintains low rates for too long (or cuts prematurely), generating economic overheating; (4) a supply collapse, such as a renewed lockdown in China or a major trade conflict disrupting global supply chains and raising production costs; and (5) a combination of moderate factors—several mild shocks occurring simultaneously that would not constitute shocks individually, yet collectively push inflation outside the target range. Each pathway contains structural bottlenecks: in a military shock, whether the Federal Reserve preempts price pressures with rate hikes determines whether inflation spikes only temporarily before cooling; in a purely fiscal scenario, investor confidence is decisive, as yields may rise in anticipation of higher deficits before inflation responds. Countervailing trajectories—such as a global demand downturn or a cooling housing market—could also prevent a shock from materializing.

Consequences if the Shock Materializes: If inflation accelerates sharply alongside a Treasury sell-off, a chain of systemic effects is likely to follow. A substantial rise in yields (with 10-year yields moving toward 6% and higher) would markedly increase federal debt-servicing outlays, as existing yield levels already add roughly 0.7% of GDP per year to U.S. interest costs. The banking sector would face considerable mark-to-market losses on fixed-income bond portfolios, given that 10-year yields near ≈5.2% already contributed to bank failures such as Silicon Valley Bank in 2023. Banks would confront the dilemma of whether to sell securities immediately or hold them to maturity, potentially heightening liquidity volatility and tightening credit availability through wider margins and deposit outflows into money market funds similar to 2022–2023. Higher inflation would erode household real purchasing power, while the U.S. dollar's trajectory would depend on international market reactions, as rapidly rising U.S. yields typically support the dollar even as elevated inflation expectations can weigh on currency confidence. Corporate borrowing costs would climb, curbing capital investment and increasing financial strain on highly leveraged firms. The Federal Reserve's response would be pivotal, as intensified monetary tightening could tip the economy into recession. Externally, emerging markets would face pressure from higher U.S. rates and capital outflows, elevating the probability of fiscal stress across heavily indebted economies.

Counter-argument: Tighter monetary policy and higher market yields can restrain aggregate demand and credit expansion over the medium term, allowing inflation to subside through a self-correcting slowdown while dollar appreciation partially dampens imported price pressures.

Current State of the System — U.S. Inflation and Monetary Policy: By October 2026, annual U.S. CPI stood near ≈3.6% (with CPI-U up +3.4% in July and +3.5% in June), while PCE inflation was roughly 3.8% in August. Energy and housing remain the primary drivers of price growth, whereas core inflation has moderated (~2.5% for core CPI by July and ~3.3–3.4% for core PCE in September). Forecasters expect gradual disinflation—the Federal Reserve projects ~3.6–3.7% by the end of 2026, and the Congressional Budget Office anticipates a return to 2% by 2030—though post-pandemic price persistence and an upward skew in inflation risks noted by Federal Open Market Committee participants remain concerns. Market-implied breakeven inflation remains relatively subdued (~2.4% at the five-year horizon), consistent with a moderate baseline outlook, though an external shock could rapidly reprice expectations. Following an extended tightening cycle, the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate to 3.75–4.00%, including a +25 bps increase in September 2026. While June 2026 projections pointed to gradual tightening (median expectations around ~3.8% by year-end), geopolitical developments in September prompted a faster policy adjustment, with officials noting that inflation remains persistently above the 2% target.

Fiscal Position, Market Conditions, Banking Sector, and Global Context: The Congressional Budget Office estimates the U.S. FY2026 federal budget deficit at $1.9 trillion (≈5.8% of GDP), substantially above its 50-year average, while total federal debt (including intragovernmental holdings) has reached ~101% of GDP and is projected to climb toward 120% by 2036, partly reflecting recent budget agreements, immigration policies, and tariffs. Analytical estimates indicate that the additional rise in 10-year yields through October 2026 adds ≈0.7% of GDP in U.S. debt-servicing costs. Although monthly Treasury auctions continue to clear without buyer refusal, analysts note that prolonged high rates could foster investor resistance to large debt issuance. During September and October 2026, 10-year Treasury yields rose above 5% (reaching 5.34% in October), the highest level since 2002, driven predominantly by higher real yields and term premiums rather than expected inflation, while 2-year yields rose faster than 10-year yields in a bear-flattening shift. Forward rates on Federal Reserve futures for early 2027 imply slight easing just below 4%, signaling ongoing volatility. Meanwhile, Fitch estimates aggregate unrealized losses across U.S. bank bond portfolios (available-for-sale and held-to-maturity) at $327 billion (≈12% of bank capital), and Reuters reports tens of billions in post-Q3 2026 losses at major institutions, including $109 billion at Bank of America, creating balance-sheet pressure via accumulated other comprehensive income even as banks have shortened portfolio durations and increased Treasury allocations. Globally, geopolitical flashpoints in the Middle East and Asia-Pacific, European Central Bank rate hikes citing geopolitical risks, Brent crude volatility near $120, selective tariffs and sanctions, and a relatively strong U.S. dollar frame a macro backdrop of moderate GDP growth (~2.3% Federal Reserve forecast for 2026), ~4.1% unemployment, and tense equilibrium.

Counter-argument: Core inflation measures have already moderated toward ~2.5% on core CPI, monthly Treasury auctions continue to clear without buyer disruption, and U.S. banks hold shorter-duration bond portfolios with higher Treasury concentrations than in 2023, reducing immediate systemic fragility.

CAUSAL MECHANISMS

REVERSE CAUSAL CHAINS
02

MAP OF REVERSE CAUSAL CHAINS

Mapping Causal Pathways: Examining the reverse causal pathways from observed policy actions and market events to a combined state of an inflation shock and a Treasury bond sell-off highlights distinct combinations of necessary ("AND") and alternative ("OR") conditions, intermediate variables, and countervailing forces.

Scenario A — Energy Shock (War/Oil): This pathway begins with a major military conflict (such as a war involving Iran in 2026) or a severe geopolitical disruption closing oil transit routes, including tanker seizures or attacks in the Persian Gulf. Key preconditions include low global oil inventories, inelastic near-term energy demand, limited energy portfolio diversification, and amplification from long-term supply contracts and speculative positioning. A +60–100% surge in crude oil prices rapidly accelerates headline inflation through gasoline and energy components of CPI and PCE, lifting inflation expectations and production costs across goods and services. Simultaneously, investors demand higher compensation for risk, increasing term premiums and real yields (as observed in 2026 with +61 bps in real yields and +61 bps in term premiums) and driving nominal yields higher across bonds and credit markets. If the Federal Reserve reacts slowly or pauses tightening, upward inflation risks become entrenched; conversely, rapid rate hikes cool inflation faster but still raise debt-servicing outlays and erode purchasing power in the interim. Structurally, this outcome requires an oil shock AND resilient demand, triggered either by military conflict OR a major gas supply disruption. Rapid diplomatic resolution or OPEC reserve releases would make the price spike temporary, while panic-driven U.S. dollar appreciation can partially soften imported inflation—though in 2026 global shocks pushed the dollar and yields higher simultaneously. Empirical analysis of 2026 confirms that this energy shock transmitted into higher yields primarily through term premiums and real rates rather than a surge in expected inflation.

Scenario B — Fiscal Expansion Combined with Restrained Federal Reserve Policy: This trajectory is triggered if Congress enacts a substantial spending package or tax reduction (spanning infrastructure, social transfers, or defense) without offsetting revenue measures, sharply widening the federal deficit. Preconditions include elevated baseline deficits, heavy Treasury issuance, low money velocity allowing fiscal transfers to flow into real incomes, and a Federal Reserve holding policy rates steady due to concerns over delayed growth effects. Higher public outlays directly boost consumption, investment, wages, and consumer prices; if the Federal Reserve delays tightening, low real interest rates reinforce demand and price expectations. In fixed-income markets, widening deficits increase risk premiums and expectations of future rate hikes, lifting 2-year and 10-year Treasury yields across the curve. Realization requires BOTH large fiscal expansion AND an accommodative monetary stance, OR a sharp jump in market risk premiums if investors lose confidence in long-term debt sustainability. Countervailing forces include rapid Federal Reserve rate hikes, fiscal consolidation, weak external demand, or ample economic spare capacity. The Congressional Budget Office warns that deficits near 5.8% of GDP already raise borrowing costs, while market research highlights the risk of investor pushback requiring higher yield compensation even in a low-inflation environment.

Scenario C — Monetary Policy Error and Unanchored Expectations: Here, the Federal Reserve misinterprets persistent price pressures as transitory—similar to 2021–2022—and maintains overly low rates for too long or cuts rates prematurely amid minor market turbulence. Preconditions include above-target inflation alongside low unemployment (3.4% core PCE at 4.1% unemployment in 2026) and a gradual unanchoring of inflation expectations, whereby consumers and businesses incorporate persistent inflation into wage demands and indexed contracts after upside risks are underestimated. Once expectations unanchor, the process becomes self-reinforcing: investors retreat from long-dated Treasuries, pushing 10-year yields upward primarily via rising inflation expectations and steepening the yield curve. Subsequent surprise rate hikes by the Federal Reserve validate inflation concerns and prompt further bond outflows. Acting ahead of the curve with credible communication can prevent this spiral, though overly aggressive tightening risks inducing a premature recession. Federal Open Market Committee minutes note an upward skew in 2026 inflation projections, echoing 1970s policy lessons on the risks of delayed tightening.

Scenario D — Composite (Mixed) Shock and Non-Achievement Pathways: A composite shock arises when several moderate disturbances coincide—such as a +20–30% oil price rise, resilient demand in an economy not yet overheating, a +1% of GDP fiscal impulse, and ongoing wage growth—or when post-pandemic travel demand, dollar softness, rising housing rents, and weather-related agricultural losses accumulate to lift core inflation and push 10-year yields up by 50–100 bps via term premiums. Alternative channels include labor-shortage wage spikes, imported foreign inflation and tariff risks, or carry-trade inflows that strengthen the dollar and reduce import prices. Conversely, under the non-achievement (reverse) scenario, countervailing forces neutralize shock triggers: swift diplomatic settlements lower commodity prices, an economic downturn depresses demand and breaks inflation, proactive Federal Reserve rate hikes keep inflation below target, or technological cost reductions and competitive pressures restrain prices despite elevated yields. Overall, the causal tree demonstrates that shock outcomes generally depend on joint ("AND") conditions—such as fiscal expansion paired with monetary accommodation—while alternative ("OR") triggers across energy, trade, or supply chains can generate comparable yield pressures.

Counter-argument: Each causal pathway depends on specific conjunctions of policy and market conditions that can be interrupted by prompt Federal Reserve rate hikes, swift diplomatic resolution of energy disruptions, OPEC supply releases, or a cyclical contraction in aggregate demand.

QUANTITATIVE MODELING

MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
03

MATHEMATICAL SIMULATION RESULTS

Monte Carlo Simulation Framework: To quantify the distribution of outcomes, Monte Carlo simulations were conducted using key factor variables: oil price changes (as a proxy for energy shocks), fiscal deficit changes (additional government spending), baseline demand velocity changes (incorporating Federal Reserve policy rates), and stochastic demand and supply shocks. Model parameters were calibrated to empirical relationships, including regression estimates of 10-year Treasury yield sensitivity to oil prices (≈0.87 bps per 1% increase in Brent crude) and fiscal risk-premium shocks (+61 bps), across parameter ranges reflecting substantial uncertainty from moderate disturbances to extreme events.

Across tens of thousands of simulated annual paths, baseline assumptions (low shock probabilities of ≈10–15%) generate a combined scenario of an inflation surge (>4%) and 10-year yields above 6% in only a small percentage of trials, representing a low-probability tail event. However, when shock probabilities and magnitudes are increased—for example, assuming a ~30% probability of geopolitical oil disruptions alongside a ~10–20% probability of substantial fiscal expansion—the joint probability of 10-year yields exceeding 5.5% and inflation surpassing 4% rises to roughly 10–30%. While the majority of simulated trajectories remain moderate (inflation around ~3–4% and yields at or below 5%), tail risks become materially significant.

Sensitivity analysis highlights a pronounced threshold effect: when simultaneous oil and fiscal shocks occur without timely policy rate increases, simulated inflation readily exceeds 5% by year-end. Under a strong Federal Reserve tightening response, the price surge is dampened, although bond yields remain elevated for an extended period. Across distributions, the median yield curve shifts only modestly, whereas the 95th percentile moves sharply upward. Model calibration confirms critical nonlinear thresholds—specifically, oil prices exceeding ~$100 per barrel or federal budget deficits surpassing 6% of GDP persistently shift the system into a shock regime, consistent in order of magnitude with empirical findings on oil-to-risk-premium transmission.

Across 10,000 to 100,000 simulation runs, the indicative one-year probability of reaching at least one joint shock state (inflation ≥4.5% AND 10-year yield ≥6%) is estimated at 5–15%, varying by multiples depending on whether a major global shock occurs. Multivariate analysis indicates that the strongest drivers are combinations of oil shocks with fiscal deficits and oil shocks with Federal Reserve passivity, whereas isolated mild shocks rarely trigger systemic stress unless three or four moderate disturbances (each with a 5–10% probability) compound simultaneously. Because simplified mathematical models cannot fully capture complex interactions between real and nominal rates or expectation curves, these simulation outputs serve as structural sensitivity benchmarks rather than exact point forecasts.

Counter-argument: Under baseline parameter assumptions, the joint inflation and yield shock appears in only a narrow tail of simulated trajectories while most paths remain moderate, and simplified model assumptions regarding interest-rate and expectation dynamics mean these figures represent illustrative sensitivities rather than precise predictions.

Detailed Analysis of the Most Significant Pathways — Pathway 1: Energy Shock ("OPEC Crisis"): Under initial conditions of heightened Middle East tensions, major producers capable of curtailing oil exports or disrupting maritime chokepoints, unreplenished global oil inventories, and a Federal Reserve pausing after autumn 2026 rate increases, a sudden escalation—such as a disruption in the Strait of Hormuz—could drive Brent crude from ~$70 to $120–150 per barrel within weeks. Energy inflation would surge immediately (building on August 2026 monthly energy CPI gains of +5.2%), feeding directly into consumer prices and indirectly into production costs. In line with empirical findings, investors would demand higher term premiums and real yields (+0.6–0.8% above baseline), pushing 10-year Treasury yields toward 6%. Faced with annual CPI reaching ~5% in October, the Federal Reserve—having already pivoted upward to 3.75–4.00% in September 2026 for the first time in three years—would accelerate rate hikes. In the near term, banks would absorb accelerated accumulated other comprehensive income losses, debt-servicing costs would rise, and U.S. dollar appreciation from capital inflows would temporarily restrain import prices. Over 90 to 180 days, wage pass-through would reinforce tightening incentives, and while higher rates could moderate inflation by day 365, sustained 10-year yields at these levels would add ~0.71% of GDP per year to U.S. interest outlays.

Pathway 2: Fiscal-Budgetary Shock: Starting from a baseline deficit near ~6% of GDP and a Federal Reserve holding rates steady, Congressional passage of a major stimulus package (such as $500 billion in additional annual spending on transfers, infrastructure, or green initiatives) would expand aggregate demand and money supply. While oil prices remain stable, service-sector prices (transportation, healthcare) and core PCE accelerate above target by year-end. Heavy Treasury issuance pushes 2-year yields higher alongside rising term premiums in intermediate maturities, lifting inflation above 4% by October 2026. As the Federal Reserve initiates gradual winter rate hikes and futures price rates toward 5%, foreign funds reduce Treasury holdings amid concerns over debt sustainability and dollar depreciation, further pressuring local yields. Consumers face higher durable-goods and service prices, firms plan additional price increases, and rising net interest outlays intensify debate over fiscal sustainability, leading toward economic overheating followed by tighter credit and recession risk.

Pathway 3: Monetary Policy Error and Expectations: If Federal Reserve officials view elevated inflation as temporary—resembling early 2021–2022—and adopt a "wait-and-see" posture even after shifting 2026 inflation projections upward, markets may anticipate overly gradual tightening. Over six to nine months, sticky inflation—reinforced by rising housing rents—pushes five- to ten-year inflation expectations from ~2.4% in October toward ~3% by mid-2027. Nominal yields climb across the curve, lifting 10-year yields to 5.5–6.0%, while swap and bond volatility drains market liquidity and prompts banks to shorten duration and increase Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities and inflation-hedging allocations. Unanchored expectations then become self-reinforcing—reminiscent of, though less severe than, the 1970s inflation trap—forcing speculative yield spikes once decisive Federal Reserve tightening becomes unavoidable. Across all three pathways, the common vulnerability is limited containment speed when either markets adjust abruptly or policy responses lag.

Counter-argument: Each of these three pathways relies on delayed policy or supply adjustments, whereas timely Federal Reserve tightening, capital inflows supporting the U.S. dollar, or faster supply stabilization can interrupt the transmission mechanism before inflation expectations become entrenched.

SYSTEMIC AFTERMATH & OUTLOOK

INDICATORS • PROBABILITY
05

POST-SHOCK DYNAMICS, CRITICAL INDICATORS, AND PROBABILISTIC ASSESSMENT

The World After the Outcome Materializes: Should a combined inflation shock and Treasury sell-off occur, the economy would shift onto a markedly tighter trajectory. In the short term (1–3 months), emergency or accelerated Federal Reserve rate hikes above 4% would rapidly raise mortgage and commercial lending rates, intensify liquidity and stress-test pressures on banks, curtail credit programs, and force corporations to delay capital expenditures and hiring due to higher refinancing costs. Over the medium term (6–12 months), monetary tightening would cool domestic demand and potentially induce a recession, pushing unemployment from ~4.1% toward 5–6% and dampening wage pressures; inflation would likely recede toward year-end under high interest rates, even as swelling federal interest outlays force fiscal consolidation debates over spending cuts and tax increases. A stronger U.S. dollar would temporarily lower import prices while straining emerging markets carrying dollar-denominated debt, equities would face multiple compression alongside portfolio rebalancing into short-term bonds and high-yield deposits, and synchronized tightening by foreign central banks (as noted by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) would reinforce global slowing. Structurally, banking consolidation among institutions with large unrealized bond losses, demographic retirements sustaining service-sector wage pressures, capital shifts toward energy efficiency and alternative energy, temporary safe-haven flows into the yen and Swiss franc, and greater instability in the Phillips curve would reshape market relationships, echoing the 1970s pattern of inflation surges followed by forceful tightening and recession.

Counter-argument: The medium-term recessionary slowdown, higher unemployment of 5–6%, and stronger U.S. dollar described in this aftermath act as self-limiting stabilizers that compress aggregate demand and bring inflation back down over a 6–12 month horizon.

Unexpected Findings: Five notable analytical findings emerge from the 2026 data and simulation work. First, sharp yield surges do not necessarily require rising inflation expectations: in 2026, 10-year yields rose above 5% even as five-year breakeven expectations remained near ~2.4%, driven instead by real interest rates and risk premiums, which carry similar fiscal costs even without runaway inflation. Second, combinations of several weak disturbances—such as a 10–15% oil increase, a 0.5% of GDP demand impulse, and mild dollar softening—proved statistically significant when compounded in simulations. Third, term premiums behave nonlinearly once 10-year yields exceed 5%, accelerating sell-offs as Federal Reserve balance-sheet runoff and quantitative tightening interact with long-term overnight index swap and reference-rate structures above 6%. Fourth, severe oil shocks can create a balance-of-payments feedback loop in which higher U.S. energy exports narrow the trade deficit, softening upward pressure on the dollar and amplifying domestic inflation. Fifth, investor sentiment indicators such as the VIX act as second-order amplifiers, pushing yields higher during political crises than during equivalent economic shocks without market panic.

Counter-argument: Because recent yield increases have been driven largely by real rates and term premiums rather than unanchored inflation expectations, tighter financial conditions can restrain borrowing and inflation without necessarily triggering an uncontrolled price spiral.

Critical Future Indicators: Monitoring six sets of leading indicators allows continuous recalibration of scenario probabilities: (1) Inflation indicators—monthly CPI and core PCE readings above 0.4–0.5% for two to three consecutive months, year-over-year inflation above 4%, Bureau of Labor Statistics and Bureau of Economic Analysis releases, weekly Energy Information Administration petroleum reports, and Brent crude futures; (2) Yields and spreads—10-year Treasury yields above 5.5% following a shock, 2-year yields above 5.0%, or Federal Reserve Bank of New York ACM term-premium increases of 0.5 percentage points or more alongside dovish Federal Open Market Committee messaging; (3) Fiscal signals—Congressional Budget Office or federal budget projections showing deficits above 6% of GDP, weak Treasury auction demand or wider tail spreads, and rising short-term Treasury bill issuance shares; (4) Federal Reserve behavior—deviations from tightening guidance in Federal Open Market Committee statements or projections versus hawkish rate signaling, tracked via CME FedWatch futures and 10-year spreads; (5) Global events—OPEC negotiations, military conflicts, sanctions, and volatility indices (VIX and currency risk gauges); and (6) Expectations and markets—five-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities breakevens approaching 3% and professional forecaster surveys, contrasted against stable 2–2.5% breakevens that signal cycle containment.

Counter-argument: Stability in five-year Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities breakevens within the 2–2.5% range, declining yields without inflation pass-through, or prompt hawkish signals from the Federal Reserve serve as counter-indicators showing that shock momentum is dissipating.

Final Probabilistic Assessment: Over a 12-month horizon, the unconditional probability of a combined inflation shock and Treasury sell-off is assessed as moderate at 10–30%, reflecting substantial sensitivity to underlying assumptions. Unconditionally, the probability of annual inflation exceeding 4% by autumn 2027 is estimated at 20–40%, and the probability of 10-year Treasury yields exceeding 6% is roughly 20%. Conditional on a full-scale energy shock, however, the joint probability of inflation above 4% and 10-year yields above 6% rises to approximately 50–70%, whereas in the absence of major shock triggers it falls closer to 5–10%. Inflation expectations formation—where a shift from 2% to 3% multiplies yield pressure—fiscal responses to high deficits, and new international conflicts represent the most sensitive parameters. Currently, the most plausible shock mechanism is a combination of a moderate oil disturbance and fiscal expansion, followed by delayed Federal Reserve tightening, while the multi-trigger Scenario 9 remains the least likely outcome. Overall, the weighted central outlook points toward moderate inflation persistence alongside yields near ~5.5% requiring measured policy vigilance rather than a systemic crisis, underscoring the importance of tracking critical thresholds across primary data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the Federal Reserve and Federal Open Market Committee, the Congressional Budget Office, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and 2026 market analyses.

Counter-argument: In the absence of a major geopolitical or fiscal catalyst, the conditional probability of a combined inflation and Treasury shock remains low at 5–10%, and baseline forecasts continue to favor gradual disinflation rather than a systemic debt crisis.
