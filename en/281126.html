Global Outlook

GLOBAL ECONOMY • INSTITUTIONAL POWER
01

The Limits of Direct Political Intervention

Late November 2026 subjected the global economy and international politics to an unusually revealing stress test. Events over just a few days—from Jensen Huang's rapid return as Nvidia chief executive following a personal call from Donald Trump and the closure of a loophole enabling American chips to reach Chinese AI developers, to Washington's renewed tariff offensive, reductions in Iranian bread subsidies, and a Ukrainian maritime drone attack in Libya's Benghazi—appear to share a common pattern. On both sides of the Atlantic, in the Middle East, and in Asia, political leaders and technology corporations are attempting to manage highly complex systems through direct intervention, informal deals, and coercive pressure. Each such intervention, however, can provoke resistance from markets, courts, and allies.

These events involve distinct legal, commercial, and security decisions. Their timing alone does not establish that direct political intervention is the sole cause of the resistance described.

Corporate, macroeconomic, military, and diplomatic developments suggest that these seemingly disconnected stories reflect a broader structural shift. Checks and balances—including independent corporate boards, multilateral trade and climate agreements, predictable monetary policy, and even the price signals of commodity markets—are coming under growing pressure from geopolitical competition and concentrated capital. At the same time, those pursuing discretionary control increasingly face resistance from their own bureaucracies, fiscal constraints, and voters weary of rising prices.

Institutional checks continue to operate through the courts, corporate governance, and market reactions, even when their authority is contested.

AI Governance

CORPORATE GOVERNANCE • EXPORT CONTROLS
02

Executive Power and the U.S. AI Industry

Jensen Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia CEO on November 22–23, just 11–12 days after his November 11–12 resignation amid reported admissions of extramarital affairs, including relationships with subordinates and with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, marked a significant development in corporate governance. U.S. authorities reportedly suspected Alice Huang of links to Chinese intelligence and chip smuggling. As recently as six days before the reversal, Nvidia's board chairman had publicly ruled out the founder's return. The turning point came when President Donald Trump, who reportedly owns Nvidia shares personally, joined a board meeting with assistance from Vice President JD Vance and pressed for reinstatement, assuring directors that Huang's return would pose no national-security threat and that he would not face criminal prosecution. The Washington Post traces a similar model of CEO insulation to Elon Musk beginning in 2018: he weathered $20 million SEC penalties and repeated controversies before Tesla shareholders approved a potential $1 trillion compensation package conditional on the company's valuation reaching $8.5 trillion. In 2023, Sam Altman's return to OpenAI against the board's wishes reinforced that pattern.

The three executive reinstatement and compensation episodes illustrate different governance arrangements; they do not establish that every major technology executive is protected against enforcement or shareholder scrutiny.

The market reaction was striking. On Monday, November 23, Nvidia's market capitalization rose by almost $1 trillion, but by Wednesday, November 25, its shares had fallen another 3.9% to $149.30, against an annual high of $243.37. The Nasdaq 100 had recently entered bear-market territory, more than 20% below its peak, and approached month-end down 8.2%. A potential explanation is the tension between the White House's political support and increasing institutional opposition within the U.S. government. While Trump posted an AI-generated image of Huang holding a red bra and told CNBC that America needed to protect its geniuses, Secretary of State and National Security Advisor Marco Rubio reportedly circulated intelligence concerning Alice Huang's alleged ties to Beijing. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly criticized Nvidia's handling of Megaspeed, although he softened his rhetoric after presidential intervention. Jay Clayton, identified in the reporting as director of national intelligence, was also reported to have confirmed a federal investigation involving Megaspeed and Alice Huang.

Share-price movements can reflect changes in expected earnings and export access as well as political conflict. The reported federal investigation is not a finding of wrongdoing.

An investigation by The Wall Street Journal identified further corporate and regulatory vulnerabilities: Huang was reportedly not the only Nvidia executive whose personal relationships involved a representative of a major customer in the China-linked supply chain. Jay Puri, Nvidia's longtime global sales chief, who stepped back from day-to-day leadership in July, had been in a relationship for years with Senli Chen, chief growth officer at Super Micro Computer, and married her in California on March 7. Chen later joined ChronoScale, a cloud company spun out of another Nvidia partner, Applied Digital. Less than two weeks after their wedding, on March 19, federal prosecutors in the Southern District of New York, under Jay Clayton's signature, unsealed charges against Super Micro cofounder Yih-Shyan Liaw, a sales manager, and an outside broker alleging a conspiracy to smuggle servers containing advanced Nvidia chips into China worth more than $500 million—at least $510 million out of $2.5 billion ordered in 2024–2025. Chen denies involvement in chip purchases or exploitation of personal relationships; Super Micro says it was a victim of the alleged scheme. The FBI has nevertheless reportedly interviewed witnesses about the company's senior management.

Personal relationships and an indictment of other individuals do not establish that Chen or Puri participated in the alleged conduct. The denials and continuing investigations are relevant to assessing the claims.

Export regulators, rather than the press or corporate boards, delivered what may be the most consequential challenge to Nvidia's business model. Amid the leadership dispute, the U.S. Commerce Department, which had postponed Biden administration rules in May 2025, moved in November to close a major avenue through which Chinese companies accessed advanced computing resources. The Institute for Progress estimated that direct smuggling supplied around 22% of China's AI computing capacity this year and that legal remote access to Nvidia servers in Southeast Asian data centers accounted for another approximately 24%. During the first ten months of 2026, Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand imported $46 billion in AI servers from Taiwan, 16% of Taiwan's exports in that category, compared with $10 billion two years earlier. Nvidia booked $104 billion in 2025 revenue through its Singapore entity, including some sales to Taiwan and China. In mid-November—November 16 according to The Economist, which refers to three countries, and November 19 according to The Wall Street Journal, which identifies five including Malaysia—Washington required overseas operators using Nvidia equipment to obtain a U.S. license or discontinue service to Chinese customers within 90 days, by February. Nvidia instructed assembly partners including Dell and Super Micro to suspend server shipments to four countries, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Indonesia. Megaspeed was blacklisted on November 23. The immediate commercial effects included Dubai-based Damac abandoning a $49 million land deal in Malaysia and forfeiting its deposit, while banks froze approximately $480–500 million in credit, according to differing business-press estimates, for Singapore-based Aolani, which planned to lease computing capacity to ByteDance entities.

The different dates and country counts reported by the publications leave the exact scope of the rules uncertain, and the commercial impact depends on whether operators can obtain licenses.

White House support did not insulate Huang or the sector from an intense policy dispute in Washington. Nvidia's CEO faces hearings before joint House committees on December 1 and the Senate Banking Committee on December 3. At least eight Republican senators and as many House members have expressed dissatisfaction. Other developments include a November 19 subpoena from California's attorney general, reports of SEC scrutiny of the company's accounting following Bessent's remarks about potentially suspicious sales used to inflate revenue, and an S&P warning of a possible credit-rating downgrade over board independence and the absence of a successor. Business publications offer divergent interpretations. Venture investor Sarah Guo, writing in The Economist, defended Huang's return as necessary to sustain the global investment in open AI models—on which Nvidia plans to spend $26 billion over five years—against China's increasing open-source leadership. The Economist's own editorials and Financial Times columnists Andrew Hill and John Thornhill saw the episode as an erosion of corporate law, signaling that executives who control chips can override their own ethics codes. Marc Short, former chief of staff to Vice President Mike Pence, suggested that Trump's intervention could prompt the incoming Democratic-controlled Congress in January to enact comprehensive export controls with a veto-proof majority.

The open-model investment rationale and the ongoing regulatory inquiries point to competing policy priorities. The proposed congressional response remains conditional on legislative support.

AI Investment

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE • CAPITAL MARKETS
03

Scientific Breakthroughs and Financial Strain

The market turbulence surrounding Nvidia exposed a deeper tension in the AI economy between scientific advances and financial returns. On November 19, Anthropic reportedly completed the largest initial public offering in history, reaching a valuation above $1 trillion on annual revenue of approximately $80 billion. According to The Economist, even after shares fell below their $83 IPO price—briefly dropping below $74 on November 25, taking the valuation under $1 trillion, before closing at $82.79—employees' holdings were worth around $470 billion, with approximately $210 billion earmarked for charity. On November 20, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpoge announced that he had used Claude to find a proof of the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis and also claimed a proof of its generalized version, releasing 17 million lines of code in the Lean formal verification language. Two independent groups of specialists reportedly confirmed the logical validity of the Riemann hypothesis proof on November 21. Alpoge declined to publish the machine-generated textual explanation, which ran to more than a quarter of a million pages, while mathematicians in Oxford and London acknowledged that they did not yet understand the conceptual logic by which the machine had reached the result.

A formally verified mathematical result does not itself establish commercial returns, and independent understanding of the reported proof remains limited.

The reported breakthrough immediately raised concerns beyond academia. At the presentation of the Bank of England's financial stability report, Governor Andrew Bailey warned that exponential improvements in AI's mathematical capabilities could threaten the cryptography protecting bank data well before the 2030s, the period for which banks had been preparing in anticipation of quantum computers. Cryptographers quoted by Financial Times clarified, however, that the publicly presented results did not create an immediate risk of compromise. Physical scaling efforts were also accelerating. On November 25, FCC Chairman Brendan Carr approved the application by SpaceX, which had merged with xAI and raised almost $86 billion in a June IPO, to deploy a Starmind orbital constellation of one million space-based data centers by 2040, subject to launching the first satellite by November 2033, despite objections from Amazon and astronomers. In robotics, research firm Robocurve estimated that advanced language models' ability to control physical tasks was doubling every 54 days. The average manufacturing cost of Chinese company Unitree's humanoid robots fell 72% between 2023 and 2025, from 593,400 yuan (about $82,600) to 166,400 yuan, bringing retail prices for adult-sized robots from Unitree and AgiBot to roughly $50,000 versus $1 million two decades earlier.

The cryptographers cited do not regard the mathematical result as an immediate threat to banking security. The proposed orbital capacity also depends on future deployment.

On Wall Street and in energy markets, enthusiasm has given way to closer scrutiny of balance sheets. Financial Times columnist Robert Armstrong argues that the U.S. equity bubble lies less in elevated price-to-earnings multiples—the S&P 500's forward P/E ratio is 25% below its 2025 peak—than in potentially unrealistic earnings expectations. After S&P 500 earnings increased more than 30% in the third quarter, consensus forecasts assumed growth of 28% in the fourth quarter and 17% in 2027. The cyclically adjusted Shiller ratio was almost 40% above its level four years earlier. Shares of Dell, which doubled its revenue and margins from data-center server shipments, fell almost by half in two months as investors questioned the durability of extraordinary profits. Third Point founder Dan Loeb calculated that to justify projected capital expenditures of $5–7 trillion in 2025–2030, major technology companies and AI laboratories would need to generate $2–3 trillion in additional annual revenue. Leopold Aschenbrenner's leveraged AI investment fund, Situational Awareness, reported a third-quarter loss of 65%, although it remained up 88.5% year to date through September. Against that backdrop, investors shifted from semiconductors toward more defensive software companies with established customers. Cybersecurity companies Palo Alto Networks (+89%) and CrowdStrike (+72%, following its announcement of a $44 billion Zscaler acquisition) were among the year's leaders. Adobe and Salesforce lost more than 30%, while Germany's SAP was reportedly considering eliminating at least 10,000 of its 113,000 jobs to reorganize around AI.

Falling valuations and quarterly losses do not establish that all AI investments will prove unprofitable. Earnings expectations and financing conditions remain decisive.

Independent cloud providers, often called neoclouds, and energy-equipment suppliers appear particularly exposed. Nscale, favored by the British government, had a $106 billion contract portfolio, more than four-fifths of which came from Microsoft and Anthropic, but earned only $308 million in revenue during the first nine months of 2026 against $5.5 billion in debt. Its British data-center projects encountered setbacks due to high electricity prices, bureaucracy, and grid-connection queues extending into the 2030s. Nvidia itself committed up to $32 billion in guarantees for neocloud capacity, yet CoreWeave shares fell by more than half in six months, Australia's Firmus canceled its October IPO, and the $500 billion financing program Huang had been preparing with Wall Street in August stalled. Cleanview calculations reported by The Wall Street Journal showed that of 90 gigawatts of gas-fired generation planned for independent and non-hyperscale data centers, only 2.7 gigawatts had been built or were under construction; SoftBank had already canceled some reservations for GE Vernova turbines. Since early October, Bloom Energy shares lost 44%, Caterpillar 33%, and GE Vernova 24%. China experienced a parallel overcapacity problem. In response to U.S. sanctions, local authorities built more than 500 data centers, with another approximately 400 under construction, primarily using less powerful Huawei and Cambricon chips. Nationwide utilization averaged only 20–30%, prompting cities such as Shantou to subsidize exports of AI tokens to overseas customers.

A gap between contracted capacity and completed infrastructure does not mean every project will be canceled. Utilization and profitability depend on future demand, delivery schedules, and operating costs.

The investment race has also distorted related markets, from electronics and industrial metals to private investment funds. Exceptional data-center demand for memory—SK Hynix sold out its DRAM and HBM allocation through 2029, and Micron secured $150 billion in contracts—led to a tenfold increase in the price of some memory chips over a year. Apple raised MacBook prices by $100–500 and the iPhone 18 Pro by $100. Amazon Echo Dot prices increased 60% and Xbox Series S prices 33%; Sony PS5 sales fell 35% and Nintendo Switch 2 sales 41%. The shortages also encouraged gray-market brokers and thefts of memory from warehouses. South Korea's chip export boom was expected to push its current-account surplus toward $500 billion this year, compared with a record $123 billion a year earlier, even as the Kospi stock index fell nearly 50% from its June peak. The Bank of Korea raised its policy rate again to 3.25% on November 26. Copper became a proxy investment in AI even though data centers consume only about 1% of global copper supply. On November 16, the Shanghai Futures Exchange asked traders to settle contracts in cash because of physical shortages; within days, London prices fell 6–10% alongside the technology sell-off, potentially discouraging investment in new mines ahead of a 10-million-ton shortfall projected by S&P Global for 2040. A similar imbalance in capital markets has forced pension funds and university endowments to sell stakes in older venture funds at an average 32% discount to net asset value, according to Campbell Lutyens. Direct stakes from 2021 software funding rounds were trading at a median 61% discount, according to PitchBook. Buyout giants such as Apollo, whose hybrid division surpassed $100 billion, KKR, and Blackstone have turned toward hybrid financing to provide liquidity.

The price of copper also reflects physical supply constraints, while the discounts on older private assets need not apply uniformly to all investment portfolios.

04

Tariffs, Interest Rates, and Generational Divides

The political backdrop to market volatility was governments' struggle to meet voters' principal demand: a lower cost of living. Reports on the November U.S. midterm elections described a decisive Democratic victory, with a lead of more than 80 seats in the House, control of the Senate with 55 Democrats and aligned independents, and six additional state government trifectas, bringing the total to 22. These included Arizona, Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, alongside victories in the Texas governor's race, a U.S. Senate seat, and the Texas House. Senator Chris Van Hollen openly challenged Senate leader Chuck Schumer. Exit polling indicated that 65–66% of voters, approximately two-thirds in surveys, believed Trump's tariffs had harmed the economy, while 33–35% of his 2024 supporters did not vote.

Polling about tariffs indicates dissatisfaction, but election outcomes alone cannot isolate one economic policy as the decisive cause.

Nevertheless, on November 25, when the Commerce Department reported PCE inflation accelerating to 3.8%, core PCE rising from 3.1% to 3.3%, and revised third-quarter GDP growth of 4.3%, the Trump administration proposed a new wave of tariffs against 14 trading partners: China, Vietnam, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Bangladesh, Cambodia, the European Union, and Switzerland. The White House invoked Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, alleging that trading partners subsidized excess production capacity. In Singapore's case, the criticism focused on policies encouraging savings that allegedly suppressed domestic demand, even though the United States runs a $33 billion trade surplus with Singapore. Proposed rates reached 20% for China, with a threatened doubling to 40% if the truce collapsed before Trump's December meeting with Xi in Miami, up to 35% for Vietnam, and 25% for India. Yale University's Budget Lab estimated that this would raise the average effective U.S. tariff rate from 11.4% to 13.9%, adding 0.3% to consumer prices according to The Wall Street Journal's account, or as much as 0.5% according to Financial Times.

The price estimates are forecasts based on the proposed tariffs, and their effects depend on the final rates and on how businesses and consumers respond.

Business publications emphasized two complementary motives for the tariff initiative. The Washington Post editorial board and Wall Street Journal writers focused on the president's political determination to restore tariffs after the Supreme Court struck down his emergency duties in February and July tariffs justified as measures against forced labor became embroiled in litigation. Financial Times pointed to a fiscal calculation. The Economist reported a budget-deficit figure of 173 billion for the previous fiscal year, while Financial Times estimated net receipts of $264 billion in the previous year. The new tariffs, together with the July duties, could recover up to 80% of revenue through 2036 that was lost following February's Supreme Court decision. Meanwhile, nominal economic growth of 6% at an annual rate and surging prices for electrical equipment left new Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh in a difficult position. Despite a flat Treasury yield curve and warnings of recession from investors such as Bill Ackman, LSEG data put the probability of the Fed raising rates above the current 3.75–4% range at its December 8–9 meeting at 97.9%.

The expected rate increase reflects market pricing rather than a completed policy decision. Tariff revenues also depend on the measures surviving legal challenges and on future trade volumes.

Economic constraints also limit the agenda of the victorious Democrats. The Economist notes that real disposable income per capita in the United States has risen 13% since the end of 2019. Mortgage rates, driven by higher term premiums on government debt, have placed particular pressure on household budgets, adding about $2,500 a year to payments on a new mortgage, according to Yale's Budget Lab. Simply repealing tariffs, restoring Affordable Care Act subsidies, and reinstating curtailed Medicaid programs would cost the federal budget $4–5 trillion over ten years without offsets, according to the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget. A broad Medicare for All reform was estimated at $25–35 trillion by the same committee and $47.4 trillion by the White House Council of Economic Advisers. Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed and Trump nevertheless publicly entertained the idea of a deal under the Trumpcare label. A proposed 10% cap on credit-card interest rates amid record balances of $1.3 trillion was similarly contentious. Industry estimates suggested that it could eliminate access to credit cards for up to 88% of open accounts, redirecting borrowers to installment-payment services such as Klarna and Affirm. A test of Congress's willingness to address supply constraints came on November 19, when the Senate passed the bipartisan BAAJA permitting reform bill by 62 votes to 33. Trump, former first-term officials William Barr and Dan Brouillette writing in The Washington Post, and The Economist supported it, with the latter citing C2ES and Rhodium Group estimates of a 36% increase in clean power generation and a 3–4% reduction in electricity bills. Thirty Democratic senators voted against it over concessions to gas pipelines and limits on environmental litigation.

The fiscal and credit-access estimates depend on the design of the policies and how they are financed. The permitting bill also faces disagreements over environmental protections.

In Europe, the cost-of-living crisis has intensified generational tensions and fiscal instability. On November 26, eurozone leaders agreed on Spanish economist Pablo Hernández de Cos, head of the Bank for International Settlements, to become ECB president in February 2027. The package with France and Germany allocated key board positions to France's Laurence Boone and Germany's Markus Brunnermeier; the Netherlands withdrew Klaas Knot at the last moment. Current ECB president Christine Lagarde was set to leave nine months early to participate openly in debates ahead of France's April presidential election. Eurozone inflation reached 4% in October, pointing toward a possible ECB rate increase to 2.75% on December 17. France's deficit reached 5.5% of GDP, real wages fell for six consecutive years (down 1.3%), purchasing power contracted for a second year, and ten-year bond yields exceeded 5% in October for the first time since 2002. Marine Le Pen, leading polls with 30–35%, promised to cut France's EU budget contribution by €11 billion in 2027, end aid to Ukraine after six months, and place national law above EU law. Her ally Jordan Bardella was again responding to Mediapart and Le Monde reporting about alleged antisemitic correspondence from 13 years earlier. The weakened center was divided between Édouard Philippe (9–10%) and liberal Cannes Mayor David Lisnard (11–12%).

The projected rate increase and proposed French policies remain subject to future institutional decisions and election results.

The generational divide was even more pronounced in Spain ahead of the November 29 election. The Economist, Financial Times, and reporting from Barcelona highlighted the paradox facing Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez: despite economic growth at twice the eurozone average, he risked losing office to a coalition of the conservative People's Party and the far-right Vox. Growth was driven in large part by a larger workforce. The foreign-born population rose by 3.8 million to 10 million, with the total population rising by 3.2 million and an amnesty for one million undocumented immigrants. Real wages and productivity have barely grown since 2018; the Bank of Spain estimated a housing shortfall of 750,000 units, no budget had been approved since 2023, and corruption scandals affected the prime minister's associates. Financial Times identified pension and property disparities as a major source of dissatisfaction among men younger than 45. According to OECD calculations, Spanish public pensions replace 80.4% of gross wages, versus 56.6% in France and 44.7% in Britain. The average pension rose to €1,578 a month, €357 above the minimum wage, and 47% of households own a second home. Younger voters who cannot access mortgages and are concerned about migrant arrivals in Ceuta and the Canary Islands increasingly support Santiago Abascal's anti-immigration Vox. In Sweden, Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson assembled a center-right minority coalition on November 26, leaving the far-right Sweden Democrats outside the cabinet.

Population growth, housing shortages, and pension disparities can contribute to electoral dissatisfaction without determining the result of the Spanish election.

In Britain, Labour Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who replaced Keir Starmer in July, was balancing growth in business services against deteriorating fiscal discipline. Services lifted third-quarter GDP growth to 1.9% at an annual rate and offset a £58 billion goods-trade deficit. London sought an EU reset under a Mark 4 model: the November 20 announcement included removal of veterinary checks, linking carbon markets, and visa mobility for 200,000 young people in exchange for dynamic alignment with EU regulations. Brussels also wanted tuition reductions for European students within six months. Meanwhile, social spending continued to rise. The Economist projected that annual disability and incapacity benefit spending in England, Scotland, and Wales would reach £87 billion by 2031, or 2.4% of GDP versus 1.4% before the pandemic. The share of the working-age population receiving benefits for anxiety and depression rose from 0.3% in 2009 to 1.7%, with those conditions accounting for 39% of all Personal Independence Payment claims. Fearing a parliamentary revolt, Burnham refrained from steep cuts. An attempt to raise £1.5 billion by doubling the bank surcharge from 3% to 6%, costing JPMorgan alone £120 million ($157 million), reportedly prompted private opposition from Jamie Dimon and internal discussions about possibly scaling back investments in Bournemouth and Canary Wharf. The London Stock Exchange also risked losing another major company: U.S. tobacco group Altria was exploring a purchase of Imperial Brands, valued at £26 billion–$30 billion, involving Japan Tobacco, whose shares had dropped a quarter in a month after the nationalization of its Russian factories at a cost of $8.6 billion. Italy's UniCredit received final clearance from Polish authorities to gain control of Germany's Commerzbank in a transaction valued at €40.6–45 billion.

The projected benefit bill and possible reductions in bank investment are conditional, and the proposed corporate acquisitions remain distinct from fiscal policy.

War and Energy

ENERGY MARKETS • INTERNATIONAL SECURITY
05

Iran, Ukraine, and the Changing Geography of War

Inflationary pressure and fiscal risks are being reinforced by two prolonged conflicts that have entered a phase of asymmetric attrition. The U.S. and Israeli war against Iran, which began on February 28, developed into a severe naval blockade after an April ceasefire broke down in July. Expectations in Washington and Gulf capitals suggest that it could last until 2029. Reporting from Iran illustrates the economic and social strain: the blockade deprives Tehran of $3–4 billion in oil revenue each month, the rial has fallen to 2.9 million per dollar (approximately 300,000 tomans versus 32,000 in 2015), and official food inflation has exceeded 110%. Iranian economist Hadi Kahalzadeh estimates GDP will contract 4–6% this year. President Masoud Pezeshkian acknowledged a lack of funds in a televised address and introduced rolling power outages lasting up to two hours a day. Reports differ on the extent of the food crisis. The Washington Post described millions of people being removed from food assistance and limits on purchases of subsidized bread in at least three provinces. The Wall Street Journal, citing Iran's flour and bread council, reported that allocations of low-cost flour had already been reduced in seven of 31 provinces, although officials refused to characterize the measures as rationing. Bakers in Zanjan went on strike, oil workers rallied in Abadan, and Pezeshkian faced criticism from reformists and hard-liners alike.

The duration of the blockade and the magnitude of the economic contraction are projections; differing accounts of subsidy restrictions also leave their precise extent uncertain.

Economic pressure has not compelled Tehran to retreat, and the conflict has extended across the Persian Gulf. On November 26, a drone strike against air-defense facilities in Kuwait killed two Italian technicians from Germany's Rheinmetall who were servicing the Skyguard system and injured Kuwaiti and Pakistani citizens. Rome and Kuwait City blamed Iran; Tehran denied involvement and suggested that a false-flag operation might have occurred. During the same period, Yemen's Houthis advanced toward Aden, attacked Aramco's Jizan refinery, killing a Nepalese worker and shutting down some capacity, claimed an attack on the Ras Tanura terminal that prompted Riyadh to shift shipments toward Yanbu, and twice struck the headquarters tower of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund (PIF) in Riyadh, injuring 11 people on Tuesday. Following the October cancellation of the Future Investment Initiative, known as Davos in the Desert, the kingdom was preparing to postpone January's Future Minerals Forum. The Gulf Cooperation Council economies were experiencing substantial losses. Container traffic through the Strait of Hormuz fell from 20 ships a day to fewer than one, with 227 vessels since March according to Lloyd's List. Regional inflation doubled; the IMF expected contraction in five of the six monarchies, including 14.5% in Qatar and 3.3% in Saudi Arabia. Allianz estimated the bloc's combined 2026–2030 deficit at $475 billion. The $910 billion PIF, following losses of 2.2% in 2024 and twice that in 2025—about $30 billion in write-downs by Tim Callen's calculations, amid the collapse of LIV Golf, reductions to NEOM, and debt nearing $200 billion—turned to external borrowing. Oman, geographically outside the strait, was an exception, with 4.5% growth and a 28% increase in port traffic. Trump had nevertheless threatened Oman with bombing in August over its perceived closeness to Tehran.

The allegations concerning the Kuwait strike remain disputed. Regional economic performance also differs, as illustrated by Oman’s growth and port traffic.

The oil market approached the November 29 OPEC+ meeting in a paradoxical position. Although Brent was trading around $96 per barrel on November 26 and WTI at $84–85 amid the risk of Middle East escalation, the International Energy Agency's balance indicated that the global market had already moved into surplus in October because of higher production in the Americas. The cartel was also facing divisions. The UAE left OPEC in the spring, in April or May according to differing accounts, and increased output above four million barrels a day. Iraq rejected the results of the first independent capacity audit and threatened to leave, while Russia produced only about 8.4 million barrels a day in October against a quota of nearly ten million, following Ukrainian attacks on its refining and export infrastructure. High aviation fuel costs were meanwhile putting pressure on vulnerable airlines. Norwegian low-cost carrier Norse Atlantic halted flights on Thanksgiving and was preparing for bankruptcy, becoming the twelfth European airline to collapse or seek creditor protection since the war in Iran began.

The estimated global oil surplus could ease some price pressures even while disruption to particular routes, production facilities, and airlines remains severe.

In Europe, Russia's war against Ukraine, approaching its sixth year, expanded geographically in an unexpected way. Reporting by The Economist, Financial Times, and The Wall Street Journal describes the technological context for Ukraine's November operations. On October 14, SpaceX's Starshield satellite network, which uses the same infrastructure as Starlink, reportedly restricted connectivity for Ukrainian high-speed drones above 160 kilometers per hour outside standard Pentagon procedures. After a temporary lifting of the limit at the U.S. Defense Department's request, restrictions were reportedly imposed again in early November over occupied territory. Elon Musk denied intervening, and Marco Rubio said the White House was not involved. The restrictions nevertheless disrupted Ukraine's Mid-Strike campaign against Russian rear areas at a time when Kyiv and Western agencies estimated Russian casualties at about 54,400 in a month, with total casualties since the war began exceeding 1.5 million according to CSIS. U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth was reportedly delaying deliveries of previously approved Patriot missiles. Ukraine responded with communications-relay drones, struck the Crimean Bridge on November 17, reportedly sank the Merkury corvette with maritime drones and damaged a cargo ship in Libya's Benghazi on November 22, and set fire to the Sparta 2 in the Baltic, which subsequently drifted into Finnish waters.

The accounts describe restrictions and military responses, but the denials leave the attribution of the decision unresolved.

Ukraine's demonstration of operational autonomy quickly exposed political divisions within Europe and increased maritime risks. On November 26, Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni publicly criticized Kyiv for extending hostilities into the Mediterranean, noting Libya's proximity to Lampedusa, 180 miles away, and its role as a major irregular migration corridor. Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini called such operations unacceptable without prior coordination. To the north, the Baltic Sea, through which more than 60% of Russia's seaborne oil exports travel, was already experiencing heightened tensions. Following 14 detentions of shadow-fleet vessels since late 2024, Russia deployed electronic-warfare equipment to Kaliningrad and conducted more than 260 military tanker escorts, reportedly sometimes involving nuclear-capable vessels such as the Soobrazitelny corvette. In September it delivered a written warning to NATO of its readiness to conduct preventive nuclear strikes if Baltic shipping were threatened. An October 27 call to Vladimir Putin from Finnish President Alexander Stubb and Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre eased tensions, until renewed incidents involving the drifting Sparta 2, three Russian Kh-101 cruise missiles falling in Poland on November 20, a drone flight on November 24, and Moscow's threats against Estonia over the planned December 1 border closure.

Political criticism and increased maritime activity indicate heightened risk, not certainty of further escalation or of a particular military response.

Russia has also reportedly intensified sabotage behind NATO's lines. According to IISS, the number of sabotage incidents in Europe increased 14% over the past year, while arson and explosions doubled, including an August incident involving an explosive drone at Leipzig/Halle cargo airport. CSIS counted more than 100 attacks and plots since February 2022. The share of attacks targeting facilities supporting Ukraine rose from 20% in 2025 to 60% this year, prompting Britain's MI5 to issue an urgent warning to defense contractors. European construction groups CRH, ACS, and Vinci were positioning themselves for €620 billion in defense and dual-use infrastructure investment over a decade, according to BCG estimates: €320 billion for military facilities and €300 billion for civilian resilience in Germany, France, and Poland. Actual EU budget negotiations for 2028–2034 remained difficult, however, with Paris and Berlin divided over France's insistence on buying weapons made in Europe. Proposed defense spending had already been cut from €131 billion to €92 billion, a figure German Chancellor Friedrich Merz still considered excessive. Meanwhile, Washington was taking a pragmatic approach to energy assets. On November 25, a consortium involving Trump donor Todd Boehly's Eldridge, the U.S. government agency DFC, UAE-based Catena, and Xtellus agreed to acquire Lukoil's foreign assets valued at $21.4 billion through a $10 billion noncash securities swap, pending OFAC approval, after the U.S. Treasury rejected earlier prospective buyers Gunvor and Carlyle.

Projected infrastructure investment is not equivalent to authorized spending. The disagreement over the EU budget and the pending OFAC decision illustrate that the eventual outcomes remain open.

Global Order

DIPLOMACY • GLOBAL TRADE • INSTITUTIONS
06

Multilateral Institutions and New Centers of Influence

Pressure on the established international order extended beyond the two wars. The November 19 trilateral summit between Xi Jinping, Donald Trump, and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on the sidelines of APEC drew sharply contrasting assessments. A letter from a former senior U.S. diplomat in The Washington Post cited the newspaper's coverage of the summit's 226-word document as evidence of further erosion of the postwar order. The Economist viewed the meeting as largely theatrical, judging Trump's attempt to initiate three-way nuclear negotiations after New START expired in February a failure. China had 620 nuclear warheads and had increased uranium enrichment by 50% since early 2025. Pentagon estimates suggested that it sought parity at 1,780 deployed warheads by 2035 and was constructing up to 350 underground ICBM silos. Beijing officially retained its no-first-use doctrine, although deployment of early-warning systems suggested preparations for a launch-on-warning posture intended to deter U.S. intervention in a Taiwan scenario. On November 25, Kim Jong Un inspected two nuclear-material production facilities in North Korea, one new and one being expanded. Behind China's displays of strength, signs of internal uncertainty persisted. A CSIS report counted 101 senior People's Liberation Army commanders purged or missing from early 2022 to early 2026, affecting every position on the Central Military Commission and in theater commands, and 38% of deputies. On November 24, Shanghai party chief and Politburo member Chen Jining came under anticorruption investigation, becoming the fourth Politburo member removed since 2022. These developments unsettled foreign businesses amid economic slowdown and the bankruptcy of major asset manager CreditEase, which owed $4.4 billion to 100,000 investors. Beijing was also increasing information activity ahead of local elections in Taiwan, where the island's 23 million residents generate more than 10% of worldwide Threads traffic. Doublethink Lab researchers linked 549 inauthentic accounts to the Chinese content operation Borderless Group, which produced more than 460,000 posts. Taiwanese intelligence agencies identified more than 2.5 million pieces of suspicious content over nine months.

The divergent interpretations of the Shenzhen meeting and China’s stated no-first-use policy leave the implications for future diplomacy uncertain. The reported military projections are not confirmed future outcomes.

The failure of COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, also illustrated the strain on multilateral institutions. For the first time in 17 years, a UN climate conference ended without an agreement after the EU, Britain, Canada, and small island states refused to sign a document without language on phasing out fossil fuels, which Russia, Saudi Arabia, and India opposed. In global health, international health assistance declined by 21% in 2025, from $49.6 billion to $39.1 billion according to IHME. Amid vaccine skepticism among U.S. health officials under Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who was also working with White House official Stephen Miller to classify 60% of the U.S. diet as ultraprocessed food, the United States, Bolivia, and Mexico officially lost their measles elimination status, following Britain, Spain, and Canada. U.S. courts remained an important check on executive pressure. On November 25, federal Judge Loren AliKhan blocked FCC Chairman Brendan Carr's expedited review of the licenses of eight ABC television stations, finding it constituted political retaliation for criticism of the president on Jimmy Kimmel's show. The court left in place FCC inquiries into staffing policies and The View.

The absence of a climate agreement does not establish that every multilateral process has ceased functioning; the U.S. court ruling also demonstrates continuing judicial oversight.

Fragmentation of global supply chains creates opportunities for countries in the Global South, though it does not guarantee gains. McKinsey Global Institute estimates that approximately 45% of world trade, worth more than $8 trillion, involves critical goods. China's dependence on critical imports exceeds 60%, of which 7% comes from concentrated and geopolitically unfriendly suppliers, including Australian ore and Japanese photoresists. The corresponding U.S. figures are 42% and 5% for the combination of three vulnerabilities. Trump's tariff offensive, however, risks alienating even U.S. allies. Mexico overtook China in this changing geography, accounting for 17% of U.S. imports, worth $480 billion in the first nine months of 2026, including nearly 40% of U.S. server imports in September. Yet server assembly for Texas data centers leaves only 7–8% in local value added in Mexico, compared with nearly 40% in automotive production, as imports of Taiwanese components tripled to $44 billion in six months. In West Africa, Guinea's military leader General Mamadi Doumbouya has leveraged competition among major powers. The launch of the giant Simandou iron ore mine made Guinea the world's third-largest exporter in October. At least 75% of the ore goes to China, while the Chinese-built railway uses American locomotives and French automation; the IMF approved a $425 million package on November 24. This has encouraged the United States to promote the competing Kon Kweni project through Liberia, although railway monopolist ArcelorMittal Liberia has already lobbied the Trump administration to slow its competitor. In the South Atlantic, Argentine President Javier Milei, facing domestic economic stagnation, pursued legal action against London. He was awaiting a November 28 decision by the UN's International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea in Hamburg on a possible suspension of the Falkland Islands Sea Lion oil project.

Expanded trade and investment can bring opportunities without increasing domestic value added proportionally. The competing infrastructure projects and pending legal ruling remain uncertain.

Taken together, the corporate controversies, fiscal constraints, military developments, and court decisions on both sides of the Atlantic offer little basis for expecting a swift return to earlier stability. Technology companies can no longer necessarily fund multitrillion-dollar investments solely on expectations of indefinite growth, Western governments face constraints on using cheap debt to ease social discontent, and major powers are struggling to contain regional conflicts and trade wars. The world approaches 2027 with central banks favoring tighter policy, a divided U.S. Congress, intensifying electoral competition in France, heavily indebted Gulf countries, and a widening gap between populist promises and financial realities. The central question in the coming months may be less about the next promise to make a deal than about who bears the costs of accumulated imbalances: technology-sector creditors, consumers in economies affected by tariffs and inflation, or the institutions responsible for upholding the rule of law.

The outcome depends on future policy, investment performance, and institutional responses. The observed constraints do not preclude renewed cooperation or successful adjustment.
