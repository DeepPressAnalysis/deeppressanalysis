SOVEREIGN TECH SHIFT

EXPORT CONTROLS • CLOUD SOVEREIGNTY

SOVEREIGN TECH SHIFT: DIGITAL BOUNDARIES

Technological Sovereignty: Redistribution of Power Through the Computing Stack

U.S. conditional licensing for advanced AI accelerators effectively establishes a new jurisdictional boundary by ending China's automatic commercial access to certain critical computing capacity. On January 13, 2026, BIS shifted export-license applications for Nvidia H200, AMD MI325X, and comparable accelerators destined for China to individual review subject to security requirements. Applicants must demonstrate, among other things, that a shipment will not displace capacity intended for U.S. customers, that the Chinese buyer has implemented export-compliance procedures, and that the product underwent independent testing in the United States. This is not a conventional broad trade liberalization: Washington has made access to computing hardware contingent on administrative authorization.

Counter-argument: Case-by-case review is not a general ban. The ability of exporters to seek licenses may permit continued access and would weaken a claim that the authorization regime has eliminated shipments altogether.

The relevant sovereign boundary is therefore not drawn around China's territory but around the right to deploy computing resources within Chinese infrastructure. An export license functions as a checkpoint: the product exists, demand exists, and its producer is prepared to sell, but final authorization remains with the technology's country of origin. This is an institutional inference from the BIS regime, not a claim that H200 exports are universally prohibited. The January policy, in fact, replaced a presumption of denial with case-by-case review for a defined category of accelerators.

Counter-argument: The policy still permits case-by-case approval of covered accelerators. Approved shipments would limit an interpretation that administrative control is equivalent to an outright export prohibition.

China's response is not a symmetrical licensing regime but accelerated development of domestic computing hardware. According to IDC data reported by Reuters on October 13, Nvidia shipped 678,000 data-center AI accelerators to China in the first half of 2026, down 44% year over year, and held 34% of the market. Huawei shipped 572,000 units and reached a 28% share. Shipments of non-GPU accelerators increased by nearly 80% and exceeded conventional GPUs by volume. These figures do not establish Chinese computing self-sufficiency, but they indicate a material reallocation of domestic demand alongside export restrictions.

Counter-argument: The higher share of domestic shipments does not establish comparable performance or the ability to satisfy aggregate demand. The continued 34% Nvidia share also weakens any conclusion that imported accelerators have already been displaced.

The European Union is building a different kind of boundary. On June 3, 2026, the European Commission presented its European Technological Sovereignty package, including Chips Act 2.0 and a proposed Cloud and AI Development Act. The latter would establish an EU-wide framework for evaluating the sovereignty of cloud and AI infrastructure and simplify data-center deployment. As of October 14, the measure remained a legislative proposal, not an EU-wide law already in force.

Counter-argument: The Cloud and AI Development Act had not yet been adopted as law by October 14. The proposed evaluation system cannot be treated as an already enforceable requirement across every EU cloud provider.

A procurement mechanism, however, was already operating. In April, the Commission awarded four suppliers framework contracts for sovereign cloud services worth up to €180 million over six years. The Cloud Sovereignty Framework governed eligibility. On June 1, the Commission disclosed its 48 criteria across eight categories and its Sovereignty Effectiveness Assurance Levels (SEAL): SEAL-2 refers to data sovereignty, SEAL-3 to technological autonomy, and SEAL-4 to full sovereignty.

The institutional distinction is clearer than the official terminology alone suggests. The United States regulates permission to export computing hardware; China develops national alternatives while regulating software; and the EU makes the jurisdictional acceptability of cloud services a condition of access to public procurement. In 2026, a digital boundary can be understood as a combination of permissions: buying a chip, securing megawatts, processing data, qualifying for public-sector cloud contracts, and retaining access to the software stack.

Counter-argument: The Cloud and AI Development Act is still a proposal, and BIS now allows some applications to be evaluated individually. Those qualifications limit how far the new boundaries can be described as uniform prohibitions.

HARDWARE AND ENERGY: THE PHYSICAL CONTROL LAYER

Cables and Data Centers

Subsea infrastructure has become a subject of strategic control rather than only a commercial transport service. According to the EU Blue Economy Observatory, based on 2025–2026 data, Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon control approximately 71% of utilized international subsea telecommunications capacity. On transatlantic routes, that share approaches 90%. These figures refer to utilized capacity, not ownership of 90% of physical cables and not 90% of global Internet traffic.

Counter-argument: Utilization shares do not represent the proportion of cables legally owned by these firms, nor their share of all Internet traffic. Those distinctions limit conclusions about their direct ownership of global telecommunications routes.

The distinction matters without eliminating the strategic relevance of concentration. When four U.S. technology companies account for most of the utilized capacity on a major route, potential public-sector dependencies begin before the cloud layer, at the physical paths by which data crosses the ocean. Institutionally, content providers have also become cable owners or major users of backbone infrastructure.

Counter-argument: The 71% and nearly 90% figures measure utilized capacity, not exclusive ownership of physical cables. They cannot, by themselves, establish direct corporate control over every route or over public policy.

The EU is bringing the state back into an infrastructure layer long treated principally as a commercial telecommunications business. On February 5, 2026, the European Commission introduced a Cable Security Toolbox and a list of Cable Projects of European Interest, while allocating €347 million to strategic subsea projects, including €20 million for stronger repair capacity. The Commission says subsea cables carry approximately 99% of intercontinental Internet traffic, and it explicitly linked these measures to risks of deliberate damage and sabotage.

Counter-argument: The €347 million package and €20 million repair allocation establish a policy response, not demonstrated protection against every cable disruption. The impact depends on implementation and actual repair capacity.

This represents a digital application of a conventional sovereign responsibility. A state that cannot map cable routes, secure landing stations, replace damaged segments quickly, or access repair vessels has limited operational control over its international connectivity, regardless of its digital-independence legislation. European funding for repair capabilities acknowledges this physical exposure.

Counter-argument: The Commission has allocated funding to security and repairs. If those measures provide effective redundancy and timely restoration, the practical consequences of dependence on any individual cable route would be reduced.

The Energy Constraint: AI Power

The next constraint is measured in megawatts rather than fiber-optic strands. On September 30, 2026, Constellation and Amazon announced a 20-year agreement involving Calvert Cliffs. It supports more than $3 billion in infrastructure investment and approximately 190 MW of additional nuclear capacity scheduled for 2030–2032. The agreement covers 690 MW of electricity, including the planned increment. Importantly, all generation from the plant continues to enter the shared PJM grid. Saying that Amazon "bought a nuclear power plant" would therefore be inaccurate. More precisely, a hyperscaler's long-term demand provides financial and investment certainty for upgrades to nuclear generation.

Counter-argument: The additional 190 MW is planned for 2030–2032 and remains part of PJM supply. These facts qualify any claim that Amazon has already secured exclusive new physical generation.

Six days later, on October 6, Google and Constellation announced a larger arrangement: a 20-year agreement intended to add 890 MW of nuclear capacity through uprates at 11 Constellation reactors in Illinois, Pennsylvania, and New Jersey. Initial increases are expected from 2028. Constellation projects more than $4.3 billion in investment. The companies also signed a 15-year agreement covering 2.7 GW of electricity supply and a five-year technology partnership under which the utility will use Google Cloud and Gemini Enterprise.

This arrangement gives the "the stack is the state" proposition a concrete, qualified institutional dimension. Google is both a major prospective buyer of electricity and a supplier of digital services to its producer. The company does not thereby gain legal control of PJM or the nuclear plants. It does, however, create a reciprocal commercial dependency: the generator benefits from predictable demand to support capital investment, while the hyperscaler depends on a long-range generation plan. This links investment cycles in computing and electricity more closely than an ordinary short-term power purchase would.

Counter-argument: Electricity remains supplied through PJM, and Google does not own or govern the generating assets as a result of these agreements. That shared-grid arrangement limits conclusions about corporate control of sovereign electricity infrastructure.

U.S. federal regulators have treated the interconnection of AI infrastructure as a systemwide issue. On June 18, FERC required all six regional transmission operators under its jurisdiction to justify or amend their rules for connecting data centers and other large loads. The commission also called for cost-recovery agreements, increased transparency, and safeguards against speculative applications, with the aim of preventing ordinary ratepayers from bearing the cost of grid expansions when a proposed data center is never built.

Counter-argument: The June 18 action required operators to justify or amend interconnection practices; it did not establish in the cited account that all six operators had already implemented the same final cost-allocation rules.

On September 29, during consideration of PJM reliability arrangements, FERC Commissioner Mark Rosner supported assigning costs associated with capacity shortages to areas experiencing rapid data-center load growth. He also said emergency load-curtailment rules should avoid disconnecting homes, hospitals, and schools before data centers. This was a commissioner's position, not a standalone federal law establishing a mandatory shutdown order. It illustrates an emerging regulatory dispute over priority access to scarce electrical capacity.

Counter-argument: The proposed priority for homes, hospitals, and schools was expressed by a FERC commissioner rather than enacted as a standalone federal law. The operational result depends on the rules eventually adopted by grid operators and regulators.

Concentrated Infrastructure and Physical Vulnerability

In October, Russia experienced a demonstration of the physical location of concentrated domestic cloud infrastructure. On October 8, Reuters reported a strike on Yandex's Sasovo site; the resulting fire halted a facility that housed tens of thousands of servers and two of the company's three principal supercomputers used for AI tasks. On October 11, Yandex reported suspending operations at a facility in the Vladimir region after another attack; a site in the Kaluga region had been affected earlier. On October 13, the Kremlin confirmed that the government was discussing problems at Yandex following strikes on three data centers.

Counter-argument: The reporting identifies interruptions at concentrated physical facilities, but it does not quantify the resulting loss of nationwide cloud capacity. The severity of a broader systemic effect therefore cannot be inferred from facility outages alone.

A broader assertion—that these attacks had destroyed a substantial part of Russia's disinformation infrastructure—remains unverified. Public charts showing lower activity, cited by proponents of that interpretation, could instead have reflected a decline in Yandex web-crawler activity, according to specialist observers, while distributed propaganda networks remained active. The physical vulnerability of data centers is supported by reporting; a comparable effect on coordinated bot networks is not established.

Counter-argument: The activity charts might warrant a stronger inference if the observed decline could be connected specifically to coordinated propaganda systems rather than Yandex crawlers. The information presented does not establish that link.

The physical structure of digital power can be described in four layers: cables create routes, data centers concentrate computation, power grids determine the capacity ceiling, and an attack or accident can turn a logical architecture into a geographic set of points of failure. The resilience of a state without redundancy across those layers may be limited during a significant crisis.

Counter-argument: The EU repair funding and FERC interconnection safeguards described above could reduce exposure if successfully implemented. A geographically concentrated asset is not necessarily a single point of systemwide failure if effective redundancy exists.

THE SPLINTERNET

LOCALIZATION • AI REGULATION • NATIONAL STACKS
03

THE SPLINTERNET: REGULATORY AND DATA BOUNDARIES

Localization and Selective Isolation

Russia's splinternet architecture in 2025–2026 developed through selective availability as well as blocking. In September 2025, the Ministry of Digital Development confirmed a whitelist of resources intended to remain accessible during mobile Internet restrictions. It included Gosuslugi, Yandex, VKontakte, Odnoklassniki, Mail.ru, MAX, Ozon, Wildberries, Avito, Rutube, the Mir payment system, presidential and government websites, and other Russian services. The ministry said the list would be updated regularly.

Counter-argument: The announced whitelist was designed for periods of mobile Internet restrictions. It provides evidence of selective service availability, not permanent exclusion of every service outside the list across all networks.

The scope requires precision: Russia did not permanently place the entire national Internet under a universal allowlist. The arrangement applies during restrictions on mobile data transmission. Nevertheless, it shows that an operator can limit general Internet access while maintaining a state-designated set of essential services. That is a different control mechanism from blocking individual URLs after the fact.

Counter-argument: The whitelist operates during mobile-data restrictions, not continuously across all Russian Internet connections. Its existence alone does not demonstrate a permanent nationwide allowlist or complete isolation.

On February 12, 2026, Russian authorities confirmed the complete blocking of WhatsApp, citing its alleged failure to comply with Russian law, and recommended that users switch to MAX, a state-backed national messenger. Reuters described the move as part of a broader approach to creating a controlled sovereign communications infrastructure, under which a foreign service either accepts domestic requirements or loses access to the market.

Counter-argument: The WhatsApp block and recommendation to use MAX show a state intervention in platform access, but the cited material does not establish how many users subsequently moved or whether all communications shifted to the domestic service.

Institutionally, excluding a foreign platform while preserving a domestic one on the whitelist are complementary mechanisms. The first reduces outside dependence through compulsory exclusion. The second determines which communications channels remain available during restricted connectivity. The relevant boundary shifts from access to particular websites toward control over network availability itself.

Counter-argument: The reported measures establish selective access and a blocked foreign messenger, but the whitelist is limited to specific periods of mobile-network restrictions. The conclusion should not be extended to all communications at all times.

Algorithmic Oversight

China is also establishing a regulatory boundary within the software layer. On April 10, 2026, CAC, the National Development and Reform Commission, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, the Ministry of Public Security, and the State Administration for Market Regulation published interim rules for anthropomorphic AI interaction services. They took effect on July 15. The rules link those services to risk management, provider obligations, user protection, and government supervision.

Counter-argument: The interim rules directly address anthropomorphic AI interaction services. Their issuance does not, by itself, show that identical requirements govern every AI application or every software service in China.

In parallel, CAC moved from rulemaking to large-scale enforcement. In July, the regulator reported that the first phase of its campaign against violations involving AI applications had resulted in action against more than 14,000 websites, applications, AI agents, and other products; the removal of more than 6 million items of illegal or harmful information; and measures involving more than 26,000 accounts. Cited violations included failure to complete required registrations for large models, inadequate moderation, training-data security problems, and noncompliance with labeling rules for synthetic content.

Registration requirements provide another measure of the system's reach. On July 15, CAC reported the registration of seven on-device generative AI services, including Apple Intelligence for the Chinese market. By August 31, CAC counted 1,112 registered generative AI services and 731 registered applications or functions using previously registered models.

Taken together, these measures resemble an algorithmic entry-control regime. A platform is not admitted solely on the basis of a corporate license: the state specifies requirements for models, content behavior, registration, service identification, and oversight. The passport analogy is limited but instructive: physical border controls verify people and goods, whereas this regulatory system verifies models, data practices, filters, and registration status. This is an analytical interpretation of CAC's published framework.

Counter-argument: Registration counts and enforcement totals show regulatory reach, but do not demonstrate how consistently individual models behave after approval. The analogy to a border should not be taken to imply complete operational oversight.

National Technology Stacks

The response to U.S. chip export controls is visible in China's market structure. According to IDC data reported by Reuters on October 13, Nvidia retained first place in China's data-center AI accelerator market with a 34% share, but Huawei reached 28%. Alibaba T-Head, Cambricon, Baidu Kunlunxin, and Hygon also expanded deliveries. Nine of the 11 Chinese producers tracked by IDC increased shipments year over year.

Counter-argument: Nvidia retained the largest reported single market share at 34%. Increased shipments from nine Chinese manufacturers do not establish that domestic suppliers have matched the entire capability or software ecosystem of imported accelerators.

A greater domestic hardware share does not, by itself, establish technological autonomy. The Financial Times reported on October 13 that Chinese developers continued to make extensive use of Nvidia chips and the CUDA software ecosystem. Domestic accelerator output was expanding, but demand continued to exceed supply, while software compatibility and developers' familiarity with CUDA still favored the U.S. architecture.

Counter-argument: The rise in domestic shipments and Huawei’s 28% share indicate a changing supply structure. If domestic software support and accelerator availability improve, the current advantage of CUDA and imported Nvidia hardware could narrow.

Export controls therefore have two potentially opposing effects. In the near term, they constrain a competitor's available computing capacity. Over the medium term, they strengthen the strategic case for an alternative technology stack. Huawei, Alibaba, Baidu, and Cambricon gain access to a domestic market that would be harder to capture if the highest-performing U.S. accelerators were freely available. This inference combines BIS policy with IDC's reported changes in market share.

Counter-argument: Nvidia still held 34% of the market, and Chinese developers continued to depend on CUDA. If software compatibility and domestic supply remain constrained, growing local chip shipments may not translate into equivalent stack autonomy.

Europe has pursued a different model. In its April sovereign-cloud procurement, the European Commission selected four supplier groups: Post Telecom in partnership with OVHCloud and CleverCloud; Germany's STACKIT; France's Scaleway; and a consortium led by Proximus that also uses S3NS, a joint venture between Thales and Google Cloud. Eligibility required at least SEAL-2.

That final example is informative. European "sovereignty" in this procurement does not necessarily require complete technological self-sufficiency or the exclusion of U.S. technology. A foreign technology layer may remain in the arrangement where legal, operational, and architectural separation meets the applicable standard. Brussels is attempting to make such dependency measurable and contractually bounded rather than remove it immediately.

Counter-argument: The minimum procurement threshold is SEAL-2, which addresses data sovereignty rather than full sovereignty under the framework. Compliance with that threshold alone cannot establish complete technological autonomy.

As of October 14, 2026, the Internet operated under several distinct sovereignty regimes rather than a single uniform regulatory model: U.S. control of selected chokepoints through exports and infrastructure corporations; Chinese requirements for algorithm registration combined with local hardware substitution; Russian blocking and selectively preserved national connectivity; and European jurisdictional scoring reinforced by procurement. These instruments differ, but each can increase the cost of crossing digital jurisdictions.

Counter-argument: The EU procurement includes a venture connected to Google Cloud, while the U.S. export regime uses case-by-case approvals. These examples suggest that jurisdictional boundaries remain selectively permeable rather than uniformly closed.

BIG TECH & THE STATE

DEFENSE AI • PROCUREMENT • PLATFORM DEPENDENCY
04

BIG TECH AND THE STATE: INSTITUTIONAL INTEGRATION

The Military-Information Complex

On September 1, 2026, the U.S. Army announced that Tactical Intelligence Targeting Access Node (TITAN) had entered production. Palantir received a $127 million order and Anduril a $65 million order. The first eight production systems—four TITAN Advanced and four TITAN Basic—are due within 18 months and will join nine existing prototypes. The Army describes TITAN as an AI-enabled ground station that integrates deep-sensing data, analysis, and automated target nominations.

This software performs functions beyond routine IT support for the Pentagon. It participates in the chain from sensor to analysis to target nomination to shooter. Legal decisions about the use of force remain with the government, but a private supplier builds part of the information layer through which commanders assess targets and receive analytical intelligence. The result can be functional integration without formal privatization of military command.

Counter-argument: The Army retains legal authority over the use of force, and the supplied facts do not establish that the contractor controls final targeting decisions. The arrangement is evidence of a critical software role, not transfer of command.

On September 17, the Army added another dependence: Palantir USG received a $48,094,071 order for an enterprise ammunition-management system. The system is intended to replace nine legacy platforms, consolidating ammunition records and management across the organization. The contract provides a 12-month base period and as many as five option years.

The institutional implications extend beyond the contract value. One vendor participates in both the intelligence/targeting system and the material logistics of ammunition. Vendor lock-in can therefore become a mobilization risk: changing a platform may require rebuilding parts of the information architecture used for military intelligence and supply, rather than simply replacing an administrative application. This is an inference from the stated functions of the two Army systems.

Counter-argument: The contracts identify system functions and a common supplier but do not quantify migration time or document specific contractual barriers to switching. The severity of lock-in therefore remains an operational risk assessment rather than a measured outcome.

The Pentagon is also broadening the field of commercial AI platforms eligible for classified environments. Reuters initially reported on May 1 that SpaceX, OpenAI, Google, Nvidia, Reflection AI, Microsoft, and AWS were slated for integration into Impact Level 6 and 7 environments. Subsequent public reporting added Oracle. Describing the list as permanently limited to exactly seven companies would be inaccurate.

The underlying policy direction remains a multiprovider commercial AI layer inside classified Pentagon networks. Stated uses include data synthesis, greater situational awareness, and support for military decision-making. Separately, GenAI.mil had been used by more than 1.3 million department personnel during its early months, according to Pentagon figures cited in May reporting.

Counter-argument: The reported 1.3 million GenAI.mil users demonstrate adoption, not that every user operates inside a classified network or relies on one vendor. Multiprovider integration may limit concentration in particular workflows.

The dispute involving Anthropic is also relevant. The company was excluded from the initial list amid disagreements with the Defense Department over acceptable restrictions on model use. This indicates a form of procurement sovereignty in which eligibility concerns not only pricing, performance, or certification, but also which constraints a vendor may place on the government's use of its model.

Counter-argument: The initial exclusion does not establish a permanent ban on Anthropic, particularly as the provider list was already evolving. A multiprovider strategy may also reduce the influence of any one supplier.

Proxy Sovereignty and Supplier Dependence

The United Kingdom provides a notable overseas example of dependence on a U.S. contractor. On June 2, Reuters reported that a parliamentary committee had called the British state's reliance on Palantir an "unacceptable point of weakness," specifically identifying a seven-year National Health Service contract worth approximately £330 million and the risk of vendor lock-in. On June 9, the British government confirmed a review of the contract, including whether to exercise a break clause.

Counter-argument: The government confirmed a contract review, including a potential break clause. That institutional option limits an assumption that the supplier cannot be replaced, even if switching involves costs.

Yet on October 9, the Financial Times reported that the British government had extended Palantir's contract for the Homes for Ukraine program for another year at a cost of £5 million, bringing total payments for this workstream to £15 million, even though a domestic replacement was already under development. This does not mean Palantir legally "governs" Britain. It indicates that withdrawing from an operational infrastructure platform may be politically desirable but operationally slow and costly.

Counter-argument: The NHS contract is under review and includes a possible break clause, while a replacement for Homes for Ukraine was reportedly being developed. Those steps indicate that costly substitution may still be feasible.

London also showed that dependence need not be irreversible. In June, the Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime blocked a proposed £50 million Metropolitan Police agreement with Palantir, citing, among other concerns, significant issues in the procurement process. Palantir is contesting the decision in court. A public buyer can therefore deny a platform entry, even as the dispute reflects the political importance of large AI providers in public infrastructure.

Counter-argument: The same government extended the Homes for Ukraine platform arrangement despite work on a replacement. A blocked prospective police contract therefore does not prove that all existing public-sector dependencies can be ended promptly.

Procurement Access, Not Proven Political Exchange

Available public records do not establish a direct bargain in which governments reduce antitrust pressure in return for data access or political loyalty from Big Tech. The documented relationship is narrower: compliance with defense or intelligence procurement requirements is a condition for entering a restricted market. The Anthropic dispute shows that disagreements over usage restrictions can affect supplier status, while the Pentagon simultaneously seeks to limit dependence on a single vendor by admitting several U.S. companies.

Counter-argument: Demonstrating a direct exchange between antitrust treatment and political cooperation would require additional evidence connecting the two decisions. The available examples document procurement conditions, not such an exchange.

The convergence of state and technology platforms in 2026 is therefore better characterized not as a covert transfer of sovereignty to corporations but as a durable form of institutional interdependence. Governments retain legal authority, budgets, and weapons, while contracting out increasing parts of their operational information systems: cloud infrastructure, models, GPUs, intelligence analysis, logistics, communications, and some energy planning. As these layers become more embedded, replacement becomes more costly, and commercial interoperability becomes a national-security consideration.

Counter-argument: The Pentagon is expanding the number of eligible vendors, and London has blocked a prospective Palantir procurement. Both examples show that public buyers retain some capacity to contest, diversify, or reject corporate platforms.

ACTIONABLE VERDICT

DEPENDENCY • RESILIENCE • EXIT COST
05

ACTIONABLE VERDICT: THE COST OF EXIT

The technology stack spanning defense AI, sovereign cloud services, advanced semiconductors, subsea connectivity, and data-center electricity is increasingly shaped by public policy. Palantir acts as a functional infrastructure proxy for U.S. defense institutions in important software layers. Reliance on its architecture can thereby create geopolitical and operational exposure for outside parties.

Counter-argument: The U.S. Army retains formal military authority, and the cited contracts do not quantify Palantir’s exclusivity or switching costs. Its critical contractual role supports a dependency assessment but not a claim of formal corporate sovereignty.

At the same time, Google, Microsoft, AWS, and other American hyperscalers form part of the infrastructure underpinning U.S. computing capabilities; Huawei is becoming a major component of China's domestic-hardware strategy; and the EU is seeking to turn reliance on external technology into a dependency that can be measured and regulated.

Counter-argument: Nvidia still held 34% of China’s market against Huawei’s 28%, and an EU sovereign-cloud supplier arrangement includes technology linked to Google Cloud. These details qualify claims of complete national or regional autonomy.

The broader test of sovereignty may extend beyond ownership of territory and formal public institutions to include the ability to continue functioning after the loss of a foreign technology stack. A U.S. export license can already restrict another major power's access to advanced accelerators; a small number of hyperscalers account for a substantial share of utilized international subsea capacity; EU procurement evaluates sovereign cloud arrangements against dozens of technical and jurisdictional criteria; and FERC is developing rules for how data centers pay for grid infrastructure and interact with limited capacity.

Counter-argument: Individual BIS licenses may still be approved, and EU sovereign-cloud arrangements may include U.S. technology under safeguards. These facts indicate that dependence does not always entail an inability to exercise public authority.

For a state, the practical questions are whether it can maintain international connectivity if a major cable route fails; run critical models without approval from another country's export regulator; transfer applications and data out of a foreign cloud without years of restructuring; guarantee enough electrical capacity for its own computing systems; and replace a military analytics platform without degrading intelligence and logistics. Developments in 2026 suggest these are important points at which technological dependence becomes measurable.

The U.S. model has strong external chokepoints: advanced GPUs, global clouds, a substantial share of utilized transatlantic capacity, and growing ties between major technology firms and military networks. China's model is intended to reduce those dependencies through domestic hardware and a regulated algorithmic environment, but it still faces software-compatibility costs and computing shortages. The European model relies on a large regulatory and procurement market, while permitting U.S. technology within arrangements labeled "sovereign" where safeguards meet the applicable rules. Russia has developed extensive administrative capacity to restrict network access and promote domestic services, but the reported October strikes on Yandex facilities illustrate the risks of physically concentrated national infrastructure.

Counter-argument: The stated strengths differ in kind: export control, local hardware supply, procurement criteria, and network restrictions. Without comparable resilience or substitution metrics, the evidence cannot establish a single overall ranking of sovereignty.

The resulting practical framework for technological power is as follows:

Control over chips affects who can compute; control over megawatts limits how much they can compute; control over cables shapes where the resulting data can travel; control over cloud infrastructure affects where it is stored; control over models and access rules constrains what software can do; and control over public procurement determines which organizations translate these capabilities into institutional operations.

Counter-argument: These six forms of control describe potential dependencies, but none alone establishes exclusive authority over state decisions. Shared power grids, conditional chip licenses, and multiprovider procurement can constrain how that control operates.

These dependencies intersect through export controls, electricity supply, cable infrastructure, sovereign-cloud procurement, and military AI systems.

For an outside actor, the principal geopolitical exposure is not simply "dependence on foreign technology" but the cost of exiting that dependence during a dispute. If cloud migration takes years, switching accelerators requires rewriting the software stack, backup cables depend on the same class of providers, new electrical generation is committed to long-term corporate agreements, and a critical government workflow runs on a proprietary platform, vendor lock-in can become a vulnerability that external actors may be able to exploit politically.

Counter-argument: The projected exposure is conditional on slow migration, difficult chip substitution, concentrated cable provision, and proprietary workflows. If one or more of these constraints can be removed at reasonable cost, the resulting external leverage would be less significant.

The concluding assessment is that the state has not been replaced by technology corporations in 2026. Rather, public institutions increasingly execute sovereign functions through corporate technology systems. Those companies gain political significance not through formal sovereignty but through the potentially high cost of removing their products from government, energy, or military operations.

Counter-argument: The examples of government contract review, rejected procurement, and competing approved vendors show that removal or diversification remains possible in at least some cases. Corporate importance is therefore contingent on actual substitution costs.

The actor able to control the disconnection, migration, or replacement of a technology stack may exercise substantial influence over the effective boundary of technological sovereignty.

Counter-argument: Control of a platform does not necessarily confer control of public decisions. The examples of multiprovider procurement, contract review, and rejected procurement show possible avenues for limiting a supplier's influence.
