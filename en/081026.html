01

GLOBAL MACROECONOMIC SHOCK AND BLOCKADE MECHANICS

This analytical study presents a comprehensive assessment of the macroeconomic, geopolitical, and sectoral consequences of a hypothetical 90-day isolation of the island of Taiwan by the People's Republic of China (PRC). The selection of a 90-day horizon is based on the premise that this timeframe represents a critical threshold: during this period, the island's strategic energy reserves are projected to be substantially depleted, and global supply chains transition from drawing down warehouse buffers to experiencing structural halts across multiple industries. The assessment relies on verified data from analytical centers, including Rhodium Group, Bloomberg Economics, and the Peterson Institute for International Economics, as well as the results of wargames conducted by the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS).

The implementation of an isolation of Taiwan does not necessarily imply an immediate full-scale kinetic invasion. PRC military doctrine views a blockade as an independent instrument of coercion possessing multiple gradations of escalation. Analysis of a series of 26 iterations of CSIS wargames indicates that Beijing has the capacity to flexibly vary the intensity of pressure, balancing economic restriction against the risk of a direct military clash with the United States and Japan.

A plausible initial stage, according to strategic forecasts, is an operation in the so-called "gray zone," taking the form of a customs quarantine. Under this scenario, the PRC utilizes not its armed forces, but the China Coast Guard (CCG), the Maritime Safety Administration (MSA), and thousands of maritime militia vessels. Beijing could announce the introduction of enhanced customs regulations, requiring all commercial vessels to obtain prior permission to enter key Taiwanese ports such as Kaohsiung or Keelung. The Coast Guard would assume the functions of inspecting and intercepting non-compliant vessels, while surface action groups of the People's Liberation Army (PLA) remain at a distance, performing the role of deterrence and providing radar control. Such an approach allows the PRC to reduce exposure to accusations of a direct act of war, shifting the burden of deciding on escalation onto the coalition of Taiwan's allies.

A subsequent, more restrictive stage of escalation is a full naval and air blockade (Joint Blockade Campaign). This kinetic scenario involves the physical closure of sea and air space by PLA forces, the deployment of submarines for covert mining of the waters around the island's 6 main ports, and continuous patrolling by combat aircraft. Sustaining such a blockade for 90 days represents a substantial logistical challenge, though data indicates the PRC possesses the requisite resources. The PRC fleet, numbering over 370 combat vessels, along with the maritime militia, can provide continuous surveillance, limiting Taiwan's situational awareness. Expert surveys suggest that the PLA is capable of maintaining a high intensity of operations for at least 6 months.

Taiwan is highly vulnerable to disruptions in maritime logistics, as the island imports 97% of its consumed energy resources and 70% of its food. Strategic reserves of crude oil and food can support the island for up to 6 months; however, reserves of coal and liquefied natural gas (LNG), which are critical for electricity generation, are projected to be exhausted in less than 8 weeks. Without a successful breach of the blockade, by the 9th week, electricity generation on the island is projected to fall to 20%–35% of peacetime levels. This reduction would likely lead to an immediate halt of industrial activity, including energy-intensive semiconductor fabrication plants.

Counter-argument: This projection of an industrial halt by the 9th week would be weakened if the blockade remains at the initial "gray zone" customs quarantine stage rather than a full kinetic Joint Blockade Campaign, or if US and Japanese naval convoys successfully breach the cordon to deliver liquefied natural gas and coal, or if existing 6-month crude oil reserves can be partially substituted to sustain power generation exclusively for priority semiconductor fabs.

The reaction of the United States and Japan in the event of an attempt to militarily breach the blockade would likely lead to a large-scale conflict. CSIS wargames indicate that a convoy escort operation could cost the coalition the loss of hundreds of commercial vessels, dozens of warships, and hundreds of aircraft. Such dynamics create a persistent risk of the conflict transitioning to direct strikes on military bases in mainland China, Japan, and Guam.

SEMICONDUCTOR SUPPLY CHAIN

FOUNDRY CONCENTRATION • ADVANCED PACKAGING
02

THE SEMICONDUCTOR SUPPLY CHAIN: STRUCTURAL CORE OF THE ANALYSIS

A cessation of exports from Taiwan for 90 days would trigger a severe contraction in the global technology industry. The island functions not merely as a market participant, but as the primary supplier in the production of critical microelectronics. Taiwanese companies control the vast majority of the global contract chip manufacturing market in monetary terms, with the share of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) alone in the pure-play foundry market reaching 72% as of early 2024.

A core vulnerability of the global economy lies in the fact that Taiwan produces over 90% of the world's most advanced logic chips—specifically nodes smaller than 7 nanometers (7nm), including 5nm and 3nm process technologies. These semiconductors serve as the primary foundation for the artificial intelligence industry, mobile processors, and high-performance computing. In the market for mature process nodes from 14nm to 28nm and above, Taiwanese corporations UMC, Vanguard, and PSMC also hold leading positions, providing continuous supplies of automotive and industrial microcontrollers.

The heavy reliance of global technology firms on TSMC's capacity poses a direct risk to their operational continuity. The combined market capitalization of TSMC's 10 largest clients approaches $14 trillion. Apple generates approximately 40% of TSMC's revenue, holding exclusive rights to 3nm nodes for its devices. Nvidia and AMD, which account for 8% and 10% of the Taiwanese firm's revenue respectively, rely heavily on TSMC for the production of advanced-architecture artificial intelligence accelerators.

Under stable market conditions, fabless chip designers typically maintain inventory buffers (Days Inventory Outstanding, DIO) at a level of 60–90 days. However, amid geopolitical risks and the artificial intelligence expansion, several corporations have increased this metric. Financial statement analysis shows that Nvidia's inventories stand at approximately 113 days (and by some estimates up to 131 days), AMD maintains reserves at a level of 156 days, and Qualcomm holds 127 days, whereas Broadcom operates with inventories of 53 days. During a 90-day blockade, warehouse reserves would be distributed unevenly across component assortments. Due to the "golden screw" effect, a shortage of even a single critical integrated circuit can halt the assembly of a final product, which points to a potential stoppage in the supply of new servers and consumer electronics within a single quarter.

Counter-argument: The projection of a stoppage in server and electronics assembly within a single quarter would be mitigated if fabless designers with extended inventory buffers—such as AMD (156 days), Nvidia (113–131 days), and Qualcomm (127 days)—hold balanced component assortments or undocumented shadow reserves that prevent a single-component bottleneck during the 90-day window.

The possibility of rapidly shifting production volumes to alternative capacities at Samsung (South Korea), Intel (US), and SMIC (PRC) within 90 days appears limited based on current technical constraints. China's SMIC is actively expanding its share in mature processes, aiming to capture up to 33% of the mature chip market by 2027; however, the company is restricted from EUV lithography due to US sanctions and is currently unable to mass-produce comparable advanced nodes. South Korea's Samsung Foundry continues to face challenges regarding die yields on advanced process nodes. Furthermore, the manufacturing cycle of a silicon wafer on an advanced node physically requires 3 to 4 months. Transferring photomasks, adapting design codes to the design rules of a different process node, and launching serial production within a 90-day timeframe is technically constrained.

Even if capacity for wafer printing were hypothetically secured, the industry would encounter a major bottleneck in Advanced Packaging. Demand for CoWoS (Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate), which is critical for assembling AI accelerators, is projected to rise to 1 million wafers per year. TSMC's CoWoS lines are booked years in advance, with order lead times ranging from 52 to 78 weeks, and approximately 60% of all capacity is contracted by Nvidia. Additionally, Taiwan's ASE Technology Holding accounts for up to 26% of the global outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) market, and more than 50% of its factories are physically located within Taiwan. Without the packaging stage, an advanced semiconductor die cannot be integrated into a functional system.

Counter-argument: The severity of the advanced packaging and foundry bottleneck outside Taiwan would be partially reduced to the extent that ASE Technology Holding utilizes the nearly 50% of its OSAT facilities located outside Taiwan, or if industrial conglomerates re-engineer electronic architectures to use mature process nodes (14nm–28nm and above) fabricated in the US, Japan, Europe, or by SMIC.

INDUSTRIAL & SECTORAL TRANSMISSION

AUTOMOTIVE • CLOUD INFRASTRUCTURE • DEFENSE
03

CASCADING SECTORAL EFFECTS

A 90-day cessation of Taiwanese exports is projected to trigger a chain reaction across global industrial sectors, exceeding the scale of the pandemic-era chip shortage. Extrapolating from the experience of 2021, when passenger car production volumes in Europe fell by 13%–25% due to a shortage of microcontrollers, illustrates the potential scale of disruption. Whereas the 2021 crisis resulted from a supply-and-demand imbalance, a blockade represents a physical cutoff of supply. Given that TSMC produces approximately 35% of the world's automotive microcontrollers, assembly lines at corporations ranging from Toyota and Volkswagen to Ford and Tesla could face forced downtime by day 30–40.

The generative artificial intelligence industry, cloud providers, and data center operators would face significant operational constraints. The lack of access to advanced packaging capacity and new-architecture chips would impede infrastructure expansion for leading global providers. A freeze on capital expenditures by hyperscalers would likely slow or halt the training of new foundation models, delaying development roadmaps across the high-tech sector for several years.

In the consumer electronics sector, manufacturers of smartphones, laptops, and household appliances would lose access to a primary source of systems-on-chip (SoCs). Device refresh cycles would be disrupted, and a conservative estimate by Rhodium Group suggests that corporations in dependent industries could forgo up to $1.6 trillion in annual revenue due to the lack of access to Taiwanese chips.

Counter-argument: The projected $1.6 trillion revenue loss and assembly line halts by day 30–40 could be moderated if automotive and consumer electronics manufacturers have accumulated off-balance-sheet strategic component inventories following the 2020–2022 shortage, or if they rapidly substitute non-Taiwanese mature-node microcontrollers (65% of global automotive microcontroller supply is produced outside TSMC).

Industrial equipment, medical technology, and the defense industrial base would also be affected by shortages of logic chips and field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs). The production of precision-guided munitions, unmanned systems, radar stations, as well as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) machines and pacemakers, would experience substantial slowdowns. Replacing commercial-grade military components requires multi-year board redesigns and recertification, which could constrain the defense readiness of coalition countries.

MACROECONOMIC & GEOPOLITICAL SHOCK

GDP CONTRACTION • SANCTIONS • CRITICAL MINERALS
04

MACROECONOMICS, MARKET REACTION & GEOPOLITICAL COUNTERMEASURES

The financial damage from a crisis in the Taiwan Strait is projected to be substantial. Modeling by Bloomberg Economics indicates that a full-scale kinetic war could result in a loss of $10.6 trillion, equivalent to a contraction of global GDP by nearly 10% in the first year. This shock would be roughly double the combined losses of the 2008 global financial crisis. A scenario involving a strict economic blockade without escalating into a global war is estimated to cost the global economy approximately $5 trillion, or 5% of global GDP. Analysis by Rhodium Group indicates that direct trade disruptions alone would result in the loss of over $2 trillion in economic activity before accounting for secondary effects and international sanctions.

In the first 72 hours of a blockade, equity markets would likely experience a sharp sell-off. Investment models project a decline in the S&P 500 index of up to 40%, with the most pronounced impact falling on the technology sector, which forms a major share of US market capitalization. The sell-off would affect shares of multinational corporations whose supply chains are integrated into the Asian region. Foreign investors holding over $1 trillion in Chinese securities in the onshore market and more than $775 billion in offshore listings would likely initiate rapid asset liquidations due to the risk of blocking sanctions.

The banking sector is projected to react by curtailing trade finance credit due to the risk of secondary sanctions and widespread counterparty insolvency. The global pool of short-term trade finance, estimated at $6.5–$8 trillion, would contract sharply. This liquidity squeeze could freeze trade operations between the PRC and the rest of the world valued at over $270 billion.

A distinct macroeconomic shock would hit global maritime logistics. Up to 20% of global maritime trade, valued at nearly $2.5 trillion, as well as the routes of nearly 50% of the global container fleet, pass through the Taiwan Strait and adjacent waters of the South China Sea. A blockade would force shipping companies to reroute vessels around the conflict zone. A key indicator of market stress would be the war risk insurance market. Experience from crises in the Red Sea and the Strait of Hormuz indicates that insurance premiums can rise within days from a baseline of 0.3% to 5% of a vessel's hull value. For an ultra-large container vessel, this represents an increase in transit premiums from $100,000 to several million dollars. A sharp rise in shipping costs would generate an inflationary impulse, disrupting supplies not only of electronics, but also of basic commodities across the Asia-Pacific region.

Counter-argument: The projected global GDP contraction of $5 trillion to $10.6 trillion and the 40% decline in the S&P 500 depend on the duration and kinetic intensity of the isolation; if a gray-zone quarantine is resolved prior to the 8-week energy depletion threshold, direct economic losses could remain closer to or below the Rhodium Group baseline of $2 trillion in direct trade disruptions.

In response to the establishment of a blockade, the United States, the European Union, and the G7 countries are expected to activate financial and technological sanctions packages against the PRC. A strategy of economic coercion would likely include disconnecting systemically important Chinese banks from the dollar clearing system and freezing assets. According to analysis by the Peterson Institute for International Economics and the Atlantic Council, G7 exports to China valued at over $358 billion and foreign direct investment exceeding $460 billion would be directly exposed. These measures would impose significant costs on both the Chinese and global economies, as more than 100 million jobs in the PRC depend on external demand.

Rather than responding symmetrically in the dollar-dominated financial sector, China would likely deploy asymmetric pressure through export controls on critical minerals. The PRC controls 94% of global supplies of gallium and 83% of germanium, and holds a leading share in the extraction and processing of antimony, graphite, and heavy rare earth metals. A historical precedent has been established: in 2023 and 2024, in response to US technological restrictions, Beijing phased in strict export licensing for these elements under its Export Control Law (ECL). During those periods, exports of gallium and germanium fell to near zero, and global prices rose by 300%–400% within a few months. Under a Taiwan blockade scenario, a full embargo by Beijing on supplies to coalition countries would severely constrain Western manufacturing of electric vehicle batteries, solar panels, infrared optics, radars, and munitions.

Countries of the Global South that are economically linked to the PRC would also experience significant macroeconomic strain. Facing a projected contraction of its own GDP (8.9%–16.7%), China would likely curtail external lending and capital outflows under the Belt and Road Initiative. Credit lines and infrastructure investments valued at approximately $100 billion per year would be placed at risk. The absence of refinancing could trigger sovereign defaults across developing nations that rely on Chinese capital and imports.

Counter-argument: The likelihood of comprehensive G7 financial sanctions and a reciprocal Chinese mineral embargo would be reduced if both blocs seek to avoid mutual domestic economic losses—specifically the exposure of $358 billion in G7 exports and $460 billion in G7 foreign direct investment alongside 100 million export-dependent jobs and an 8.9%–16.7% GDP contraction in the PRC.

TIMELINE, ANALOGIES & UNCERTAINTIES

CONSUMER IMPACT • HISTORICAL CASES • BLIND SPOTS
05

CONSUMER TRANSMISSION, HISTORICAL ANALOGIES & ANALYTICAL BLIND SPOTS

The transmission of the macroeconomic shock and semiconductor shortage to the end consumer would occur with a lag, which can be structured across a timeline of 60, 90, and 120 days.

The first 60 days of a blockade would be characterized by logistical disruption and rising prices for basic goods. Due to surging insurance premiums and the forced rerouting of vessels, freight rates would increase sharply. Consumers would face higher prices for imported clothing, pharmaceuticals, food, and fuel. In the electronics sector, retail inventory buffers would begin to deplete. Major retailers in the US and Europe, reacting to supply disruptions, are projected to implement algorithmic price increases of 30%–50% on smartphones, televisions, laptops, and household appliances amid elevated consumer demand and precautionary buying.

As the 90-day mark approaches, manufacturing sectors would face cascading plant closures. Shortages of microcontrollers and power management chips would halt assembly lines for automobiles, refrigerators, washing machines, and industrial equipment. A shortage of spare parts would affect maintenance stations and repair services. During this same period, layoffs in dependent industrial sectors in the US and Europe would likely begin, further reducing household purchasing power.

Crossing the 120-day threshold would mark the onset of a broad structural shortage. The assembly of modern household appliances would be severely restricted. Indirect consequences would extend to the agricultural sector due to disruptions in the supply of fertilizers and spare parts for agricultural machinery, which, combined with higher logistics costs, would contribute to global food price pressures. The consumer market would experience a multi-year setback in the availability and variety of technology-dependent goods.

To evaluate the propagation speed of a global shock, it is instructive to examine two recent crises: the blockage of the Suez Canal and the semiconductor shortage during the COVID-19 pandemic.

The Suez Canal crisis, caused by the grounding of the container ship Ever Given in the spring of 2021, demonstrated the sensitivity of maritime logistics. The vessel blocked 12% of global maritime trade for 6 days. This disruption delayed an estimated $9.6 billion worth of cargo daily. Freight rates on the Asia-Europe route rose by 400%, and congestion at European and Asian ports took months to clear. In the event of a 90-day isolation of the waters around Taiwan, through which 20% of global trade passes, the scale of logistical disruption would be substantially larger, and the rerouting of Asian maritime traffic would strain alternative port capacities.

The chip shortage of 2020–2022 provides a relevant analogy for a production shock. However, the pandemic crisis was driven by a structural demand imbalance—a surge in purchases of personal computers, medical equipment, and servers—amid relatively stable global supply. Even under those conditions, temporary automotive plant shutdowns cost the global economy hundreds of billions of dollars in unrealized output. The Taiwan isolation scenario differs structurally: rather than a demand imbalance, it entails the physical removal of a core manufacturing hub from the global supply chain. The degradation of the technology sector would likely be measured in weeks rather than quarters, as replacing 90% of advanced lithography capacity in the short term is not feasible under current industrial constraints.

Counter-argument: The conclusion that consumer and industrial technology sectors would experience a rapid, unmitigated structural halt after 90–120 days would be weakened if market economies demonstrate structural adaptability comparable to Europe's response to the 2022 energy shock, or if the PRC and global firms preemptively accumulate and reroute semiconductor inventories through third countries prior to the enforcement of a strict blockade.

Uncertainties and Analytical Blind Spots: Despite the granularity of macroeconomic and logistical models, several critical factors remain outside the scope of reliable quantitative forecasting, creating analytical blind spots.

First, financial models cannot precisely quantify the impact of psychological contagion on global markets. The scale of market sell-offs, index declines, and flights to safe-haven assets could exceed fundamentally justified damage due to cascading margin calls, derivatives dislocations, and a contraction of trust among financial institutions.

Second, the volume of shadow semiconductor inventories cannot be verified with certainty. The true size of off-balance-sheet strategic component reserves held by major technology corporations and defense ministries remains undisclosed. Similarly, China's capacity to preemptively accumulate stockpiles or organize gray-market transshipment of chips through third countries before a quarantine transitions into a military phase remains uncertain.

Third, the boundary between a law-enforcement quarantine in the gray zone and a full-scale kinetic blockade is unstable. An unintended incident, such as the sinking of a commercial vessel or a collision between naval destroyers, could escalate the conflict into direct missile exchanges between the United States and the PRC. In the event of a broader military conflict, macroeconomic calculations of freight rates or inflation would be superseded by the physical destruction of infrastructure in East Asia.

Finally, the historical experience of Europe's adaptation to the 2022 energy shock indicates that market economies possess substantial capacity for structural adjustment. It remains uncertain how quickly global industrial conglomerates could redesign the architecture of their electronic systems away from advanced 3nm chips toward mature-node semiconductors produced outside the conflict zone in the US, Japan, or Europe. This potential adaptability could moderate the severity of the crisis over longer time horizons extending beyond the initial 90 days.
