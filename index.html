FRENCH SOVEREIGN RISK

DEBT STRUCTURE • FISCAL CONDITIONS
01

THE FRENCH DEBT THRESHOLD AND CURRENT CONDITIONS

Central Finding and Initial Conditions. The outcome examined in this report is a sustained crisis regime in which the yield spread between 10-year French OATs and 10-year German Bunds exceeds 300 basis points for 30 consecutive calendar days. For operational testing, every trading-day closing observation within an uninterrupted 30-calendar-day window must remain above 300 basis points, typically yielding about 20–23 market observations. A stricter version requires 30 consecutive trading days. A brief touch of 300 basis points is not equivalent to a sustained month-long regime.

As of November 18, 2026, the latest synchronized market observation identified for France and Germany, France's 10-year yield was 4.87% and Germany's was 3.38%, putting the spread near 149 basis points. Belgium traded near 4.34% and Italy near 4.44%. France was therefore borrowing at a higher 10-year yield than Italy, and the spread was roughly 151 basis points below the target threshold. On November 19, Agence France Trésor (AFT) reported a French TEC10 yield of 4.88%, but no comparable official German observation at the same time was available in the report's sample; the later French figure should not be used to construct an unsynchronized spread.

A 300-basis-point spread is especially extreme for France. During 2011–2012, the French spread against Germany reached approximately 150 basis points, while Italian and Spanish spreads exceeded 500 basis points. A sustained French spread above 300 basis points would therefore suggest a different pricing regime, potentially reflecting investor doubts about either France's political capacity to stabilize its debt or European institutions' ability to contain a breakdown in monetary policy transmission.

Counter-argument: Historical spread comparisons underline the severity of the threshold but do not make its eventual attainment inevitable. A widened French spread can also reflect changing relative yields without implying a complete loss of confidence in either French policy or the European institutional framework.

The report's overall assessment is that the event is plausible but remains a tail risk. Its central expert probability range for a sustained spread above 300 basis points within the next 12 months is approximately 0.5%–4%, with a working central estimate of about 1.5%–2%. These figures are neither observed statistical frequencies nor objective probabilities. The judgment weighs the very low historical base rate against a more vulnerable French configuration: a high deficit and debt load, slower growth, a budget impasse, record issuance, substantial nonresident ownership, and a materially wider spread than in previous years. The European Commission projects a deficit of 5.5% of GDP for 2026, growth of just 0.4%, and debt of 123.2% of GDP by 2028; INSEE had already recorded debt at 119.0% of GDP in the second quarter of 2026.

Counter-argument: The fiscal and political indicators increase vulnerability but do not establish the likelihood of an unusually persistent market regime. The report itself characterizes its probability range as an expert assessment, not a calibrated statistical result.

The most likely path to the threshold combines three moderate pressures rather than a single catastrophic shock: prolonged budget and political deadlock, further repricing of French sovereign risk, and deteriorating liquidity during a large refinancing period. The fastest path would involve a sudden political-fiscal shock, forced selling, and a flight to quality into Bunds. The most damaging version would add a bank–sovereign feedback loop and contagion to Belgium and Italy. The most important stabilizers would be credible fiscal adjustment, functioning bond auctions, and an ECB judgment that widening spreads represent impaired monetary transmission rather than a justified response to fiscal fundamentals.

Counter-argument: These pathways require several conditions to coincide. Continued auction demand, fiscal compromise, or a credible European response could interrupt the chain before a lasting 300-basis-point spread develops.

Current State of the System. France's sovereign debt stock is already substantial. AFT reported €2.921 trillion in outstanding negotiable government debt on October 31, 2026, with an average remaining life of 8 years and 139 days. Medium- and long-term instruments accounted for approximately €2.705 trillion. That maturity structure provides a significant buffer: market yields can rise rapidly, while the average interest cost of the entire outstanding stock adjusts more slowly.

The buffer is nevertheless gradually reduced by the scale of refinancing. AFT's 2026 program envisages €310 billion in net medium- and long-term issuance, while 2027 budget documents indicate approximately €340 billion. Medium- and long-term redemptions are expected to increase materially in 2027; AFT estimates the state's total financing requirement at €339.7 billion. As of late October 2026, approximately €187.1 billion of OATs were scheduled to mature in 2027.

Counter-argument: The refinancing volume raises France's exposure to higher marginal funding costs, but the average debt maturity exceeds eight years. Higher market yields would not immediately reprice the full sovereign debt stock.

The market is still functioning. On November 19, 2026, an auction placed €11.996 billion across four OAT lines, and bids exceeded the amounts allotted in each line. This is important evidence against the assertion that buyers had already withdrawn from French debt: stress was visible in the price of borrowing, not in an inability to issue securities.

Nonresident ownership adds another dimension to the risk. Banque de France reported that nonresidents held 58.4% of long-term French general-government debt in the second quarter of 2026, up from 55.9% in the preceding quarter. Broad international ownership supports market depth under normal conditions but could make prices more sensitive to global risk aversion and large-fund portfolio reallocations during a crisis.

Counter-argument: A diversified overseas investor base is also a source of liquidity and demand. Its significance as a destabilizing factor would depend on whether investors actually undertake simultaneous large net sales.

Fiscal policy is facing a tangible institutional impasse. On November 4, 2026, the National Assembly rejected the first part of the 2027 budget bill (PLF 2027); all 458 votes cast opposed it. On November 19, the bill was formally sent to the Senate after being deemed rejected by the National Assembly. France also remains subject to the European Union's excessive deficit procedure.

The government's proposal targets a deficit of 5.0% of GDP and debt of 121.7% in 2027. By contrast, the European Commission's November 17 forecast, based on a more conservative assessment of the measures, anticipates a 2027 deficit of 5.4%. This difference between the official target and the external forecast may contribute to the risk premium: investors assess not only a proposed trajectory but its prospects for political implementation.

Counter-argument: A rejected initial budget provision and a gap between forecasts do not establish that fiscal adjustment is impossible. A subsequent budget compromise could improve confidence in the proposed trajectory.

French banks are more strongly capitalized than they were in 2011–2012. The ACPR reports a Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) ratio of approximately 15.7% for the six largest banking groups. In the 2025 stress test, aggregate CET1 for major French banks declined from 15.9% to 10.5% under a severely adverse scenario but remained above requirements. The largest banks' exposure to French sovereign risk represented approximately 4.1% of their balance sheets, with most holdings booked at amortized cost, reducing the immediate profit-and-loss impact of falling market prices.

That does not remove the liquidity channel. The ECB notes that euro-area banks have increased sovereign holdings in recent years and use government bonds both as high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) and as repo collateral. Repo links between banks and nonbank financial institutions have also intensified. The European Banking Authority estimates EU/EEA bank sovereign exposures at approximately €4.5 trillion as of June 2026.

Counter-argument: Capital buffers and amortized-cost accounting may reduce the immediate transmission of mark-to-market losses to bank solvency. Liquidity stress would require additional mechanisms, such as collateral revaluation, funding pressure, or forced sales, rather than follow automatically from wider OAT spreads.

CAUSAL MODEL AND SIMULATION

REGIME CHANGE • STRESS TESTING
02

HOW A 300-BASIS-POINT SPREAD COULD PERSIST

Reverse Causal Modeling. A poor fiscal position alone is unlikely to keep the OAT–Bund spread above 300 basis points. Such an outcome would require factors that increase France's sovereign risk premium by roughly another 150 basis points and prevent a rapid reversal.

The endpoint of the causal model is an OAT–Bund spread above 300 basis points for at least 30 calendar days. The required combination has four components: (1) a substantial increase in the French credit and political risk premium; (2) additional safe-haven demand for Bunds, or at least German yields rising more slowly than OAT yields; (3) a deterioration in OAT liquidity such that incremental selling produces disproportionate price movements; and (4) a French, EU, or ECB response that arrives too slowly or is perceived as conditional.

Several alternative mechanisms could produce that combination:

  • Budget crisis → no credible medium-term fiscal trajectory → rating actions → portfolio reallocations.
  • Several weak or failed auctions → larger issuance concessions → reduced dealer inventories → wider bid–ask spreads → forced selling.
  • Political crisis → expectations of further fiscal deterioration → flight to quality into Bunds.
  • Sovereign shock → lower collateral values → margin calls and higher repo haircuts → forced sales.
  • External risk-off episode → simultaneous OAT sales by global funds → a larger France-specific premium.
  • Banking stress → sovereign losses increase bank funding costs → banks contract balance sheets → the economy weakens → fiscal outcomes deteriorate → sovereign risk increases.
  • Contagion involving Belgium or Italy, in either direction → redenomination or systemic euro risk premiums displace country-specific fundamentals.

Counter-argument: The model specifies possible reinforcing mechanisms rather than an unavoidable sequence. Its endpoint requires multiple reinforcing conditions, and auction resilience, a policy response, or a reversal in global risk aversion could disrupt any of the paths.

Historical episodes illustrate two nonlinear channels. During Italy's May 2018 political and budget conflict, sovereign spreads widened quickly and liquidity deteriorated sharply; on some trading venues, bid–ask indicators worsened by orders of magnitude, and the relationship between sovereign and bank credit default swap (CDS) spreads strengthened. In the United Kingdom's 2022 gilt and liability-driven investment (LDI) crisis, margin calls and forced sales created a self-reinforcing spiral that prompted Bank of England intervention.

France also has meaningful stabilizers. Average debt maturity exceeds eight years, limiting the immediate budget impact of higher rates. The OAT market is large and liquid, with a broad investor base. Banks are relatively well capitalized, and the ECB has the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) and Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT). The IMF identifies long maturity, liquid markets, and the ECB's stabilizing role as factors that mitigate French debt risks.

Counter-argument: The 2018 Italian and 2022 British episodes demonstrate possible market dynamics but do not establish that France will reproduce either crisis. Its market depth, debt maturity, bank capital, and European policy tools could meaningfully limit escalation.

TPI is not an automatic guarantee at any spread level. Activation is discretionary, and the ECB evaluates monetary transmission, proportionality, and fiscal and macroeconomic criteria. These include adherence to the EU fiscal framework, absence of severe macroeconomic imbalances, debt sustainability, and compliance with European program commitments. Purchases may cease if market tension is judged to reflect fundamentals. OMT is more restrictive: it requires an ESM/EFSF program with effective conditionality and focuses primarily on the one- to three-year maturity segment.

The Pandemic Emergency Purchase Programme (PEPP) no longer provides a flexible reinvestment buffer: reinvestments stopped completely at the end of 2024. The Asset Purchase Programme (APP) is also contracting passively after reinvestments ended. Consequently, the ECB's response in 2026 could be more institutionally and politically demanding than during active reinvestment periods.

Counter-argument: The end of reinvestment flexibility narrows the available channels, but it does not remove the ECB's TPI and OMT frameworks. Their effectiveness would depend on the assessed source of spread widening and the applicable eligibility conditions.

Stochastic Simulation. The source research reports 1,500,000 simulated annual paths, divided evenly into three regimes: low stress, central scenario, and high stress, with 500,000 simulations per regime. Each path covers 252 trading days. The initial spread is 148 basis points, close to the synchronized November 18 observation. The main event test requires 21 consecutive trading-day closes above 300 basis points, as an approximation for 30 calendar days; a stricter test requires 30 consecutive trading days.

The spread was modeled as a regime-dependent, mean-reverting jump process:

S(t+1) = S(t) + κ[μ − S(t)] + σε(t) + J(t) − C(t) + F[S(t)]

Here, S(t) is the spread; κ is the speed of mean reversion; μ is the normal spread level for the regime; σ is daily volatility; J(t) represents infrequent upward jumps arising from political, fiscal, or liquidity risks; C(t) represents stabilizing policy intervention at elevated spreads; and F[S(t)] is endogenous positive feedback that grows at stress levels.

This is not an econometrically estimated model. Jump frequencies and sizes were selected as scenario parameters because France's historical experience contains almost no comparable sustained 300-basis-point episodes. Pooling a handful of different European crises into one statistical sample would create misleading precision. The results therefore describe conditional sensitivity, not an empirically estimated forecast.

In the high-stress regime, the modeled probability of a sustained event rose from approximately 0.0024% by 30 calendar days to 0.20% by 90 days, 0.58% by 180 days, and 1.33% within one year. In the central mechanical regime, the event was almost absent. Elevated volatility alone was insufficient: a persistent spread above 300 basis points required a change in regime.

Counter-argument: These output frequencies depend on the deliberately selected jump, mean-reversion, feedback, and intervention parameters. They should not be interpreted as directly measured event probabilities for French sovereign debt.

The central model's very low incidence must not be treated as an actual forecast of approximately 0.005%. The model cannot endogenously generate rare institutional breaks, including an unexpected coalition collapse, failed fiscal agreement, change in ECB reaction, or large forced-selling loop. The report's final forecast therefore blends historical baseline information, regime stress tests, and an expert assessment of the chance of entering the high-stress state.

Sensitivity is highly nonlinear. A modest increase in daily volatility produces few sustained events, whereas simultaneously increasing jump frequency, reducing mean reversion, and weakening stabilizing policy responses increases the modeled annual probability by several orders of magnitude. This is why the forecast should be updated using regime indicators, not the spread level alone.

Counter-argument: The nonlinear behavior is a property of the specified model rather than proof that markets will make the same transition. The precise impact of institutional shocks remains difficult to calibrate from the limited French historical record.

Analysis of the Most Important Paths. The most likely trajectory is the gradual accumulation of risk. Three preconditions already exist: a high fiscal deficit, a growing debt burden, and parliamentary deadlock. The European Commission anticipates only 0.4% growth in 2026, a deficit of 5.5% of GDP, and rising interest costs. The IMF also warns that weak demand and fiscal vulnerabilities could trigger renewed market pressure.

That combination alone is unlikely to take the spread from approximately 150 to 300 basis points. The second stage would require investors to judge fiscal adjustment in 2027–2028 politically infeasible. A third stage could then involve ratings changes, reallocations by long-horizon funds, larger auction concessions, and weaker liquidity. Only after those developments might a self-reinforcing process emerge.

Counter-argument: A parliamentary budget agreement or credible medium-term consolidation could interrupt the gradual path. Existing auction demand also shows that higher yields have not yet prevented market access.

The fastest route is less a slow deterioration in debt sustainability than a market accident. Consider a political shock coinciding with global risk aversion: investors buy Bunds as safe-haven assets while selling OATs; leveraged funds face margin calls; and dealers reduce inventory. Under such conditions, spreads could move within days through levels that might otherwise take months to reach. Similar trading mechanisms amplified Italy's 2018 sovereign stress and Britain's 2022 gilt disruption.

Counter-argument: This scenario depends on overlapping political, market, and financing stresses. It is not directly implied by the current level of French yields, and France's large OAT market may continue to absorb selling pressure.

The most persistent route to a 300-basis-point spread is political and fundamental rather than merely a liquidity event: several quarters without credible consolidation, renewed increases in the debt-to-GDP ratio, economic weakness, and investor concern that the ECB cannot clearly separate unjustified fragmentation from a justified fiscal risk premium. Such a path is slower but potentially harder to reverse with technical market interventions.

Counter-argument: The same gradual process leaves time for fiscal negotiations, policy changes, and debt-management adjustments. A credible consolidation program or stronger economic outlook could weaken the fundamental risk premium.

A comparatively less demanding mechanical route does not require French yields to rise by the full change in the spread. If a global flight to safety reduces Bund yields by 50–80 basis points while OAT yields rise by 70–100 basis points, a large portion of the required spread increase would occur with a smaller absolute move in French yields. Flight-to-quality dynamics are therefore central to the relative-pricing risk.

Counter-argument: This arithmetic requires Bund and OAT yields to diverge sharply. If their yields move more closely together during global market stress, the French spread could widen substantially less.

An unexpected route would involve a stabilization attempt that temporarily amplifies stress. If the government announces major fiscal consolidation but investors view it as politically unworkable or economically contractionary, the near-term growth outlook could worsen without an immediate improvement in projected debt dynamics. Likewise, an ECB signal viewed as excessively conditional could invite investors to test the limits of TPI rather than restore confidence.

Counter-argument: Credible consolidation and credible ECB communication are also central stabilizing forces in the report. Whether they calm or intensify markets would depend on investor assessments of feasibility, growth effects, and the actual availability of policy instruments.

The most sensitive transition is from spreads of 180–220 basis points into the 230–260-basis-point range. At such levels, risk limits, value-at-risk models, collateral calls, benchmark substitutions, and political attention may begin reinforcing one another. In the simulation, weaker mean reversion and stronger feedback above stressed levels accounted for much of the increase in the modeled probability of sustained spreads above 300 basis points.

Counter-argument: The 180–260-basis-point zones are scenario-sensitive indicators, not established universal market thresholds. The modeled feedback depends on assumptions about participant behavior and the effectiveness of stabilizing responses.

EURO-AREA SPILLOVERS

BANKS • COLLATERAL • CONTAGION
04

THE EURO AREA AFTER A FRENCH DEBT SHOCK

Conditions After a Sustained Spread Above 300 Basis Points. In the first 30 days, France would not experience an immediate debt-service explosion. Because of its long average debt maturity, most outstanding obligations would continue to pay their existing coupons. The first effects would appear in pricing of new issuance, mark-to-market portfolio values, collateral, and market expectations—not across the entire debt stock at once.

Nevertheless, with €300 billion–€340 billion in annual medium- and long-term issuance, even several months of high yields could materially affect marginal funding costs. An additional 100 basis points on €300 billion of newly issued or refinanced debt corresponds to roughly €3 billion in additional annual interest on that specific flow once fully issued, not on the whole sovereign debt stock. The simplified calculation “300 basis points × €3 trillion = €90 billion immediately” is therefore incorrect.

Counter-argument: The incremental interest calculation illustrates exposure to refinancing at higher yields, not an immediate annualized cost for the entire state. The maturity schedule provides time for repricing to work through the debt portfolio.

Within 30 days, likely symptoms include larger auction concessions, wider bid–ask spreads, increased haircuts demanded by private counterparties, declining market valuations of OAT portfolios, higher bank and insurer funding spreads, a stronger scarcity premium for Bunds, and pressure on the euro. The ECB would need to determine whether conditions represent disorderly impairment of monetary transmission or a fundamentally justified sovereign repricing.

Counter-argument: These market effects are conditional on a sustained extreme spread and the surrounding liquidity conditions. An effective policy response or resilient market-making capacity could reduce their intensity.

By 90 days, markets would likely be testing the European institutional response. A credible prospect of TPI activation could reduce tail risk, but France's excessive deficit procedure (EDP) would make fiscal conditionality politically sensitive. The EDP does not automatically disqualify France from TPI: the criteria allow consideration of whether a government is taking effective corrective action under EU recommendations. Activation remains discretionary.

Counter-argument: TPI eligibility is neither automatically granted nor automatically denied because of an excessive deficit procedure. The policy outcome would depend on the ECB's assessment of fiscal actions, debt sustainability, market dysfunction, and proportionality.

At 180 days, a persistent spread above 300 basis points could create a material bank–sovereign macroeconomic feedback loop: more expensive government funding raises bank funding costs; tighter credit weakens investment and housing; a weaker economy reduces the tax base; and fiscal deterioration adds to sovereign risk. French banks would enter this shock with strong capital buffers, but prolonged sovereign stress could gradually shift the problem from market risk toward credit risk.

Counter-argument: The reported capital ratios and stress-test performance indicate that banks have some capacity to absorb market losses. A broader credit contraction would require sustained transmission from sovereign pricing to bank funding and lending conditions.

Over a full year, the issue could become one of euro-area architecture. If France—the euro area's second-largest sovereign issuer after Italy—remains unstable despite a European response, investors might begin pricing a systemic euro risk premium rather than only a French sovereign premium.

Counter-argument: A large French spread would not necessarily imply that the euro area as a whole has entered a systemic crisis. The distinction would depend on the effectiveness of the European response and whether stress extends to other national markets.

Italy's position is mixed. Its public debt is higher than France's, at approximately 138% of GDP, but its 2026 deficit is substantially lower, around 2.9%, and the country has a primary surplus. Automatic contagion is therefore not assured. If the French episode is interpreted as a weakness in euro-area architecture, however, Italy would likely face a reassessment of its sovereign risk.

Belgium may be more exposed than commonly assumed. Its debt is approximately 111.2% of GDP and its 2026 deficit is 5.2%, with the European Commission expecting further deterioration. France could thus become the center of a northwestern European fiscal-risk cluster, with Belgium experiencing early spillovers rather than France merely repeating Italy's previous role in euro-area debt stress.

Counter-argument: Italy's primary surplus and lower deficit distinguish its starting position from France's, while Belgium's smaller market introduces different liquidity dynamics. The extent of contagion would depend on whether investors interpret the shock as French-specific or systemic.

Less Obvious Findings. First, France is no longer an unambiguous “semi-core” benchmark. On November 18, its 10-year yield exceeded Italy's by approximately 43 basis points. This does not prove that France's fundamental credit risk is greater than Italy's, but it shows the extent of the relative-value shift.

Second, the main accelerator may not come from banks. French banks are substantially more resilient than during the previous euro-area crisis, whereas the ECB emphasizes the growing role of leveraged nonbank financial institutions (NBFIs) and hedge funds in sovereign bond and repo markets. A crisis accelerator may therefore be found in the interaction of NBFIs, repo funding, and collateral rather than a traditional bank run.

Counter-argument: The increasing role of nonbank investors creates an additional transmission channel but does not establish that those investors will precipitate stress. Bank capitalization and collateral-market safeguards remain important counterweights.

Third, Germany is no longer a fiscally neutral anchor. The European Commission projects a German deficit of 4.1% of GDP in 2026 and debt rising to 68.9% of GDP by 2028 because of defense and infrastructure spending. Greater Bund supply may reduce safe-haven scarcity and mechanically restrain the French spread, although Bund liquidity and perceived safety could still dominate during a severe panic.

Counter-argument: Additional Bund issuance may limit the scarcity component of relative pricing, but it may not prevent flight-to-quality demand from lowering German yields during a broader market shock.

Fourth, the cessation of PEPP reinvestments removed a flexible channel for relatively unobtrusive country-specific stabilization. In another sovereign crisis, the ECB might have to move more quickly from informal flexibility toward politically visible tools such as TPI.

Counter-argument: The loss of this reinvestment buffer does not eliminate the ECB's formal instruments; it changes the likely visibility and conditions of a response, rather than precluding intervention.

Fifth, foreign ownership creates an asymmetry. International investors provide depth to the French debt market during normal conditions but can also increase sensitivity to global portfolio shifts. The initial shock might therefore originate outside France—for example, from a sharp global risk-off event, liquidation of basis trades, or a U.S. dollar funding shock.

Counter-argument: The same diversified investor structure that exposes France to global reallocations can also support demand across varying market conditions. An external trigger would have to translate into concentrated OAT selling and persistent relative repricing to create the proposed crisis.

INDICATORS AND PROBABILITIES

EARLY WARNINGS • EXPERT FORECAST
05

CRITICAL INDICATORS AND PROBABILITY ASSESSMENT

Critical Forward Indicators. The assessed probability of a sustained French OAT–Bund spread above 300 basis points should rise materially if several of the following signs appear together:

  • The OAT–Bund spread persistently exceeds 180–200 basis points and then 225–250 basis points, with widening driven by a growing France-specific premium rather than a general rise in yields.
  • Coverage at AFT auctions declines, concessions relative to secondary-market pricing increase, auction tails deteriorate, and bid–ask spreads widen noticeably.
  • The nonresident ownership share begins to fall alongside sizable net sovereign-bond sales.
  • Bank senior, covered-bond, and Additional Tier 1 (AT1) credit spreads begin moving together with OAT spreads.
  • French sovereign CDS rises faster than the cash-bond spread.
  • Repo haircuts or OAT specialness deteriorate relative to Bund collateral.
  • The European Commission or Council finds corrective action under the excessive deficit procedure insufficient.
  • Several rating agencies revise their outlooks or ratings in the same direction.
  • The parliamentary budget process fails to deliver credible medium-term fiscal adjustment.
  • ECB communication begins explicitly distinguishing market dysfunction from fundamental fiscal repricing.
  • Italian BTP–Bund and Belgian OLO–Bund spreads widen alongside the French spread.
  • A Bund rally accelerates during OAT selling, indicating flight to quality rather than a general interest-rate shock.

Counter-argument: No single indicator proves that a sustained 300-basis-point outcome is approaching. Several concurrent signals, particularly those linking France-specific repricing to auction quality, collateral markets, and regional spillovers, would make the risk assessment more persuasive.

The forecast should be revised downward under the opposite configuration: durable adoption of a budget with verifiable primary-balance improvement; normal auctions even at elevated yields; stabilization of debt in European Commission or IMF projections; no joint widening in bank credit spreads; and a clear, credible ECB backstop.

Counter-argument: Favorable fiscal and market signals would reduce the assessed probability but could not eliminate external risk-off shocks or nonlinear movements in leveraged funding markets.

Conditional risk is materially higher after markets enter a stressed regime. If the spread has already moved sustainably above 225–250 basis points while the budget conflict persists and the ECB's response remains uncertain, the modeled and expert-assessed chance of subsequently reaching a sustained 300-basis-point spread increases by roughly an order of magnitude. A simultaneous liquidity or forced-selling episode could make the process self-reinforcing within days.

Counter-argument: This conditional escalation depends on the spread remaining elevated alongside unresolved political and policy uncertainty. A credible fiscal agreement, orderly auctions, or clearer ECB support could prevent the assumed feedback loop.

The report estimates the conditional probability of meaningful contagion after a sustained French 300-basis-point spread at approximately 35%–60% for Belgium and 20%–45% for Italy. These are expert ranges, not statistical estimates. Both markets could be repriced, but the extent would depend on whether investors perceive a France-specific fiscal event or a broader euro-area institutional crisis. Belgium and Italy have materially different initial fiscal positions.

Counter-argument: Italy's lower deficit and primary surplus could limit spillovers, while Belgium's higher deficit and different market structure may produce distinct outcomes. The French threshold does not mechanically determine the severity of contagion.

The largest unresolved variable is the ECB's reaction function. TPI was established to address disorderly fragmentation, but its use depends on an assessment of eligibility, proportionality, and the fundamental causes of stress. France presents a difficult trade-off: the more clearly a crisis stems from fiscal fundamentals, the more urgently markets may seek a backstop and the more difficult it may be to justify unconditional support under existing rules.

Counter-argument: ECB discretion also provides flexibility. A determination that market functioning or monetary transmission is impaired, together with effective fiscal action, could support a stabilizing response despite the complexity of the eligibility assessment.

The second uncertainty is whether France's political institutions can turn the parliamentary deadlock into a credible long-term fiscal trajectory. At the time of the report, this is an observed constraint rather than a hypothetical one: the 2027 budget had been rejected by the National Assembly and was before the Senate.

The third uncertainty lies in nonbank financial institutions and repo-market microstructure. Official data provide useful aggregate exposure estimates but do not reveal every leveraged basis trade, stop-loss order, or collateral chain. Such positions could contain nonlinear mechanisms similar to those observed during the United Kingdom's LDI episode.

Counter-argument: Incomplete visibility into leveraged positions is a reason to monitor funding and collateral markets, not proof that a destabilizing structure exists at a scale sufficient to trigger a crisis. Political compromise could also reduce uncertainty independently of those market exposures.

The central conclusion is that a future systemic euro crisis could begin in France rather than in a traditionally vulnerable peripheral economy. Today's fiscal deterioration alone is insufficient to sustain a spread above 300 basis points. The more dangerous transition would be from “expensive French debt” toward investor distrust of the stabilization framework itself: a combination of fiscal failure, nonlinear market behavior, and doubts about the European backstop. Accordingly, the most significant warning is not an isolated spread of 200 or even 250 basis points, but simultaneous transmission of French sovereign risk into banks, repo markets, Belgium and Italy, and the ECB's policy response. At that point, a national debt episode could become a broader euro-area crisis.

Counter-argument: The report's central outcome remains a low-probability tail event. France's long debt maturity, currently functioning auctions, stronger bank capitalization, and available European instruments could prevent sovereign repricing from becoming a system-wide crisis.
