EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: 14-DAY FEDERAL REGISTER AUDIT

An audit of the FederalRegister.gov database over the past 14 calendar days (from October 3 to October 17, 2026) records an absence of new direct rules from the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) revising technical thresholds for AI accelerators (ECCN 3A090/4A090) or expanding the Entity List to include Chinese semiconductor foundries. Within the strict 14-day window, targeted indirect measures took effect, including BIS guidance to U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) on assessing "U.S. Content" in metals (including copper and aluminum for server infrastructure) dated October 14, 2026, a Foreign-Trade Zones Board (FTZ Board) decision dated October 15, 2026 granting a limited two-year production authorization for semiconductor wafers and chips to Qorvo US, Inc., an Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) rule on chemicals for silicon wafer surface treatment, and a series of Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) sanctions notices regarding the Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) List. At the same time, an analysis of documents with open comment periods and delayed effective dates reveals a critical concentration of regulatory triggers over the next 20 to 50 days that may fundamentally alter the operational environment for the semiconductor and AI sectors.

The primary upcoming event is the automatic resumption on November 10, 2026 (Phase II of Final Rule 90 FR 50857) of the BIS "Affiliates Rule" (the 50% or greater ownership test), which had been suspended for exactly one year as part of the U.S.–China trade truce in November 2025. Concurrently, the market is navigating deadlines for quotas and anti-stockpiling restrictions on polycrystalline silicon and doped silicon wafers under Section 232 (Temporary Final Rule 91 FR 60505 remains in effect through December 3, 2026 ahead of the introduction of tariffs and minimum import prices on December 4), a 100% domestic allocation mandate for tungsten scrap and "black mass" under the Defense Production Act Priorities and Allocations System (DPAS, with comments open through November 4, 2026), and a review of the U.S. Munitions List (USML) and International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR) by the U.S. Department of State transitioning certain electronic and aviation components to Export Administration Regulations (EAR) jurisdiction (with comments open through November 30, 2026). The combination of the returning 50% ownership rule for subsidiaries of Entity List parties and the strict conditional licensing mechanism for AI chips in effect since January 2026 (Rule 91 FR 1684, governing shipments of NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X to China subject to mandatory inspection of each batch by independent laboratories and a 50% cap relative to domestic U.S. shipment volumes) creates a substantial risk of sudden shipment blocks to unlisted Chinese subsidiaries and joint ventures. Consequently, the companies most exposed over the next 90 days appear to be semiconductor manufacturing equipment (SME) suppliers (Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, ASML), AI accelerator vendors (NVIDIA, AMD), leading contract foundries (TSMC, Samsung), and Chinese technology holdings (Huawei, SMIC, and their subsidiary networks) (Confidence level: High).

Counter-argument: Because no new direct BIS rules revising technical parameters for ECCN 3A090/4A090 or adding Chinese foundries to the Entity List were published between October 3 and October 17, 2026, and because the November 10, 2026 reactivation of the Affiliates Rule could still be averted if BIS publishes a new extension of the suspension during the first week of November, near-term operational disruption across these supply chains is not entirely predetermined.

Detailed Verification of Enacted Documents Across Analytical Criteria: For an in-depth verification of the regulatory acts published directly within the 14-day window (October 3–17, 2026), their regulatory parameters and indirect transmission mechanisms into the semiconductor and AI sectors are examined below.

The U.S. Department of Commerce Bureau of Industry and Security notice Guidance to U.S. Customs and Border Protection Regarding the Assessment of "U.S. Content" for Purposes of Implementing Tariffs Pursuant to Clause (2)(D) of Proclamation 11032 of June 1, 2026 (91 FR 65162, Doc. No. 2026-20938, Docket No. 260918-0056, XRIN 0694-XC163), signed by Deputy Assistant Secretary for Technology Security Jessica Kurito, was published on October 14, 2026, and took effect immediately with respect to goods entered for consumption from 00:01 Eastern Time on October 14, 2026. Pursuant to Presidential Proclamation 11032 of June 1, 2026 ("Further Adjusting the Tariff Regimes for Imports of Aluminum, Steel, and Copper Into the United States," which modified Proclamations 9704, 9705, and 10962), the document establishes a strict formula for calculating "U.S. Content" for products from Canada and Mexico claiming preferential tariff treatment under the USMCA agreement. Only the value of parts wholly obtained, produced, or substantially transformed in the United States, as well as the value of steel (melted and poured in the U.S.) and aluminum and copper (smelted and cast in the U.S.), counts toward the U.S. Content base. Although the document does not directly regulate GPU dies themselves, it impacts the hardware ecosystem of AI data centers: the assembly of high-density server racks (such as NVIDIA GB200/NVL72 architectures), copper distribution busbars, liquid cooling heat exchangers, and power transformers is heavily localized at original design manufacturer (ODM) facilities (Foxconn, Quanta, Wistron, Pegatron) in Mexico. If Mexican facilities use copper or aluminum smelted outside the U.S. in server chassis and radiators, that portion of value is not recognized as "U.S. Content" and is subject to protective tariffs upon entry into U.S. data centers, driving a high likelihood of practical impact over the next 90 days.

Counter-argument: Server integrators and ODMs that can document that their copper, aluminum, and steel inputs were physically smelted, cast, melted, and poured in the United States—or whose components were wholly produced or substantially transformed domestically—can still qualify for preferential USMCA tariff treatment under Proclamation 11032.

The U.S. Department of Commerce Foreign-Trade Zones Board notice Foreign-Trade Zone (FTZ) 39; Authorization of Limited Production Activity; Qorvo US, Inc.; (Semiconductor Wafers, Modules, and Chips); Richardson, Texas (91 FR 65522, Doc. No. 2026-21039) was officially published on October 15, 2026, following notification to the company on October 8, 2026, and is currently in effect. Following review of Qorvo US, Inc.'s application dated January 15, 2026 (91 FR 2726), the FTZ Board authorized production activity involving semiconductor wafers, modules, and chips at the Richardson, Texas site, but imposed a restriction pursuant to 15 CFR 400.14—granting the authorization on a limited basis strictly for a two-year period. This decision allows Qorvo to optimize customs duties when importing raw wafers and subcomponents for manufacturing 5G/Wi-Fi radio frequency (RF) modules and defense chips inside the U.S., though the two-year limit reflects heightened Department of Commerce oversight over the origin of semiconductor materials in foreign-trade zones, resulting in a medium probability of practical impact on Apple, Qualcomm, and defense supply chains over the next 90 days. Concurrently, on October 15, 2026, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) published the rule Significant New Use Rules on Certain Chemical Substances (26-1) (Doc. No. 2026-21051), introducing notification requirements for the export and significant new use of chemical substances used in semiconductor wafer surface treatment, which carries a low short-term probability of supply disruption but increases administrative costs for fab chemical suppliers.

A series of U.S. Department of the Treasury Office of Foreign Assets Control notices—Notice of OFAC Sanctions Action (91 FR 65295, Doc. No. 2026-20994 of 10/14/2026; 91 FR 65850, Doc. No. 2026-21212 of 10/16/2026) and Notice of OFAC Sanctions Actions (91 FR 64224, Doc. No. 2026-20526 of 10/07/2026; 91 FR 65620, Doc. No. 2026-21085 of 10/15/2026)—codifies enacted changes to the SDN, NS-PLC, and SSI lists. Specifically, Document 2026-20994 (issued October 8 and published October 14, 2026, signed by OFAC Director Bradley Smith) imposes blocking sanctions against legal entities and maritime vessels under Executive Order 13902 (sectoral sanctions against Iran), with the designated targets published as graphical tables on pages 65296–65302, whereas the October 7, 2026 notice updates entries across the SDGT, SDNTK, GLOMAG, and IRAQ2 programs. These actions build upon the recently enacted OFAC Sanctions Penalties Regulations (31 CFR Part 505, 91 FR 60821 of 09/25/2026 and 91 FR 61727 of 09/30/2026), which standardized penalty imposition procedures under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) and mandated that OFAC publicly disclose the names of violating companies and settlement amounts. For the semiconductor and cloud sectors, SDN updates carry direct significance through 15 CFR § 744.8 of the EAR, which links OFAC sanctions to BIS export controls even for reexports that do not involve the U.S. financial system, resulting in a low probability of direct impact on physical GPU shipments but a continuous compliance screening burden for Infrastructure-as-a-Service (IaaS) cloud providers.

Documents Not Yet in Effect (Transitional and Pending Triggers): Of particular significance for institutional investors and supply chain participants are regulatory acts currently in a transitional phase: rules with a delayed effective date, temporary rules preceding permanent tariffs, and Proposed Rules and requests for comments with deadlines in October–December 2026.

First in magnitude of impact among documents with a delayed effective date is Phase II of the BIS Final Rule One Year Suspension of Expansion of End-User Controls for Affiliates of Certain Listed Entities (90 FR 50857, Doc. No. 2025-19846, modifying Interim Final Rule 90 FR 47201 of 09/30/2025). The one-year stay of this rule (Phase I) expires on November 9, 2026, and on November 10, 2026 (in 24 calendar days from the audit cutoff), Phase II automatically and indefinitely takes effect unless BIS publishes a new rule extending the pause during the first week of November. The rule affects all global manufacturers of semiconductors, semiconductor manufacturing equipment (SME), electronic design automation (EDA) software developers, server integrators, and cloud providers whose products are subject to the EAR (including foreign-produced items subject to the Foreign Direct Product Rules—FDPR). The market significance of this trigger stems from the fact that on September 30, 2025, BIS replaced its prior "legally distinct standard" with an OFAC-style 50% ownership rule: any company worldwide that is owned 50% or more, directly or indirectly, individually or in the aggregate, by one or more parties on the Entity List, the Military End-User (MEU) List, or SDN sanctions programs specified in 15 CFR § 744.8(a)(1), automatically becomes subject to the strictest license requirements applicable to its owners. Following the U.S.–China leaders' summit in Busan on November 1, 2025, Washington froze this rule for exactly one year in exchange for Beijing suspending export controls on rare earth metals, gallium, germanium, antimony, and graphite. As of mid-October 2026, no extension of the pause has been published in the Federal Register, and the transitional General License (General Order No. 7) that existed in 2025 expired on December 1, 2025. Consequently, on November 10, 2026, without any grace period, thousands of unlisted subsidiaries, regional research centers, and joint ventures of Chinese technology groups are slated to lose the ability to receive items and technology subject to the EAR without special BIS licenses, most of which are reviewed under a policy of presumption of denial (Confidence level: High).

Counter-argument: Because the initial suspension of the Affiliates Rule was enacted swiftly following the November 1, 2025 Busan summit in exchange for Chinese mineral export concessions, a renewed bilateral diplomatic understanding reached prior to November 9, 2026 could still prompt BIS to publish an administrative extension of the stay.

The second critical transitional document is the BIS Temporary Final Rule Measures To Restrict Stockpiling of Polysilicon and Polysilicon Derivatives Under Proclamation 11052 (91 FR 60505, Doc. No. 2026-19537, 15 CFR Part 705). The anti-stockpiling monitoring and quotas introduced by the document are in effect from September 22 through December 3, 2026, and the window for submitting electronic waiver applications to Polysilicon232@bis.doc.gov remains open through December 3, 2026, after which permanent tariffs and Minimum Import Prices (MIPs) established by Presidential Proclamation 11052 of August 6, 2026 under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 take effect on December 4, 2026. This measure directly affects importers of polycrystalline silicon, photovoltaic cells, and—critically for the semiconductor industry—doped silicon wafers and discs for electronics classified under Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States (HTSUS) codes 3818.00.0020, 3818.00.0040, 3818.00.0045, 3818.00.0050, and 3818.00.0091. For new importers (registered after August 6, 2026), a strict import limit of just 7 kg per week is established for codes 3818.00.0020–0091, while for existing importers, exceeding historical average weekly volumes from 2025–2026 triggers an automatic CBP directive blocking any further customs clearance until December 4, 2026. After December 4, 2026, the introduction of minimum import prices and duties is expected to raise the cost of imported silicon substrates for U.S. semiconductor fabs that have not secured advance BIS waivers (Confidence level: High).

Counter-argument: Existing U.S. semiconductor fabs that maintain import volumes within their 2025–2026 historical weekly averages or secure formal BIS waivers via Polysilicon232@bis.doc.gov by December 3, 2026 can mitigate immediate customs blocks and tariff cost escalations.

The third document currently under public review is the U.S. Department of State Proposed Rule International Traffic in Arms Regulations (ITAR): Review of the U.S. Munitions List and Related Definitions and License Exemptions (91 FR 62361, Doc. No. 2026-20079, 22 CFR Parts 120, 121, 123, Docket ID DOS-2026-0991). Comments are open through November 30, 2026, with publication of the final rule expected in the first quarter of 2027. The rule directly affects U.S. and international manufacturers of specialized semiconductors, RF components, radar chips, and dual-use electronics (Qorvo, Analog Devices, Texas Instruments, Intel, AMD/Xilinx, Broadcom). The document proposes two fundamental changes for chip supply chains: first, the Department of State removes most radar altimeters from Category VIII(h)(10) of the U.S. Munitions List (USML) and narrows the scope of Category XX(c), transferring these items to the more flexible commercial jurisdiction of the EAR (BIS); second, it adds a new paragraph 22 CFR § 120.41(b)(7) to the definition of "specially designed". Under the current text, if an electronic component was originally developed for a defense item without catch-all controls (for example, an electronic support system under ECCN 3A611.x within the EAR), but was subsequently used without modification (or with modifications solely for physical fit) in a military submarine under USML Category XX(a)(1), it retroactively ("post hoc") fell under strict ITAR jurisdiction. The new provision § 120.41(b)(7) eliminates this regulatory trap, protecting suppliers of 3A611.x series microelectronics from sudden militarization of their export status, while a new section § 123.26 exempts from licensing the temporary export of foreign defense articles for repair at an original equipment manufacturer (OEM) (Confidence level: High).

The fourth document with an open comment period is the BIS Temporary Final Rule DPAS Directive Allocation Order and Additional Requirements for Recoverable Critical Minerals and Materials (91 FR 50701, Doc. No. 2026-16078, 15 CFR Part 700, Docket ID BIS-2026-0364). While the directive allocation order itself is in effect from August 27, 2026 through August 27, 2027, the public comment period on whether additional restrictions on sales of critical materials are needed for national defense closes on November 4, 2026. The rule affects processors and suppliers of critical minerals, tungsten raw materials (used to synthesize tungsten hexafluoride $\text{WF}_6$ and form interconnects at advanced semiconductor fabs), and battery manufacturers. Using emergency authorities under Section 101 of the Defense Production Act (DPA), BIS required all U.S. persons to direct 100% of monthly sales of "black mass" and tungsten waste/scrap exclusively to U.S. buyers and prohibited their unlicensed export. The closing of the comment window on November 4, 2026 may lead to an expansion of the list of materials subject to mandatory domestic retention, establishing a precedent for using DPAS mechanisms to physically enclose raw material supply chains for the semiconductor and battery industries within the United States (Confidence level: High).

Finally, the fifth procedural document with an open comment window is the BIS notice Agency Information Collection Activities; Submission to the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) for Review and Approval; Comment Request; Five-Year Records Retention Requirement for Export Transactions and Boycott Actions (Doc. No. 2026-20058, OMB Control No. 0694-0096), for which the 30-day OMB comment period closes on October 30, 2026. The document covers approximately 100,000 companies engaged in the export, reexport, transshipment, or diversion of items subject to the EAR (15 CFR §§ 760, 762.6(a)). Maintaining a five-year archive of primary documentation is directly synchronized with the statute of limitations for export control violation investigations and, combined with the September renewals of Temporary Denial Orders (TDOs) against Russian airlines (Ural Airlines — 91 FR 61384, Aeroflot — 91 FR 58840, Azur Air — 91 FR 58843, UTair — 91 FR 58846) for flights to China, Turkey, the UAE, India, Thailand, Vietnam, and CIS countries without BIS licenses under ECCN 9A991, confirms the focus of the BIS Office of Export Enforcement (OEE) on retrospective audits of transit chains (Confidence level: High).

SUPPLY CHAIN ARCHITECTURE

CHIPMAKERS • FOUNDRIES • HYPERSCALERS
03

MULTI-CONTOUR SUPPLY CHAIN TRANSMISSION ANALYSIS

Cross-referencing enacted 2026 regulations with upcoming restrictions—above all the November 10, 2026 reactivation of the Affiliates Rule, the September 24, 2026 polysilicon and wafer quotas, the October 14, 2026 U.S. Content calculation rules, and the baseline AI chip licensing regime under 91 FR 1684 of January 15, 2026—demonstrates a complex multi-contour transformation of global technology supply chains.

NVIDIA: Within NVIDIA's product matrix, the ultra-high-performance B200 and GB200 (NVL72) architectures substantially exceed BIS thresholds for Total Processing Performance ($\text{TPP} < 21,000$) and Total DRAM Bandwidth ($\text{Total DRAM Bandwidth} < 6,500\text{ GB/s}$), keeping their export to China, Hong Kong, Macau, and Country Group D:5 under a strict presumption of denial. At the same time, the NVIDIA H200 accelerator and lower-performance solutions (including the H20) are explicitly named in the BIS Final Rule of January 15, 2026 (91 FR 1684, Doc. No. 2026-00789) as eligible for a case-by-case license review policy for export to China and Macau. However, admission of the H200 into the Chinese market under Rule 91 FR 1684 is conditioned on stringent operational requirements: the exporter must document that there is sufficient domestic supply of the product in the U.S. and that exports to China will not delay fulfillment of any current or new orders from U.S. customers for advanced chips; aggregate shipments of the product to China and Macau cannot exceed 50% of the total volume of the same product shipped to customers for end-use within the U.S.; and prior to shipment from the U.S., each batch of advanced computing commodities must undergo physical inspection at a qualified independent third-party testing laboratory to verify the technical parameters declared in the license. Over the next 90 days, the primary pressure on NVIDIA's Chinese sales channel for H200/H20 chips stems from the November 10, 2026 implementation of the BIS Affiliates Rule (90 FR 50857). Because Rule 91 FR 1684 explicitly prohibits license approval where end-user restrictions apply (Part 744), the automatic extension of Entity List status to all subsidiaries and joint ventures (with ownership $\ge 50\%$) of sanctioned Chinese holdings would immediately invalidate the ability to ship chips to them without separate authorizations. Simultaneously, the BIS notice on "U.S. Content" calculation that took effect on October 14, 2026 (91 FR 65162) increases customs costs and the risk of border delays when importing finished GB200 server racks into the U.S. from contractor plants in Mexico if the copper busbars and aluminum cooling components used in them were smelted outside the U.S. (Confidence level: High).

Counter-argument: Strong domestic demand from U.S. hyperscalers for B200 and GB200 systems—which are prioritized by Rule 91 FR 1684—could offset potential revenue contractions in NVIDIA's Chinese H200/H20 channel caused by independent laboratory bottlenecks or the 50% Affiliates Rule.

AMD: For AMD's product lineup, the MI325X accelerator (alongside the NVIDIA H200) is explicitly cited in the preamble of BIS Rule 91 FR 1684 of January 15, 2026 as a benchmark chip qualifying under the $\text{TPP} < 21,000$ and memory bandwidth $< 6,500\text{ GB/s}$ thresholds for case-by-case exports to China. Newer accelerator generations (MI350) exceeding these limits are completely blocked for export to Country Group D:5, whereas lower configurations of the MI300 series and the MI325X are subject to the 50% China-to-U.S. shipment ratio cap and mandatory pre-export laboratory testing of every batch. Die fabrication for the MI300/MI325X series and High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) integration (CoWoS) depend critically on TSMC capacity in Taiwan, and because domestic U.S. sales of the MI325X are smaller in absolute volume than NVIDIA's dominant products, pegging the permitted export volume to China at 50% of domestic U.S. shipments of that specific model (91 FR 1684) structurally constrains AMD's revenue ceiling in China more tightly than that of its primary competitor. At the same time, within the domestic U.S. market, AMD eliminated a significant patent risk: according to an International Trade Commission notice (ITC Investigation No. 337-TA-1465, 91 FR 23306), the semiconductor device investigation initiated by Adeia's complaint against AMD was terminated in its entirety based on a settlement agreement. Furthermore, for AMD's embedded and defense chip division (Xilinx), the rule proposed by the Department of State on October 1, 2026 (91 FR 62361, § 120.41(b)(7)) reduces the risk of dual-use field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) falling under strict ITAR controls when reused in military platforms (Confidence level: High).

Intel / Apple / Qualcomm / Broadcom / Tesla (and Server Integrators Supermicro, Dell, HPE): Intel Corporation faces two-way regulatory pressure: in client and server processors (Xeon), the November 10, 2026 return of the 50% ownership rule (90 FR 50857) will require halting shipments to dozens of unlisted subsidiaries of Chinese telecommunications and supercomputing groups, whereas in Intel Foundry Services, the company's U.S. fabs face rising costs due to restrictions on importing doped silicon substrates under HTSUS codes 3818 (91 FR 60505) and EPA requirements for wafer surface treatment chemicals (Doc. 2026-21051), although they benefit from the domestic retention of secondary tungsten raw materials under the DPAS order (91 FR 50701). Supply chains for RF modules used in Apple smartphones and Qualcomm modem platforms received localized support from the October 15, 2026 FTZ Board decision (91 FR 65522) authorizing Qorvo US to manufacture wafers and chips in FTZ 39 in Texas for a two-year term. However, for Broadcom and Qualcomm, the reactivation of the Affiliates Rule on November 10, 2026 creates a high compliance risk when shipping networking switch chips (Tomahawk/Jericho) and mobile system-on-chips (SoCs) to Chinese ODMs and peripheral equipment vendors in which the aggregate equity stake held by Entity List or MEU List parties reaches 50%. Tesla is directly affected by two acts: first, the BIS Temporary Final Rule under DPAS (91 FR 50701, comments through November 4, 2026), which prohibits the export of "black mass" and requires 100% of monthly volumes to be sold within the U.S., supporting the raw material base of the company's U.S. battery plants while restricting cross-border recycling; second, Rule 91 FR 65162 of October 14, 2026 on calculating the share of U.S. steel, aluminum, and copper in automotive components and server assemblies from Mexico and Canada. Meanwhile, server OEM integrators (Supermicro, Dell, HPE) stand on the front line of BIS Notice 91 FR 65162 regarding USMCA tariffs on copper, steel, and aluminum (as a substantial share of AI server assembly for the U.S. market takes place in Mexico) and bear the direct operational burden of pre-export laboratory testing for each batch of H200/MI325X-based AI servers shipped abroad pursuant to 91 FR 1684 (Confidence level: High).

TSMC / Samsung: Contract semiconductor manufacturers (foundries) and outsourced semiconductor assembly and test (OSAT) contractors operate within the strict framework of the Foreign Direct Product Rules (FDPR) and 15 CFR § 744.23 of the EAR ("Supercomputers," "Advanced-Node Integrated Circuits," and SME), updated in December 2024 (2024-28270) and January 2026 (91 FR 1684). Any wafer produced outside the U.S. using U.S. equipment or software (EDA) is subject to U.S. export controls when supplied to Entity List parties (especially those designated with Footnote 4 and Footnote 5) or when meeting advanced-node IC thresholds. Through November 9, 2026, TSMC and Samsung Foundry, when accepting orders from lesser-known Chinese fabless chip designers, could rely on the customer's formal absence from the Entity List provided the chip's performance thresholds were respected and no Red Flags were present. Starting November 10, 2026 (90 FR 50857), the compliance standard shifts substantially: if a Chinese fabless startup is owned $\ge 50\%$ in the aggregate—through a chain of venture funds, municipal investment platforms, or holding structures—by one or more parties on the Entity List or MEU List, shipping even legacy-node chips ($28\text{ nm}$ and above) subject to EAR/FDPR jurisdiction becomes a direct violation of U.S. law. For the U.S. divisions of TSMC (Arizona) and Samsung (Taylor, Texas), an additional risk factor is the import control on doped silicon wafers under HTSUS codes 3818 as part of Section 232 anti-stockpiling monitoring (91 FR 60505) through December 4, 2026 (Confidence level: High).

ASML / Applied Materials / Lam Research / KLA: Over the past two years, semiconductor manufacturing equipment (SME) vendors sustained substantial revenue in China by supplying mature-node systems to formally independent Chinese fabs and subsidiaries of regional semiconductor clusters. The return on November 10, 2026 of the 50% ownership rule (90 FR 50857) closes a major legal avenue in the SME sector. Many new production lines in Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Wuhan are structured as separate special-purpose vehicles and joint ventures (SPV/JV) in which sanctioned groups (such as SMIC or entities linked to state defense holdings on the Entity List and MEU List) hold equity stakes directly or through affiliates. Effective November 10, 2026, without a transitional license (as General Order No. 7 expired on December 1, 2025), Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, and ASML (with respect to systems containing U.S. components above the de minimis threshold or subject to the SME FDP Rule) will be required to immediately freeze not only shipments of new equipment from their order backlog, but also the supply of spare parts, consumables, and servicing to such affiliated fabs. Furthermore, the extension of the 5-year record retention requirement for all transshipment and reexport transactions (Doc. 2026-20058) heightens the risk of retroactive penalties for component shipments routed through Asian distributors (Confidence level: High).

Microsoft / Amazon / Google / Meta / Oracle / CoreWeave: For U.S. hyperscalers and specialized AI cloud providers (Microsoft Azure, AWS, Google Cloud, Meta, Oracle, CoreWeave), BIS Rule 91 FR 1684 creates a structural advantage in physical access to scarce accelerators: chip suppliers (NVIDIA and AMD) are prohibited from exporting H200/MI325X accelerators to China if doing so would cause any delay in fulfilling existing or new orders from U.S. customers for domestic end-use, and total Chinese export volume is capped at 50% of domestic U.S. shipments. At the same time, the regulatory burden on cloud providers (IaaS) to police remote access is increasing. Rule 91 FR 1684 requires chip recipients to implement strict Know Your Customer (KYC) procedures to prevent unauthorized remote access to AI compute capacity (including model training and inference) by restricted parties under Part 744, and mandates submission to BIS of complete lists of remote end-users located in Belarus, China, Cuba, Iran, Macau, North Korea, Russia, and Venezuela, or whose parent companies are headquartered in those jurisdictions. In parallel, implementation continues on rules requiring verification of foreign IaaS customers and their foreign resellers (Customer Identification Program — CIP) pursuant to E.O. 13984 and E.O. 14110, as reflected in the 2026 Unified Agenda of Regulations (2026-16621) and the AI Diffusion Framework (which expanded the Validated End User — VEU mechanism for foreign data centers outside Country Group D:5). Finally, construction of new data centers in the U.S. over the next 90 days faces higher costs for electrical and server infrastructure imported from Mexico and Canada due to the strict U.S. smelting criteria for copper, aluminum, and steel under Notice 91 FR 65162 of October 14, 2026 (Confidence level: High).

Chinese Companies: For Huawei and SMIC, which are listed on the Entity List (including under Footnote 4 and Footnote 5 regimes), the one-year suspension of the Affiliates Rule (from November 10, 2025 through November 9, 2026) served as a critical buffer allowing their unlisted subsidiaries and joint ventures (with ownership stakes $\ge 50\%$) to procure components, test instruments, and materials not controlled by special end-use rules. Starting November 10, 2026, this entire corporate periphery is automatically treated on par with Huawei and SMIC themselves, with ownership stakes held by different parties across the Entity List, MEU List, and SDN List (§ 744.8) aggregated ("individually or in the aggregate"). Major Chinese cloud computing providers and large language model (LLM) developers (Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud, Baidu) remain the primary potential recipients of NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X accelerators under the case-by-case mechanism of Rule 91 FR 1684. However, to receive such chips, they must pass through a multi-stage filter: demonstrating civilian end-use, implementing a BIS-verifiable KYC contour that excludes remote IaaS access by any Part 744 parties (and, from November 10, 2026, all of their 50%-owned subsidiaries), and agreeing to full disclosure to U.S. regulators of their registry of clients from China, Russia, Iran, and other D:5 countries. Moreover, any attempts by Alibaba Cloud, Tencent, or Baidu to lease or procure compute clusters through foreign subsidiaries outside China and Macau are blocked by Rule 91 FR 1684, which maintains a strict presumption of denial for exports of advanced chips to third countries destined for any entity whose parent company is headquartered in China, Macau, or another Country Group D:5 jurisdiction (Confidence level: High).

Hidden Market Consequences (Second- and Third-Order Effects): A cross-examination of the Federal Register texts reveals several non-obvious second- and third-order effects that may currently be underestimated by market consensus forecasts. First, market participants largely interpreted the BIS Temporary Final Rule of September 24, 2026 under Proclamation 11052 (91 FR 60505) solely as a measure targeting Chinese solar panel manufacturers. However, a detailed examination of the restrictive quota table in the rule text shows that alongside raw polysilicon (2804.61.00) and photovoltaic cells (8541.42.00, 8541.43.00), strict restrictions were applied to HTSUS codes 3818.00.0020, 3818.00.0040, 3818.00.0045, 3818.00.0050, and 3818.00.0091, setting a quota of just 7 kg per week for new importers and a ban on exceeding historical average volumes for existing players through December 4, 2026. In the U.S. tariff schedule, heading 3818 covers chemical elements doped for use in electronics, in the form of discs, wafers, or similar forms (i.e., semiconductor silicon wafers). Any semiconductor fab currently under construction or expanding in the U.S. (such as new lines in Arizona, Texas, or Ohio) that lacked a high historical baseline of wafer imports in 2025 risks receiving an automatic customs clearance block from CBP unless it submits a waiver application to Polysilicon232@bis.doc.gov by December 3, 2026 (Confidence level: High).

Similarly, the BIS guidance to U.S. Customs published on October 14, 2026 under Proclamation 11032 (91 FR 65162) appears at first glance to be a specialized metallurgical notice, but in practice it introduces an indirect infrastructure surcharge on the deployment of AI clusters. Modern AI accelerator racks require substantial quantities of high-purity copper (busbars, cables, liquid cooling cold plates) and aluminum (chassis and radiators) assembled at plants in Mexico and Canada. Because effective October 14, 2026, only copper and aluminum physically smelted and cast within the United States count toward the duty-free "U.S. Content" share—and primary copper and aluminum smelting capacity inside the U.S. is constrained—a substantial portion of server and transformer imports from Mexico is likely to incur elevated tariffs, increasing the total cost of ownership (TCO) of data centers for hyperscalers and compressing gross margins for Supermicro, Dell, and HPE (Confidence level: High).

An equally substantial risk of rapid repricing in semiconductor equities is linked to the "November 10 cliff" and the cumulative ownership aggregation rule (90 FR 50857). The Affiliates Rule returning on November 10, 2026 requires aggregating ownership stakes ("individually or in the aggregate") across all entities not only on the Entity List, but also on the Military End-User (MEU) List and SDN sanctions programs under 15 CFR § 744.8(a)(1), applying the "rule of the most restrictive requirement" in cases of mixed ownership. Moreover, if an exporter cannot document the ownership structure of a foreign counterparty (a Red Flag), it must proceed on the assumption that a BIS license is required. Because the entire U.S.–China package arrangement (including Chinese general licenses for the export of gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite, and rare earth metals, as well as U.S. Section 301 measures) has expiration dates concentrated within a narrow corridor between November 9 and November 27, 2026, any diplomatic friction in early November could trigger a sharp correction in chipmaker and equipment vendor valuations due to a simultaneous halt in shipments to China and the risk of a retaliatory suspension of Chinese gallium and germanium supplies for compound semiconductor manufacturing ($\text{GaN}$, $\text{GaAs}$) (Confidence level: High).

Counter-argument: If exporters have already completed beneficial ownership mapping during the one-year suspension period (November 2025–November 2026) and diversified compound semiconductor substrate inventories ahead of the November 9–27 diplomatic window, the market impact of the November 10 transition could be partially absorbed.

An additional latent factor is the transformation of independent testing laboratories into a physical bottleneck for export shipments under Rule 91 FR 1684. The requirement that prior to export from the U.S., every batch of AI chips (such as the H200 and MI325X) must be inspected by a qualified third-party testing laboratory to verify TPP and memory bandwidth parameters makes the throughput capacity of those laboratories a physical limiter on the speed of quarterly revenue recognition for NVIDIA and AMD on Chinese contracts. Finally, an analysis of the BIS evidentiary record in the TDO renewal orders of September 17 and September 29, 2026 (91 FR 61384, 91 FR 58840, 91 FR 58843, 91 FR 58846) alongside the provisions of Rule 91 FR 1684 clearly delineates the geography of transit hubs under targeted monitoring by OEE BIS: key transshipment nodes include the UAE (Dubai), Turkey (Antalya, Bodrum), India (Delhi), Thailand (Phuket, Rayong), Vietnam (Phu Quoc, Nha Trang), Malaysia, Singapore, as well as Central Asian and South Caucasus nations (Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Armenia, Azerbaijan). At the same time, within the AI chip segment, BIS preemptively closed the corporate transit channel through these hubs by maintaining in Rule 91 FR 1684 a presumption of denial for the supply of advanced accelerators to any third country worldwide if the ultimate consignee or its parent company is headquartered in China, Macau, or another Country Group D:5 nation (Confidence level: High).

INVESTMENT SEGMENTATION & 90-DAY REGULATORY OUTLOOK

Based on a comprehensive assessment of the October 2026 Federal Register docket and upcoming fourth-quarter 2026 regulatory triggers, technology sector companies can be segmented into three distinct investment categories (Confidence level: High):

Under Pressure: The category of companies whose shipments, margins, or target markets face the greatest headwinds over the next 90 days includes, first and foremost, semiconductor manufacturing equipment vendors Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, and ASML, which face a substantial risk of addressable market contraction and frozen service revenue in China starting November 10, 2026 due to the automatic extension of Entity List restrictions to 50%-owned subsidiaries and joint ventures of Chinese semiconductor groups (90 FR 50857). Under direct regulatory pressure are Chinese technology leaders Huawei, SMIC, Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud, and Baidu, which represent the primary targets of the returning Affiliates Rule, the blocking of indirect procurement via foreign subsidiaries, and the strict shipment cap on H200/MI325X accelerators (91 FR 1684). Server integrators Supermicro, Dell, and HPE are likely to experience gross margin pressure from the strict U.S. smelting assessment rules for copper, aluminum, and steel enacted on October 14, 2026 for server racks imported from Mexico (91 FR 65162), as well as transaction costs for pre-export laboratory inspection of AI server batches. For NVIDIA and AMD (specifically regarding their Chinese revenue segment), despite robust global demand, sales of H200 and MI325X chips to China will remain constrained by the 50% peg to domestic U.S. shipments, the throughput capacity of third-party testing laboratories, and the scheduled disqualification of affiliated Chinese buyers on November 10, 2026.

Relatively Insulated: The group of companies exhibiting high resilience to current export restrictions includes U.S. hyperscalers and cloud operators Microsoft, Amazon (AWS), Google (Alphabet), Meta, Oracle, and CoreWeave, which are protected by the provision of Rule 91 FR 1684 mandating unconditional priority for U.S. orders of advanced chips ahead of any exports to China, ensuring stable allocations of B200/GB200/H200 accelerators for domestic data centers alongside a manageable rise in KYC compliance and server metalwork costs. Apple and Tesla remain relatively insulated from new AI chip export barriers, with Tesla's U.S. battery supply chain additionally gaining guaranteed domestic access to secondary raw material ("black mass") through the 100% DPAS allocation quota (91 FR 50701). This category also includes TSMC and Samsung (within the global segment of advanced $2\text{ nm}$ and $3\text{ nm}$ nodes and CoWoS packaging), as potential order losses from specific Chinese fabless customers with opaque ownership structures after November 10, 2026 are likely to be offset by capacity shortages among U.S. customers.

Potential Beneficiaries: Structural beneficiaries of the new rules include Qorvo US, Inc. and U.S. suppliers of RF and defense microelectronics (Analog Devices, Texas Instruments, AMD/Xilinx), which directly benefit from the October 15, 2026 FTZ 39 production authorization for Qorvo wafers and chips in Texas (91 FR 65522) and the Department of State's October 1, 2026 Proposed Rule 91 FR 62361 removing radar altimeters from ITAR and protecting dual-use electronic components (ECCN 3A611.x) from retroactive inclusion on the USML. Direct commercial beneficiaries also include independent U.S. testing and certification laboratories, as well as providers of corporate and export compliance systems (KYC / Supply Chain Due Diligence), driven by the mandatory requirement under 91 FR 1684 for physical testing of every exported batch of AI chips and the need for comprehensive mapping of 50% ownership chains by November 10, 2026. Furthermore, domestic U.S. producers of polysilicon, silicon substrates, primary copper, aluminum, and tungsten processors gain a direct pricing and market premium under the protectionist umbrella of Section 232 (91 FR 60505 on polysilicon and wafers with minimum import prices taking effect December 4, 2026; 91 FR 65162 on USMCA metals) and DPAS directive allocations (91 FR 50701).

Final 90-Day Regulatory Outlook: The trajectory of the regulatory environment over the next 90 days (October 17, 2026 – January 15, 2027) is defined by a transition from targeted agency notices in mid-October to the execution of major structural triggers in November and December 2026 (Confidence level: High). Regarding the regulatory calendar and comment deadlines, market participants should sequentially monitor the October 30, 2026 closing of OMB comments on the BIS 5-year record retention requirement for export and transit transactions (Doc. 2026-20058) and the November 4, 2026 conclusion of the comment period on the DPAS Temporary Final Rule mandating 100% domestic allocation of tungsten scrap and "black mass" (91 FR 50701, Docket BIS-2026-0364), which may be followed by an expansion of the list of non-exportable critical materials. The pivotal event of the quarter will be the November 9–10, 2026 threshold—the expiration of the one-year suspension and automatic entry into force of Phase II of the BIS Affiliates Rule (90 FR 50857 / 90 FR 47201), coinciding with the expiration window of bilateral U.S.–China arrangements (November 9–27, 2026), including Chinese general licenses for the export of gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite, and rare earth elements. Subsequently, on November 30, 2026, comments close on the ITAR/USML reform (91 FR 62361), and on December 3–4, 2026, the waiver application window closes and permanent Section 232 tariffs and minimum import prices take effect on polysilicon and doped silicon wafers under Proclamation 11052 (91 FR 60505).

In the zone of maximum operational risk during this 90-day window are semiconductor equipment suppliers (Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA, ASML), AI accelerator manufacturers with active licensed shipments to China (NVIDIA, AMD), server integrators with manufacturing footprints in Mexico (Supermicro, Dell, HPE), and Chinese technology ecosystems (Huawei, SMIC, Alibaba Cloud, Tencent Cloud, Baidu). The primary early warning signals of further tightening by Washington will be the absence of a filing in the Public Inspection section of FederalRegister.gov between November 2 and November 9, 2026 extending the suspension of the Affiliates Rule (which would indicate fulfillment of U.S. Congressional committee demands to close subsidiary loopholes and the conclusion of the November 2025 technology truce), the issuance by CBP of initial directives blocking imports of silicon substrates (HTSUS 3818) for exceeding historical quotas under Rule 91 FR 60505, and the publication of the final rule mandating identification of foreign IaaS cloud users (Customer Identification Program) and reporting on large AI model training runs.

Counter-argument: Should BIS publish an administrative extension of the Affiliates Rule suspension in Public Inspection between November 2 and November 9, 2026 alongside a broader renewal of the November 2025 bilateral trade truce, and should CBP approve timely Section 232 wafer waivers for domestic fabs prior to December 3, 2026, the most severe fourth-quarter supply chain disruptions could be substantially moderated.
